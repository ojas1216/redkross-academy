## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every technique taught in this course, from enumerating a web server to verifying a default credential, is something a criminal could also do. What separates a penetration tester from an intruder is not skill, intent or the tools used. It is **authorisation**: written permission to test, granted in advance by someone who has the authority to grant it. The document that records that permission is the **authorisation letter**, sometimes called a letter of authorisation, a permission-to-test memo, or, informally and a little dangerously, the "get out of jail free" letter.

The informal name is dangerous because it suggests the letter protects the tester whatever happens. It does not. A letter protects you only for what it actually authorises, only if the person who signed it had the power to do so, and only while you stay inside its terms. A letter signed by the wrong person, or covering the wrong systems, or expired, is close to worthless, and testing on the strength of it is acting without authorisation.

This lesson explains what the authorisation letter must contain, who can validly sign it, how it relates to the contracts around it, and why third parties such as cloud and hosting providers must often give their own consent. In India, as Lesson 5 explains, the key provisions of the Information Technology Act, 2000 turn on whether an act was done "without permission of the owner or any other person who is in charge of" a computer resource. The authorisation letter is your proof of that permission.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> No authorisation, no test. Not a port scan, not a "quick look", not a single request. Authorisation is signed and dated before any activity begins, including active reconnaissance.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Authorisation | Written permission to perform defined testing activities against defined systems for a defined period, given by a person with authority over those systems. |
| Asset owner | The legal person (company, government body, individual) that owns or lawfully controls a system or data. |
| Empowered officer | A person entitled to act for the owner in this matter, typically under a documented delegation of authority (for example a CISO, CIO or director). |
| Sponsor | The person who wants the test and manages it day to day. Often not the person who can authorise it. |
| Third-party provider | A cloud, hosting, SaaS, content-delivery, managed-security or network provider whose infrastructure carries the client's systems. |
| MSA | Master services agreement: the umbrella commercial contract between client and testing firm. |
| SOW | Statement of work: the contract for one engagement (deliverables, dates, price), usually referring to scope and rules of engagement. |
| NDA | Non-disclosure agreement: the confidentiality obligations of both parties. |
| Revocation | The client's withdrawal of authorisation, after which testing must stop immediately. |

## What the authorisation letter is for

The letter does three jobs at once.

**It is legal permission.** It records that the owner, through someone entitled to act for them, consented to specific acts that would otherwise be unauthorised access. This is the core of its value.

**It is operational proof.** During the test, someone may challenge you: a security operations centre that sees unexpected traffic, a hosting provider that receives an abuse complaint, a security guard during a physical test, or the police. The letter, together with named contacts who can confirm it, lets the challenge be resolved quickly.

**It is a boundary.** Because it names what is permitted, it also defines, by omission, what is not. A tester who is asked afterwards "why did you test that?" should be able to point to the line in the letter or its scope annex that permitted it.

## Anatomy of an authorisation letter

A strong authorisation letter is short and precise. It usually refers to longer scope and rules-of-engagement documents as annexes rather than repeating them, but it must contain, or clearly incorporate, the following elements.

### 1. The parties

The legal name of the client entity that owns the systems, and the legal name of the testing organisation (or individual). For a group of companies, it matters which company owns the target: the parent company's CISO may not have authority over a separately incorporated subsidiary's systems unless a delegation says so.

### 2. The signatory and their authority

The name, title and signature of the person authorising the test, and a statement that they have the authority to do so on behalf of the owner. Good practice is to ask how that authority arises (for example, a delegation-of-authority policy or a board resolution) and to keep that evidence on file.

### 3. A statement of ownership or control

A sentence confirming that the client owns or lawfully controls the listed systems, or has obtained the consent of whoever does. This is the sentence that matters most when systems are hosted by others.

### 4. The targets

The systems that may be tested, as precisely as possible: address ranges in CIDR notation, host names, application URLs, specific accounts, physical locations. Usually this points to a scope annex (Lesson 3), which must be attached and version-controlled with the letter.

### 5. The permitted activities

What the tester may do: for example, reconnaissance, scanning, enumeration, controlled verification of findings, credential testing against named test accounts, social engineering of named groups. Anything not listed is not permitted. Detailed conduct (techniques, rates, data handling) belongs in the rules of engagement (Lesson 4), which the letter incorporates.

### 6. The period and windows

The start and end dates, and any time windows (for example, "weekdays 22:00 to 06:00 IST only"). The letter expires at the end date. Work after that date is unauthorised even if the systems and people are the same.

### 7. The named testers and source addresses

Who will perform the work, and from which IP addresses the testing traffic will originate. Named testers prevent the letter being used by someone else; known source addresses let the client's defenders distinguish the test from a real attack.

### 8. Contacts

At least one technical contact and one escalation contact on each side, with phone numbers that are answered during the test window, plus an emergency contact who can stop the test.

### 9. Conditions, revocation and signature date

Any conditions (for example, "stop and notify on discovery of personal data"), a statement that the client may revoke authorisation at any time by notifying the named contact, and the date of signature, which must precede the start of testing.

The following is the shape of such a letter for the course's fictional client. It is a teaching template, not legal advice.

```text
AUTHORISATION TO PERFORM SECURITY TESTING

Client (owner):  Nilgiri Parts Pvt Ltd (fictional)
Tester:          Example Security Services LLP (fictional)

I, [name], Chief Information Security Officer of Nilgiri Parts Pvt Ltd,
confirm that Nilgiri Parts Pvt Ltd owns or lawfully controls the systems
listed in Annex A (Scope, version 1.2), and that I am authorised under the
company's Delegation of Authority Policy to permit security testing of them.

I authorise the named testers in Annex C to perform the activities in
Annex B (Rules of Engagement, version 1.1) against the systems in Annex A
only, from the source addresses listed in Annex C, between
01-Dec-2026 09:00 IST and 12-Dec-2026 18:00 IST.

This authorisation may be revoked at any time by notice to the tester's
engagement lead. Testing must stop immediately on revocation.

Signed: ____________   Date: ____________
Emergency stop contact (24x7): +91-XXXXX-XXXXX (client SOC duty manager)
```

Notice how little the letter itself says about techniques. It anchors the authority, the period and the parties, and incorporates versioned annexes for the detail. If the scope changes during the test, a new version of Annex A is signed, and the letter's reference is updated.

## Who can authorise a test

The most important question in this lesson is not what the letter says but who signs it. The rule is simple to state: **authorisation must come from the owner of the system, or from someone empowered to act for the owner in this matter.** It is harder to apply, because the person who wants the test is often not that person.

- A **developer** who built the application does not own it; the company does.
- A **system administrator** has technical control, but technical control is not legal authority. Administrators can give you an account; they usually cannot authorise an attack simulation.
- A **project sponsor** may manage the engagement without having signing authority.
- A **CISO, CIO or director** usually can authorise, provided the organisation's delegation of authority gives them that power for these systems.

The second difficulty is that modern systems are rarely owned end to end by one entity. A client's web application may run on a cloud provider's servers, behind a content-delivery network, use a third-party payment page, and be monitored by a managed security provider. The client can authorise testing of what it controls. It cannot authorise you to attack infrastructure that belongs to someone else.

In a real engagement for Nilgiri Parts, the portal hosted with a cloud provider would pass the first decision (Nilgiri controls the application) and trigger the second (the provider's testing policy applies).

**Security implications.** A tester who skips the second decision can end up attacking a provider's shared infrastructure, affecting other customers and breaching the provider's terms. Defenders benefit too: a client that maps every third party before testing usually discovers dependencies it had not documented.

**Key learning.** Authorisation is valid only when it comes from someone with legal authority over every asset tested, and when every third party in the path has been accounted for.

## Third parties: cloud, hosting and outsourced services

Third parties deserve special attention because they are where well-meaning testers most often go wrong.

**Cloud platforms.** Major cloud providers operate a shared-responsibility model: the customer is responsible for what it deploys, the provider for the underlying platform. Providers publish penetration-testing policies that state which services customers may test without prior approval, which activities are forbidden (denial-of-service testing is commonly restricted or prohibited), and how to request permission for anything else. Read the current policy of the specific provider at the time of each engagement and record which version you relied on; these policies change.

**Traditional hosting and colocation.** Shared hosting providers usually require notice or consent, because your traffic hits infrastructure shared with other customers and may trigger their abuse processes.

**SaaS applications.** If the client uses a SaaS product (an email platform, a payroll service), the client usually owns its data and configuration but not the application platform. Testing the platform itself needs the vendor's permission, often through a published security or bug-bounty programme. Reviewing the client's own configuration of the service is a different and usually acceptable activity.

**Security and network providers.** A managed security service provider may need to be told about the test so that it does not escalate it as an incident, or the client may want it *not* told in order to measure detection. Either way that is a deliberate decision recorded in the rules of engagement. Content-delivery networks and web application firewall providers may have their own terms about testing through their edge.

**People and physical sites.** For social engineering, the client can authorise testing of its own staff but not of a supplier's staff. For physical testing, the client may lease a building owned by someone else, or share it with other tenants; the landlord and building security may need to be involved.

## The contract stack around the letter

The authorisation letter rarely stands alone. It sits within a stack of documents, each with a different job:

| Document | Job | Typical owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Master services agreement (MSA) | Commercial terms, liability, indemnity, insurance, governing law | Legal and procurement |
| Non-disclosure agreement (NDA) | Confidentiality of everything learned during the work | Legal |
| Statement of work (SOW) | Deliverables, dates, price and team for this engagement | Engagement manager |
| Authorisation letter | Permission from the owner for named testers, systems and period | Client signatory |
| Scope annex | Exactly which assets are in and out of scope | Client technical lead with tester |
| Rules of engagement | How testing is conducted, contacts, stop conditions | Tester with client technical lead |

Two contractual points are worth understanding even though lawyers will draft them. **Limitation of liability and indemnity** clauses allocate responsibility if the test causes damage despite following the rules; testers normally also carry professional indemnity insurance. **Governing law and jurisdiction** matter when testers and targets are in different countries; Lesson 5 explains that the IT Act can apply to acts committed outside India that involve computer systems located in India.

## Step-by-step: obtaining and verifying authorisation

The process of obtaining authorisation is itself a workflow with several parties and several hand-offs, and it is worth doing in the same order every time.

**Purpose.** The sequence diagram shows the messages exchanged, in time order, between the parties involved in obtaining a valid authorisation. It matters because each message is a check that, if skipped, leaves a gap in the tester's protection.

**Components.** There are four lanes. **Client sponsor** (blue) is the person who wants the test. **Testing firm** (orange) is the engagement lead. **Client signatory** (dark) is the empowered officer, the trust anchor whose signature makes testing lawful. **Third-party provider** (slate) represents a cloud or hosting provider in the path.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows are requests or deliveries; dashed arrows are replies. The sponsor sends **Request: test the customer portal** to the firm. The firm replies with **Scoping questions + draft scope/RoE** (dashed). The firm then sends **Draft letter + annexes** directly to the signatory, not via the sponsor. The firm also sends **Verify authority via known channel** to the signatory, and receives **Delegation of authority confirmed** (dashed). The firm checks the third party's conditions with **Check provider testing policy**, and receives **Conditions / approval** (dashed). Only then does the signatory return **Signed, dated letter** (dashed) to the firm, and the firm sends **Start notice: dates, testers, source IPs** to the sponsor.

**Execution flow.** Requirements first, then drafting, then verification of authority, then third-party conditions, then signature, then the start notice. The letter is the last document signed, because it incorporates the final scope and rules.

**Decision logic.** If the authority check fails, the flow returns to the sponsor to identify the right signatory. If the provider refuses an activity, that activity is removed from the rules of engagement before signature.

**Technical interpretation.** Verifying authority "via a known channel" means using contact details obtained independently (the company's switchboard, a known email domain, an in-person meeting), not the phone number in the email that delivered the request. A forged request to "test our systems" is a known social-engineering pretext, and a tester who acts on it may be used as the attacker's instrument.

**Practical example.** Nilgiri Parts' IT manager (sponsor) asks for a portal test. The firm drafts scope and rules, sends the letter to the CISO, confirms the CISO's authority by calling the company's published number, checks the cloud provider's policy, receives the signed letter dated 28 November, and sends a start notice for 1 December listing two testers and one source address.

**Security implications.** The start notice lets the client's security operations centre recognise test traffic, or deliberately not be told if detection is being measured. Without it, the test may be escalated as a real incident, wasting time and possibly triggering law-enforcement involvement.

**Key learning.** Obtain authorisation directly from the signatory, verify their authority independently, clear third parties, and send a start notice; never start on the sponsor's word alone.

In practice the steps are:

1. **Collect requirements** from the sponsor using a scoping questionnaire: goals, systems, environments, constraints.
2. **Draft the scope and rules of engagement** and agree them with the client's technical lead.
3. **Identify the signatory** and confirm their authority over every listed asset; ask for the delegation that gives it.
4. **Verify identity and authority through an independent channel**.
5. **Identify every third party** in the path and satisfy their testing conditions; record the policy version and any approvals.
6. **Obtain the signed, dated letter** with versioned annexes attached.
7. **Send a start notice** with dates, testers and source addresses to the agreed contacts.
8. **Keep the letter accessible** throughout the test: a copy on the tester's device and, for physical work, a printed copy carried in person, with contacts who will answer the phone.

## Special cases: internal testers and bug-bounty programmes

**Internal security teams.** An employee who tests their own employer's systems still needs authorisation. Employment does not grant permission to attack the company's systems; a job description that includes security testing is a start, but each engagement should still be approved in writing by the person who owns the systems, with scope and rules recorded. Internal testers are, if anything, at greater risk of drifting out of scope, because they have network access and familiarity that an external tester lacks.

**Bug-bounty and vulnerability disclosure programmes.** A published programme is a form of standing, public authorisation with conditions. Its policy defines which assets are in scope, which techniques are forbidden, how to report, and often a "safe harbour" statement describing how the organisation will treat good-faith research that follows the rules. The same principles apply: an asset not listed in the programme's scope is not authorised, and a technique the policy forbids is not permitted, however tempting. Read the policy each time; programmes change their scope frequently.

**Retests and follow-up work.** A retest after remediation is a new period of testing. If the original letter has expired, the retest needs a new or extended authorisation, even if it covers only the same findings on the same systems.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Starting on verbal approval.** "The CIO said go ahead in a meeting" is not authorisation. Get it in writing before activity.
- **The administrator signs.** Technical control is not legal authority.
- **The letter lists "the website".** Vague targets cannot be defended. Name host names, URLs and address ranges.
- **Ignoring the hosting provider.** The client's consent does not cover a provider's shared infrastructure.
- **Testing after the end date.** Expired authorisation is no authorisation.
- **Treating the letter as unconditional protection.** It covers only what it says, and only if you stay within it.
- **Accepting a request without verifying it.** A convincing email asking for a test may itself be an attack.

## A real-world lesson: the Iowa courthouse case

In September 2019, two penetration testers from the security firm Coalfire were arrested during a physical security assessment of the Dallas County Courthouse in Iowa, United States. They were working under a contract with the state judicial branch that authorised physical testing of court facilities. The county, which owned the building, had not been party to that arrangement and disputed that the state could authorise entry to it. The testers spent a night in jail and faced criminal charges, which were eventually dropped. The episode is widely taught in the industry because the testers were doing contracted, good-faith work and still faced prosecution: the gap was between the party that signed the authorisation and the party that controlled the specific asset. The lessons map directly onto this lesson's decision graph: confirm authority over each specific asset, identify every other party with control, and make sure the people who could challenge you know whom to call.

## Security implications, detection and defence

From the defender's side, the authorisation process is a control. Organisations should have a policy that says who may authorise security testing, require that all tests be registered with the security operations centre (even if the SOC is not told the details), and treat any unregistered testing activity as a potential incident. Source addresses listed in authorisation letters let defenders tag test traffic in their monitoring; any activity from unlisted addresses that resembles testing is investigated as real. Clients should also verify the testers: request the names and identification of the individuals and confirm them on the first day.

## Best practices

- Use a standard authorisation template reviewed by counsel, with versioned scope and rules-of-engagement annexes.
- Send the letter to the signatory yourself, and verify authority independently.
- Map third parties before signature and record their policies and approvals.
- Include named testers, source addresses, windows and a 24x7 stop contact.
- Re-sign when scope, dates or testers change.
- Keep the letter with you during the test and in the engagement file afterwards.

## Key takeaways

- Authorisation is written permission, signed and dated before any activity, by someone with legal authority over each target.
- The letter names the parties, signatory and authority, ownership, targets, permitted activities, period, testers, source addresses, contacts and revocation.
- Developers, administrators and sponsors usually cannot authorise; owners and empowered officers can.
- Third-party infrastructure requires its own consent or adherence to the provider's published policy.
- The letter protects you only within its terms; verify it, keep it to hand, and re-sign when anything changes.
