## Introduction and conceptual foundation

When a penetration test ends, the client does not receive the tester's skill, the hours spent or the tools run. They receive a document. Everything the engagement achieved reaches the client through that report: whether leadership understands the risk, whether developers can reproduce and fix each weakness, whether an auditor accepts that the test was lawful and adequate, and whether anyone can later prove that the fixes worked. A technically excellent test with a poor report delivers little value; a modest test with a clear, accurate, actionable report can change an organisation's security. This is why PTES (Lesson 1) treats reporting as a phase in its own right and why professionals plan the report before testing starts.

A report template is the agreed structure that every report from a team follows. Templates exist for three reasons:

1. **Completeness.** A fixed structure makes it hard to forget the scope statement, the limitations or the retest step.
2. **Consistency.** Clients can compare reports year on year and between suppliers, and reviewers know where to look for each element.
3. **Speed and quality.** Writers spend their effort on the content of findings, not on reinventing the layout, and quality reviews can check each section against a known standard.

This lesson builds the template from the reader's point of view. A report has several audiences, and each section exists for one of them. It then dissects the core unit of every report, the **finding**, and finally follows the course lab pipeline from verified evidence to a ranked report and a retest, using the kit's real output in `LAB_ASSETS/C05/data/report_output.txt` as the worked example.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Report template | The agreed section structure, field definitions and style rules that every report from a team follows. |
| Executive summary | A short, non-technical section stating the overall risk, the most serious findings, their business meaning and a recommendation. |
| Finding | One confirmed weakness, written up with its severity, affected asset, evidence, impact, remediation and retest step. |
| Observation | A noteworthy fact that does not on its own constitute a vulnerability (for example, a disclosed version header), reported for context. |
| CVSS | Common Vulnerability Scoring System, maintained by FIRST; version 3.1 is the one the course lab uses. |
| Base score | The CVSS score from 0.0 to 10.0 computed from the intrinsic characteristics of a vulnerability, independent of a particular environment. |
| Vector string | The compact text form of the CVSS metric values, e.g. `CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N`. |
| Remediation | The specific action that removes or reduces the weakness at its root cause. |
| Retest | A repeat of the verification after remediation, to prove that a finding is closed. |
| Limitations | Anything that constrained the test (time, access, excluded areas) and so limits what the report can claim. |

## Who reads a penetration-test report

Before choosing sections, identify the readers. A typical report is read by at least four groups, each with a different question:

- **Leadership** (the board, the CEO, the business owner of the system): *How much risk are we carrying, and what must we decide?* They read one page, maybe two.
- **Security management** (the CISO and their team): *What was tested, what was found, how serious is each issue, and what is the plan?* They read the summary, the scope and the findings table, and track remediation.
- **Engineers** (developers, system administrators): *What exactly is wrong, how do I reproduce it, and what do I change?* They read individual findings in detail.
- **Auditors and regulators**: *Was the test authorised, competent and adequate in coverage?* They read the scope, methodology, limitations and evidence.

A good template serves each group without making the others wade through material they do not need. The usual solution is a layered structure: the shortest, least technical material first, and progressively more detail towards the back.

## The structure of a professional report

The sections below form a sound template. Names vary between firms, but every element appears in some form in professional reports and in the reporting guidance of PTES and the OWASP testing guide.

### 1. Document control

A cover page and a short control block: the client, the system tested, the report version and date, the classification (for example "Confidential, client and tester only"), the distribution list, and the authors and reviewers. Version control matters because reports are revised (a draft for client comment, a final version, a retest addendum), and everyone must know which version they are reading.

### 2. Executive summary

One page, in plain language: what was tested and when, the overall risk, the most serious findings and what they would mean for the business if exploited, and a clear recommendation. No tool names, no jargon that a board member would need to look up. The executive summary is often the only part leadership reads, so it must stand on its own.

### 3. Scope and methodology

What was authorised and tested (with a reference to the signed authorisation from Lesson 2), the targets, the testing window, the source addresses used, the methodology followed (for example "NIST SP 800-115 phases, PTES pre-engagement and threat modelling", as in Lesson 1), the type of test (black, grey or white box), and what was explicitly out of scope. This is the section auditors read first and the one that makes a statement such as "no critical issues were found" meaningful.

### 4. Limitations

Anything that constrained the test: a shortened window, an environment that was unavailable, credentials that arrived late, areas excluded at the client's request. Stating limitations honestly protects both parties. A report that implies full coverage when one application was down for two days misleads the client into a false sense of security.

### 5. Summary of findings

A table listing every finding with its identifier, title, severity and score, and status (open, remediated, risk accepted). Security management uses this table to plan and track the work.

### 6. Detailed findings

One entry per finding, in a fixed format, ranked by severity. This is the technical heart of the report and the subject of the next section.

### 7. Remediation roadmap

The findings regrouped into actions, in priority order, with suggested owners: for example "before go-live", "within 30 days", "next release". Several findings often share a root cause, and the roadmap is where that is made visible ("introduce a central authorisation check" may close three findings at once).

### 8. Retest results

After the client remediates, the retest records which findings are closed, which remain open and the evidence for each. It is often issued as an addendum to the original report.

### 9. Appendices

The material that supports the body without cluttering it: raw tool output, full request and response captures, CVSS vectors, the evidence manifest with SHA-256 hashes (Lesson 6), the list of tested hosts and paths, and the team's contact details.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the section structure of a penetration-test report as a stack of layers, ordered so that the least technical, most decision-oriented material comes first and the most detailed supporting material comes last. It matters because a report that mixes these levels (raw output in the executive summary, missing scope at the front) fails every audience at once.

**Components.** The top layer, **Executive summary** ("leadership: risk, impact, recommendation"), is dark because it carries the most important outcome: the decision. Below it, **Scope, methodology, limitations** ("auditors: authorised, how, what not") establishes what the report can claim. **Summary of findings** ("CISO: ID, severity, status") is the tracking table. **Detailed findings** ("engineers: evidence, CVSS, fix, retest") is the technical core, in orange to mark it as the focus of the writing effort. **Remediation roadmap** ("owners: prioritised actions") turns findings into work. **Retest results** ("everyone: closed or still open") proves outcomes. The bottom layer, **Appendices** ("raw output, captures, hash manifest"), holds the supporting evidence. Document control, the cover and version block, sits in front of the whole stack and is described in the text rather than drawn.

**Relationships and arrows.** The order of the layers is the relationship: each layer summarises the ones beneath it. The executive summary summarises the findings; the findings summary table indexes the detailed findings; the detailed findings cite the appendices for full evidence.

**Execution flow.** Readers enter at the top and stop when they have what they need. Writers usually work the other way: appendices and detailed findings first, from the evidence gathered during testing, then the summary table and roadmap, and the executive summary last, when the whole picture is known.

**Decision logic.** The main editorial decision is where each piece of information belongs. A useful test: if a board member would need to look it up, it does not belong in the top layer; if an engineer needs it to reproduce the issue, it belongs in the detailed finding or an appendix.

**Technical interpretation.** The structure is a form of progressive disclosure. Each layer is complete at its own level of detail, so the report can be read to any depth without losing accuracy.

**Practical example.** The course lab's `report.py` produces two of these layers in compact form: a one-line executive summary and the detailed findings with vectors, evidence, remediation and retest steps. The scope comes from `scope.md`, the retest results from `retest.py`, and the appendices from the saved files in `data/`.

**Security implications.** The report is itself sensitive, because the detailed findings and appendices are a map of the client's weaknesses. Classification, a controlled distribution list and encrypted delivery belong to the template, not to good intentions.

**Key learning.** Order the report by audience: decision first, claims and coverage next, technical detail and evidence last, with every layer accurate on its own.

## Anatomy of a finding

Each detailed finding follows the same fields, so that an engineer can find what they need in any finding without reading the others. A complete entry has:

| Field | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| ID and title | A stable identifier (F1, F2) and a title naming the weakness and where it is, e.g. "Insecure direct object reference on /invoice". |
| Severity and score | The qualitative rating and the CVSS base score, with the full vector string. |
| Affected asset | The host, port, URL or component, exactly as named in the scope. |
| Description | What the weakness is and why it exists, in two or three sentences. |
| Evidence | The minimum proof: the request and response or tool output, with timestamp, referencing the appendix and its hash. |
| Impact | What an attacker could achieve, in business terms, stated honestly without exaggeration. |
| Remediation | The specific fix for the root cause, plus an interim mitigation if the full fix is large. |
| Retest step | The exact check that will prove the fix, so closure is objective. |
| References | Classification such as the CWE identifier and relevant guidance. |

### Severity and CVSS

Findings are ranked so that the client fixes the most dangerous first. Most reports use CVSS, published by FIRST, because it gives a documented, reproducible score. The course lab uses CVSS v3.1. Its base score is computed from eight base metrics:

- **Exploitability**: Attack Vector (`AV`: Network, Adjacent, Local, Physical), Attack Complexity (`AC`: Low, High), Privileges Required (`PR`: None, Low, High) and User Interaction (`UI`: None, Required).
- **Scope** (`S`): Unchanged or Changed, meaning whether a successful attack affects resources beyond the vulnerable component's security authority.
- **Impact**: Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability (`C`, `I`, `A`: High, Low, None).

The score maps to a qualitative rating: 0.0 None, 0.1 to 3.9 Low, 4.0 to 6.9 Medium, 7.0 to 8.9 High, and 9.0 to 10.0 Critical. Always print the full vector next to the score. The vector lets anyone check your reasoning, and a reviewer who disagrees with one metric can say exactly which one.

Read the lab's lowest finding as an example: `AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:R/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N` for missing security headers. It is reachable over the network with no privileges, but exploiting the absence of these headers requires high complexity and a user to interact (for example, to visit a crafted page), and the impact is a low loss of confidentiality. The result is 3.1, Low. Compare the default credential, `AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H`: network, easy, no privileges, no user, full impact on all three, giving 9.8, Critical.

Two cautions. The base score describes the vulnerability, not the client's situation; CVSS also defines environmental metrics, and a good report adjusts priority for business context and says so. And CVSS v4.0 was published by FIRST in 2023; whichever version you use, state it in the vector (`CVSS:3.1/...`) so that scores are never mixed silently.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Never inflate severity to make a report look impressive, and never deflate it to please a client. A score that cannot be defended from its vector damages the credibility of every other finding in the report.

## Worked example: the lab report

The course lab produces its report in three steps, each run against the loopback target you own (Lesson 7). `verify.py` performs the minimum safe check for each suspected weakness and writes confirmed findings with UTC timestamps to `data/findings.json` (Lesson 6). `report.py` then reads that file, scores each vector with the kit's own CVSS v3.1 calculator (`cvss.py`, whose results match the FIRST reference scores for its sample vectors), sorts the findings by score, and attaches the remediation and retest text for each. Its real output, saved in `LAB_ASSETS/C05/data/report_output.txt`, is:

```terminal
$ python report.py
======================================================================
PENETRATION TEST REPORT - Nilgiri Parts Portal (authorised lab)
======================================================================
Executive summary: 4 finding(s), 2 high/critical. Most serious: Default administrator credential (admin/admin) (CVSS 9.8 Critical). Fix the critical items before any exposure beyond the lab.

F1  [Critical CVSS 9.8]  Default administrator credential (admin/admin)
    vector     : CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H
    evidence   : admin/admin accepted, returns administrator role (verified 2026-11-08T00:46:25Z)
    remediation: Remove the default account; require a unique strong credential set at first use; add rate limiting and MFA for administrative logins.
    retest     : Re-run verify.py: the default-credential check must report 'not present'.

F2  [High     CVSS 7.5]  Insecure direct object reference on /invoice
    vector     : CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N
    evidence   : unauthenticated read of another customer's invoice 1002 (verified 2026-11-08T00:46:25Z)
    remediation: Enforce an authorisation check on every object access: confirm the authenticated caller owns (or may see) the requested invoice before returning it. Use unguessable identifiers as defence in depth.
    retest     : Re-run verify.py: the idor check must fail to read another customer's invoice without authorisation.

F3  [Medium   CVSS 5.3]  Verbose error disclosure on /invoice
    vector     : CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N
    evidence   : error response leaks internal query/detail (verified 2026-11-08T00:46:25Z)
    remediation: Return generic error messages to clients; log internal detail server-side only; never expose queries or internal hints.
    retest     : Re-run verify.py: the verbose-error check must find no leaked internal detail.

F4  [Low      CVSS 3.1]  Missing HTTP security headers
    vector     : CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:R/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N
    evidence   : missing security headers: Content-Security-Policy, X-Content-Type-Options, Strict-Transport-Security (verified 2026-11-08T00:46:25Z)
    remediation: Set Content-Security-Policy, X-Content-Type-Options: nosniff and, over TLS, Strict-Transport-Security; review the full header set.
    retest     : Re-run verify.py: the missing-sec-headers check must report the headers present.

Ordering is by CVSS base score; a real report also weighs business context and the client's risk appetite.
```

`report.py` takes no options; it reads `data/findings.json` and exits with an error asking you to run `verify.py` first if that file does not exist.

### Interpreting the output against the template

**What it does well.** The executive summary leads with counts and the single most serious issue, and ends with a recommendation, not a tool name. Every finding has a stable ID, a severity and score with the full `CVSS:3.1/` vector, evidence with a UTC timestamp, a specific remediation that addresses the root cause, and a retest step that makes closure objective. The ordering is by score, and the closing line honestly states the limitation of ordering by base score alone. Look at the remediation for F2: not "fix the IDOR" but "confirm the authenticated caller owns (or may see) the requested invoice before returning it", with unguessable identifiers explicitly framed as defence in depth rather than as the fix.

**What a full report adds.** Map the output onto the template and the gaps are clear, and instructive:

- *Document control*: version, date, classification and distribution are missing.
- *Scope and methodology*: the report should state, from `scope.md`, that the only authorised target was `127.0.0.1` TCP 8099, that the work covered reconnaissance, enumeration and controlled verification only, and that denial of service and anything beyond the single lab process were out of scope.
- *Affected asset and impact*: each finding should name the asset (for example `http://127.0.0.1:8099/invoice`) and state the business impact. For F2: any unauthenticated person could read any customer's invoice, including names, items and amounts, which for a real company would be a personal-data breach with obligations under the DPDP Act 2023.
- *References*: F2 maps to CWE-639 (authorisation bypass through a user-controlled key) and F3 to CWE-209 (error message containing sensitive information).
- *Observations*: `data/enumerate_output.txt` noted that the `Server` header discloses `NilgiriPortal/0.9 Python/3.14.4` and that `/robots.txt` lists paths meant to be hidden. These are not scored findings in the lab output, but a full report would list them as observations or low-severity items so they are not lost.
- *Appendices*: the evidence manifest with SHA-256 hashes, for example `4bd809c804aba2c39647eade0c3e012aad58315bffd66c9af13edc53a590e9a4` for `data/findings.json`.

### Rewriting the executive summary for leadership

The one-line summary is a good model of priority, but a board needs it in business language. A version for the fictional Nilgiri Parts board might read:

> An authorised test of the customer parts portal (8 November 2026) found four weaknesses, two of them serious. Anyone on the internet could log in as administrator using a factory-default password, and anyone could read any customer's invoices without logging in. Either would expose customer data and could lead to a reportable data breach. We recommend that the portal is not exposed beyond the test environment until both are fixed and retested; the two lesser issues can follow in the next release.

Notice what changed: no CVSS numbers, no paths, no tool names; the consequences are stated in terms of customers and obligations; the recommendation is a decision the board can take.

## From verification to retest: the reporting pipeline

The report is not the last step. A finding is only finished when a retest proves it closed, and the retest uses the same checks as the original verification so that "fixed" means the same thing as "found". The next figure shows the whole pipeline in the course lab.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows how evidence becomes a report and how the report leads to a verified fix, using the course lab's real components. It matters because it makes the report one stage of a loop rather than a document thrown over a wall: each finding enters as evidence and leaves only when a retest closes it.

**Components.** **verify.py** ("minimum safe check per finding") is the controlled verification against the loopback target. **data/findings.json** ("confirmed findings + UTC time") is the evidence record, hashed as Lesson 6 describes. **report.py + cvss.py** ("score, rank, attach fix and retest") turns evidence into a ranked report. **Report delivered** ("executive summary + findings") is the deliverable to the client. **Client remediates** ("fix the root cause") is the client's work, represented in the lab by the hardened `target/app_fixed.py` on port 8100. **retest.py --port 8100** ("same checks, hardened target") repeats the verification. The final dark box, **Retest results** ("4 of 4 closed"), is the outcome.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each arrow is a hand-off of a concrete artefact: checks produce the JSON record, the record feeds the report, the report drives the remediation, the remediated target is retested, and the results are added to the report. The loop arrow, labelled **open findings return for further remediation**, carries anything the retest reports as still open back into the cycle.

**Execution flow.** Verify, record, report, deliver, remediate, retest, record the result. In the lab, the whole loop takes minutes; in a real engagement the remediation stage may take weeks, and the retest is scheduled under the original authorisation or a new one.

**Decision logic.** The retest is the decision point: for each finding, `CLOSED` or `OPEN`. `retest.py` exits with status 0 only if every finding is closed and with status 1 listing the open ones otherwise, so the decision is unambiguous.

**Technical interpretation.** Because `retest.py` performs the same four checks as `verify.py`, the definition of each finding stays constant across the loop. A retest that used a different, weaker check could report a false "closed".

**Practical example.** Against the hardened target, the kit's saved `data/retest_output.txt` reads `[CLOSED]` for default-credential, idor, verbose-error and missing-sec-headers, then `4 of 4 findings closed.` and `all findings closed - remediation verified.` Run against the vulnerable target on 8099, the kit's README records that all four are reported `OPEN`.

**Security implications.** Without a retest, a client has only a developer's assurance that a fix works. With one, they have evidence, captured and timestamped like the original findings. Defenders can also use the retest to confirm that detection rules written in response to the findings fire as intended.

**Key learning.** A finding is closed by evidence, not by assertion: retest with the same checks, record the result, and loop back anything still open.

### Recording the retest in the report

The retest addendum lists each finding with its new status and the evidence. The kit's real retest output against the hardened target:

```terminal
$ python retest.py --port 8100
============================================================
RETEST  target=127.0.0.1:8100
============================================================
  [CLOSED] default-credential
  [CLOSED] idor
  [CLOSED] verbose-error
  [CLOSED] missing-sec-headers

4 of 4 findings closed.
all findings closed - remediation verified.
```

`--port 8100` points the tool at the hardened target (8100 is also its default); `--host` is accepted but, as Lesson 7 showed, anything other than loopback is refused. In the addendum, each finding's status changes from Open to Closed with the retest date, and the summary-of-findings table is updated so that the client's tracking reflects the verified state.

## Quality review before release

No report should leave the team without a second person reviewing it. A practical release checklist, built into the template, covers:

- **Accuracy.** Every finding is supported by evidence in the appendices, and every hash in the manifest verifies.
- **Reproducibility.** A reviewer who was not on the test can follow each finding's evidence and understand how to reproduce it.
- **Scoring.** Every score is consistent with its vector, and the CVSS version is stated.
- **Actionability.** Every remediation is specific, addresses the root cause and has a retest step.
- **Scope and limitations.** The scope matches the signed authorisation, and limitations and untested areas are stated.
- **Data protection.** Personal data and live credentials are redacted or removed; anything that remains is justified.
- **Executive summary.** It can be understood by a non-technical reader and ends with a recommendation.
- **Handling.** Classification, version and distribution list are set, and delivery will be encrypted to named recipients.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"The scanner output is the report."** Raw output is unranked, unverified and unreadable to most audiences. It belongs, at most, in an appendix.
- **"A longer report is a better report."** Length hides priorities. Fewer, verified, well-written findings beat pages of unvalidated noise.
- **"The executive summary is a list of findings."** It is a statement of risk and a recommendation. Findings belong in the table and the detail.
- **"No findings means the system is secure."** It means no findings within the stated scope, time and method. Without the scope and limitations sections, the statement is meaningless.
- **"Fix the vulnerability" is a remediation.** It is not. Say what to change, where, and how to confirm it.
- **"Retesting is optional."** Without a retest, the client cannot distinguish a real fix from an attempted one.

## Security implications, detection and defence

The report is a defensive instrument. For the client's security team, the detailed findings and evidence are the basis for detection engineering: for each finding they can ask whether the test activity appeared in their logs, and write or tune rules so that a real attacker attempting the same thing would be seen. The lab's IDOR, for example, would appear in web server logs as sequential `/invoice?id=` requests from one client reading many different customers' records; a defender who knows the finding can watch for that pattern while the fix is deployed.

The report is also a target. It concentrates the client's weaknesses, sometimes with working credentials, into one file. Treat it like the evidence it is built from (Lesson 6): classify it, encrypt it in transit and at rest, limit distribution to named people, hash the delivered version, and agree how long the testing firm keeps its copy.

## Best practices

- Agree the report template, delivery method and retest arrangements in the pre-engagement stage.
- Write findings as you confirm them, from the evidence, not from memory at the end.
- Use one fixed format for every finding, with the full CVSS vector and a retest step.
- State the scope, methodology and limitations explicitly; list untested areas.
- Write the executive summary last, in business language, ending with a recommendation.
- Keep raw output and full captures in appendices with a hash manifest.
- Peer-review every report against a checklist before release, and deliver it encrypted to named recipients.

## Key takeaways

- The report is the product of a penetration test; a template makes it complete, consistent and reviewable.
- Order the report by audience: executive summary, then scope, methodology and limitations, then the findings table, detailed findings, remediation roadmap, retest results and appendices.
- Every finding has an ID, severity with a full CVSS vector, affected asset, evidence, honest impact, specific remediation and a retest step.
- CVSS v3.1 base scores map to Low, Medium, High and Critical bands; state the version and adjust priority for business context openly.
- The lab pipeline (`verify.py`, `findings.json`, `report.py`, `retest.py`) shows the full loop: a finding is closed by a retest with the same checks, recorded in the report.
