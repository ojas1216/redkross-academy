## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A penetration test is an authorised, time-boxed attempt to find and demonstrate weaknesses in a system the way a real adversary might, so that the owner can fix them before an attacker finds them. That one sentence hides a hard problem. Two testers given the same target and the same week can produce completely different results: one runs an automated scanner, pastes its output into a document and calls it a test; the other works out what the business actually cares about, maps the exposure, verifies each weakness safely, and reports ranked findings that a development team can act on. The difference is not talent alone. It is **methodology**.

A methodology is a documented, repeatable way of doing the work. It answers four questions that every engagement must answer whether or not anyone writes them down:

1. **What happens before testing starts?** Who agrees to what, and in what form.
2. **In what order is the technical work done?** So that nothing important is skipped and later steps build on earlier ones.
3. **How do we know we have covered enough?** So that "we found nothing" means something.
4. **What is handed over at the end?** So that the client gets a usable result rather than raw tool output.

Without a methodology, coverage depends on the tester's mood, evidence is collected inconsistently, and two tests of the same system cannot be compared. With one, a test becomes repeatable (a second team following the same method should find substantially the same issues), auditable (a reviewer can see what was and was not done) and defensible (if something goes wrong, the tester can show they followed a recognised professional standard).

This lesson introduces the three reference frameworks that this course uses. The **Penetration Testing Execution Standard (PTES)** describes the full lifecycle of an engagement in seven phases, from the first conversation with the client to the final report. The **Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual (OSSTMM)**, published by ISECOM, takes a more scientific view: it treats security testing as measurement of how much an attacker can see, reach and abuse, across defined channels. **NIST Special Publication 800-115**, the *Technical Guide to Information Security Testing and Assessment* (September 2008), gives a compact four-phase model of penetration testing that this course uses as its spine. None of these is a law and none of them is perfect, but together they give you the vocabulary that clients, auditors and colleagues will expect you to speak.

> [!NOTE]
> A methodology is not a checklist of tools. PTES, OSSTMM and NIST SP 800-115 describe *what must be achieved* in each stage. The tools you choose to achieve it will change every year; the stages will not.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Methodology | A documented, repeatable process for planning, executing and reporting a security test. |
| PTES | Penetration Testing Execution Standard: a practitioner-written description of a full engagement in seven phases. |
| OSSTMM | Open Source Security Testing Methodology Manual, maintained by ISECOM; version 3 is the widely cited release. |
| ISECOM | Institute for Security and Open Methodologies, the non-profit body that publishes OSSTMM. |
| NIST SP 800-115 | US National Institute of Standards and Technology guide to technical security testing; defines Planning, Discovery, Attack and Reporting phases for penetration tests. |
| Engagement | One contracted piece of testing work, with agreed scope, rules and deliverables. |
| Attack surface | Everything through which an attacker could interact with the target: hosts, services, people, processes, physical access. |
| Porosity | OSSTMM term for the openings in a target's operational security, measured as visibility, access and trust. |
| Channel | OSSTMM term for a medium of interaction: human, physical, wireless, telecommunications or data networks. |
| Vulnerability assessment | Breadth-first identification and ranking of weaknesses, usually without exploiting them. |

## Why three frameworks, not one

New testers often ask which methodology is "the right one". The honest answer is that they were written for different purposes and are best used together.

PTES was written by a group of working penetration testers who wanted a common language for what a client should expect when buying a test. Its strength is the **engagement lifecycle**: it is the only one of the three that spends serious effort on what happens before testing (pre-engagement interactions) and on threat modelling, which forces the tester to ask what the business would actually lose. Its weakness is that parts of its technical guidance have aged, and the official site has at times been hard to reach, so practitioners usually cite its structure rather than its tool lists.

OSSTMM was written to make security testing *scientific*: consistent, measurable and independent of any one tool or vendor. Its strength is **coverage and measurement**. It defines the channels a test can address, defines what counts as an opening, and lets you express the result as a measurement rather than a list of anecdotes. Its weakness is that it is dense and abstract, and many clients do not need a full OSSTMM measurement; they need prioritised findings.

NIST SP 800-115 was written as guidance for organisations planning and running technical assessments. Its strength is a **clear, compact process** and a good catalogue of assessment techniques (review techniques, target identification and analysis techniques, and target vulnerability validation techniques). Its four penetration-testing phases are easy to remember and easy to map onto contracts, which is why this course uses them as the spine.

A sensible professional approach, and the one this course follows, is: use NIST SP 800-115's four phases as the backbone of the plan; use PTES vocabulary for the engagement lifecycle (especially pre-engagement and threat modelling); and use OSSTMM's ideas of channels and porosity to check that coverage is complete and to explain exposure in measurable terms.

## The PTES lifecycle in depth

PTES divides an engagement into seven phases. They are not strictly sequential in practice (information found late often sends you back to earlier phases), but each phase has a clear purpose and a clear output that feeds the next.

### 1. Pre-engagement interactions

This is everything agreed before any packet is sent: the goals of the test, the scope (what may be tested), the rules of engagement (how it may be tested), timelines, communication channels, emergency contacts, payment terms and the written authorisation. PTES puts this first deliberately. Most serious failures in penetration testing, legal trouble included, start here: a vague scope, an authorisation signed by someone without authority, or no agreed way to stop the test. Lessons 2, 3 and 4 of this module are entirely about this phase.

**Output:** signed authorisation, scope and rules of engagement.

### 2. Intelligence gathering

The tester collects information about the target: domains, address ranges, technologies, people, publicly exposed documents and services. PTES distinguishes passive gathering (using information already published, without touching the target) from active gathering (interacting with the target, for example by enumerating its services). Even passive work must stay inside the authorised scope and purpose. Module 2 of this course covers reconnaissance in detail.

**Output:** an inventory of assets and an initial picture of the attack surface.

### 3. Threat modelling

This phase is what separates a professional test from a scan. The tester identifies the business assets that matter (customer data, payment processing, intellectual property, availability of a critical service), the business processes that use them, and the threat communities that might attack them, along with their likely capability. The result is a set of priorities: which paths to the important assets deserve the most testing effort. A test of an online parts portal, for instance, should spend more effort on "can one customer read another customer's invoices" than on a cosmetic header issue, because the first is what the business would actually lose.

**Output:** prioritised attack scenarios tied to business impact.

### 4. Vulnerability analysis

The tester discovers weaknesses in the systems identified, using both active techniques (scanning, enumeration, manual probing) and passive techniques (reviewing versions, configurations and documentation), and then validates them to remove false positives. Validation matters: a scanner's "possible vulnerability" is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**Output:** a list of candidate weaknesses, validated as far as safely possible.

### 5. Exploitation

The tester attempts to use the weaknesses to gain access or demonstrate impact, choosing precise, reliable techniques over noisy ones. In a professional engagement exploitation is bounded by the rules of engagement: the aim is to prove that a weakness is real and to show its consequence, using the minimum action that does so. This course calls this **controlled verification** and never uses weaponised exploits.

**Output:** confirmed findings with evidence.

### 6. Post-exploitation

Once access is gained, the tester assesses its value: what data and systems can now be reached, whether privileges can be raised, and what the access means for the business. PTES stresses rules that protect the client during this phase, for example not modifying data, not installing anything that is not agreed, and documenting every change so that it can be cleaned up. Module 9 of this course treats this phase from a review and defender perspective.

**Output:** a demonstrated business impact, and a record of every change made so it can be reversed.

### 7. Reporting

The tester communicates the results in a form both executives and technical staff can use: an executive summary of risk, and a technical report with each finding, its evidence, its severity and its remediation. PTES treats the report as the product of the engagement, which it is: an excellent test with a poor report delivers little value. Lesson 8 of this module covers report templates.

**Output:** the report, and usually a debrief and an agreed retest.

## OSSTMM: testing as measurement

OSSTMM approaches the same problem from a different angle. Its central idea is that security is about **separation** between an asset and a threat, and that a test should measure where that separation fails. Rather than asking "what vulnerabilities did we find?", OSSTMM asks "how many ways can a threat interact with the target, and what controls govern each of those interactions?" This produces a result that can be compared over time and between targets.

### Classes and channels

OSSTMM 3 organises the things that can be tested into three classes and five channels:

- **Physical security (PHYSSEC)**: the **human** channel (people, and how they can be influenced or deceived) and the **physical** channel (buildings, rooms, hardware, locks, perimeters).
- **Spectrum security (SPECSEC)**: the **wireless** channel (all electromagnetic and wireless communication, such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and RFID).
- **Communications security (COMSEC)**: the **telecommunications** channel (telephony and similar networks) and the **data networks** channel (wired networks and the systems on them).

The value of this structure is coverage. When a client asks for "a penetration test", the channel list forces a conversation: does that include staff (human), the office (physical), the guest Wi-Fi (wireless)? A typical web-application test touches only the data networks channel, and the report should say so plainly, so that nobody mistakes it for an assessment of the whole organisation.

### Porosity: visibility, access and trust

OSSTMM measures the openings in operational security, which it calls **porosity**, in three categories:

- **Visibility**: assets that a threat can see or detect, such as a listening service or an exposed host name.
- **Access**: points where a threat can interact with the target, such as a login form that accepts input.
- **Trust**: points where the target accepts interaction from another entity without authentication or verification, such as an API that trusts any request carrying a customer number.

The course lab illustrates all three. The `Server` header and the readable `/robots.txt` reported by `enumerate.py` increase visibility. The `/login` endpoint is an access point. The `/invoice` endpoint, which returns any customer's invoice for a given id without checking who is asking, is a trust failure: the application trusts the requester's claim about which object they may see.

### Controls and limitations

Against porosity, OSSTMM counts **controls**, the mechanisms that protect each interaction. OSSTMM 3 defines ten operational controls in two groups. The interactive controls are authentication, indemnification, resilience, subjugation and continuity; the process controls are non-repudiation, confidentiality, privacy, integrity and alarm. It also records **limitations**: weaknesses and failures in the controls themselves. Combining porosity, controls and limitations, OSSTMM computes a measure it calls the **rav**, which expresses the attack surface as a number. You do not need to compute a rav in this course, but the logic behind it is valuable: every opening should be matched by controls, and a test should report where it is not.

### OSSTMM test types

OSSTMM also classifies tests by how much each side knows. It describes six common types: **blind** (the tester knows nothing about the target; the target knows the test is coming), **double blind** (neither side has advance knowledge, which tests the defenders' detection), **grey box** (the tester has limited knowledge; the target knows), **double grey box** (the tester has limited knowledge; the target is told the scope and time frame in advance but not which channels or vectors will be tested), **tandem** (both sides have full knowledge, as in an audit) and **reversal** (the tester has full knowledge and the target does not know when or how the test will happen, as in a red-team exercise). The more familiar black, grey and white box terms are simplifications of this idea and are discussed in Lesson 3.

Finally, OSSTMM asks that results be reported in a standard form it calls the **Security Test Audit Report (STAR)**, which records what was tested, what was not, and the measured result. That emphasis on stating what was *not* tested is one of OSSTMM's most useful contributions to everyday practice.

**Practical example.** The Nilgiri Parts lab covers only the data networks channel, and within it only one service on `127.0.0.1:8099`. A STAR-style report would state that the human, physical, wireless and telecommunications channels were not tested.

**Security implications.** Attackers choose the weakest channel, not the one in scope. A clean data-network report says nothing about a receptionist who will hold the door open. Stating untested channels protects the client from false assurance and the tester from misplaced blame.

**Key learning.** OSSTMM turns "did you test everything?" into a concrete list of five channels, and insists that every report says which were tested and which were not.

## NIST SP 800-115: the four-phase spine

NIST SP 800-115 is broader than penetration testing: it describes review techniques (documentation, logs, rulesets, configurations, network sniffing and file integrity checking), target identification and analysis techniques (network discovery, port and service identification, vulnerability scanning and wireless scanning) and target vulnerability validation techniques (password cracking, penetration testing and social engineering). It also covers planning an assessment, executing it and post-testing activities such as mitigation recommendations and reporting.

For penetration testing specifically, it defines four phases:

| NIST SP 800-115 phase | What happens | PTES equivalent | Course lab tool |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Planning | Rules identified, management approval finalised and documented, goals set. No testing occurs. | Pre-engagement interactions | `scope.md` |
| Discovery | Information gathering and scanning, then vulnerability analysis. | Intelligence gathering, threat modelling, vulnerability analysis | `enumerate.py` |
| Attack | Verifying potential vulnerabilities by attempting to exploit them; new information loops back to Discovery. | Exploitation, post-exploitation | `verify.py` |
| Reporting | Runs alongside the other phases and ends with a report of findings, risk and mitigations. | Reporting | `report.py`, `retest.py` |

Two features of the NIST model deserve emphasis. First, the **feedback loop**: during the Attack phase, the tester often learns something new (an additional service, a credential, a host reachable only from inside) and returns to Discovery to analyse it. Testing is iterative, not a straight line. NIST breaks the Attack phase into gaining access, escalating privileges, system browsing and installing additional tools, each of which can reveal new surface. Second, **reporting is continuous**: NIST describes reporting as occurring simultaneously with the other phases, because notes and evidence are written as you go, not reconstructed at the end.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The feedback loop never extends the scope. If the Attack phase reveals a host that is not named in the scope, the loop sends you back to Discovery only to *record* it. Testing it requires a written scope change (Lesson 3).

## Step-by-step: choosing a methodology spine for an engagement

Suppose Nilgiri Parts Pvt Ltd (a fictional company) asks you to test its customer portal. A practical way to build your plan from the three frameworks is:

1. **Start with NIST's four phases** as the top-level plan and the structure of your statement of work. They map neatly onto contract milestones: planning signed off, discovery complete, attack complete, report delivered.
2. **Fill Planning with PTES pre-engagement items**: goals, scope, rules of engagement, authorisation, contacts, windows and data handling.
3. **Use PTES threat modelling** at the start of Discovery to decide where effort goes. For a parts portal, the business assets are customer and invoice data and the integrity of orders; the most likely threat is an opportunistic external attacker or a curious customer.
4. **Use OSSTMM channels** to state coverage. Here: data networks only; human, physical, wireless and telecommunications explicitly not tested.
5. **Use OSSTMM porosity** as a coverage check during Discovery: list what is visible, where interaction is possible, and where the system trusts input without verification.
6. **Plan the Reporting deliverables** now, not at the end: executive summary, ranked technical findings, and a retest.

The course lab follows exactly this plan, which is why its tool chain looks the way it does: `scope.md` (Planning), `enumerate.py` (Discovery), `verify.py` (Attack, as controlled verification), `report.py` and `retest.py` (Reporting).

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"A methodology is a tool list."** Tool lists age; phases and objectives do not. Cite the phase you are in, not the tool you happen to be running.
- **"Following PTES means doing every phase in full."** Methodologies are scaled to the engagement. A two-day web test still has pre-engagement and reporting, but its threat model may be one page.
- **"OSSTMM is only for audits."** Its channel list and the porosity idea are useful in every engagement, even when you do not compute a full measurement.
- **"The feedback loop lets me follow anything I find."** It lets you analyse what you find inside scope. Anything outside scope is recorded and reported, not tested.
- **"Vulnerability assessment and penetration testing are the same."** A vulnerability assessment identifies and ranks weaknesses broadly; a penetration test goes further and verifies and demonstrates impact. Both need authorisation.
- **"Reporting starts after testing."** Evidence and notes written days later are incomplete and harder to trust. Write as you go.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Methodology is also a defensive tool. For the client, insisting on a recognised methodology in the contract is the simplest way to make test results comparable from year to year and between suppliers. For the security operations team, knowing the phases tells them what to expect in their logs: discovery activity (connection attempts across many ports, enumeration of paths) arrives first; verification attempts against specific weaknesses arrive later. Defenders can use authorised tests to check that each phase is visible to them. If Discovery produced no alerts at all, that is itself a finding about detection coverage.

For the tester, the methodology is protection. If a system fails during a test, a documented plan that followed recognised phases, with authorisation, scope and rules agreed first, is the evidence that you acted professionally.

## Best practices

- Name your methodology in the proposal and the report, and say how you adapted it.
- Keep NIST's four phases as milestones in the statement of work.
- Write a short threat model even for small engagements; it is the best guide to where effort should go.
- State coverage per OSSTMM channel, including channels not tested.
- Treat every scanner result as a hypothesis until validated.
- Record new information found during the Attack phase, return to Discovery for in-scope items, and raise out-of-scope items with the client.

## Key takeaways

- A methodology makes a test repeatable, auditable and defensible; it describes objectives, not tools.
- PTES gives the engagement lifecycle in seven phases, from pre-engagement interactions to reporting, with threat modelling as the step that ties testing to business impact.
- OSSTMM treats testing as measurement: five channels in three classes, porosity measured as visibility, access and trust, and controls that should match every opening.
- NIST SP 800-115 provides the four-phase spine (Planning, Discovery, Attack, Reporting) with an Attack-to-Discovery feedback loop and continuous reporting.
- Use them together: NIST for structure, PTES for the lifecycle and threat model, OSSTMM for coverage, and always state what was not tested.
