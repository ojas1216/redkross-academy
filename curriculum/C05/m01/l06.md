## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A penetration test produces two things: a set of claims about a system ("an unauthenticated user can read another customer's invoice") and the evidence that supports each claim. The claims go in the report; the evidence is what makes anyone believe them. A developer who cannot reproduce a finding will close it as "cannot reproduce". A manager who is told that the default administrator password works will ask how you know. An auditor reviewing the engagement a year later will ask what exactly was done, when, and by whom. And if something goes wrong during the test (a service crashes, data appears where it should not, a dispute arises about whether the tester stayed in scope) the evidence trail is what shows what really happened.

Evidence handling is a discipline that runs through the whole engagement, from the moment the authorisation letter is signed to the moment the last copy of the data is destroyed. It has four parts, which this lesson takes in turn:

1. **Capture**: recording what you did and what the system did in reply, in enough detail that another tester could repeat it and get the same result.
2. **Integrity**: being able to prove that an evidence file has not changed since it was captured, which in practice means cryptographic hashing with SHA-256.
3. **Custody**: recording who held each item of evidence, when, where, and why, at every hand-off.
4. **Lifecycle**: storing evidence safely, keeping only what is needed, retaining it only as long as agreed, and destroying it securely and verifiably at the end.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Evidence often contains the most sensitive information in the whole engagement: credentials that worked, personal data that was exposed, internal hostnames and configuration. Handle it as if it were the client's own confidential data, because it is.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence | Any record that supports a statement in the report: requests and responses, tool output, notes, screenshots, findings records, and the documents that authorised the work. |
| Reproducibility | The property that another competent person, following the recorded steps against the same target state, obtains the same result. |
| Integrity | Assurance that a record has not been altered, accidentally or deliberately, since it was captured. |
| Cryptographic hash | A fixed-length digest computed from data such that any change to the data produces a different digest, and it is infeasible to find two inputs with the same digest. |
| SHA-256 | A member of the SHA-2 family of hash functions specified in NIST FIPS 180-4, producing a 256-bit digest, written as 64 hexadecimal characters. |
| Hash manifest | A file listing evidence files and their hashes, used to verify a whole set at once. |
| Chain of custody | The chronological record of who held an item of evidence, when, where and for what purpose, from capture to disposal. |
| Minimisation | Collecting and keeping only the evidence needed to prove a finding, especially where personal data is involved. |
| Retention period | The agreed length of time evidence is kept after the engagement before it must be destroyed. |
| Sanitisation | Making data on storage media unrecoverable; NIST SP 800-88 groups methods into Clear, Purge and Destroy. |

## What counts as evidence in a penetration test

Evidence is far more than "the screenshot of the exploit". An engagement generates several kinds of record:

**Engagement documents.** The signed authorisation letter, the scope and the rules of engagement (Lessons 2 to 4) are evidence too. They prove that the activity was permitted, which is what separates it from an offence under the IT Act 2000. Keep the signed version, with its date, alongside the technical evidence. In the course lab this is `LAB_ASSETS/C05/scope.md`.

**The activity log.** A running, timestamped record of what the tester did: which tool, against which target, with which options, from which source address, and when. This is the single most valuable record if anything goes wrong, because it lets you and the client's defenders match your actions against their logs.

**Request and response captures.** For web and network findings, the exact request sent and the exact response received are the core proof. Captures are better than screenshots because they are text: they can be searched, hashed and quoted precisely.

**Tool output.** The saved output of every tool run, unedited. Edited output is not evidence. If you must remove sensitive values for the report, do so in a copy and keep the original under integrity protection.

**Screenshots and recordings.** Useful for graphical interfaces and for showing a non-technical reader what an attacker would see. Include the clock and the URL or hostname in the frame.

**Findings records.** A structured record of each confirmed finding: what it is, how it was verified, the evidence, and when. In the lab, `verify.py` writes these to `data/findings.json`, and that file is the evidence that `report.py` later turns into a report.

**Negative evidence.** A record that you checked something and it was *not* vulnerable. This matters more than beginners expect. "We tested the login for default credentials and they were not accepted" is a statement of coverage; without a record of the check, the client cannot tell whether you tested it at all.

## Qualities of good evidence

An item of evidence is only useful if it has these properties:

- **Reproducible.** It records the exact action and the exact result, so that a developer can repeat it and a retest can confirm the fix.
- **Attributable.** It records who captured it and from where (tester name, workstation, source address).
- **Timestamped.** It records when, in an unambiguous time format, from a synchronised clock.
- **Minimal.** It proves the finding without collecting more than is needed. One record proves an insecure direct object reference; a dump of ten thousand customer records proves nothing extra and creates a data-protection problem.
- **Integral.** Its integrity can be verified later, because a hash was computed at capture and stored separately.
- **In context.** It is linked to the finding it supports, the scope item it concerns, and the step in the activity log that produced it.

The lab's findings records show most of these in a compact form. Here is the first entry in `LAB_ASSETS/C05/data/findings.json`, exactly as saved by `verify.py`:

```json
{
  "id": "default-credential",
  "title": "Default administrator credential (admin/admin)",
  "vector": "AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H",
  "evidence": "admin/admin accepted, returns administrator role",
  "verified_utc": "2026-11-08T00:46:25Z"
}
```

The `id` links the record to the check that produced it and to the remediation and retest step in the report. The `evidence` field states the observable fact, not an opinion. The `verified_utc` field is a UTC timestamp. What the record does *not* carry is also instructive: the IDOR record says "unauthenticated read of another customer's invoice 1002" but does not copy the customer's name, item or amount into the evidence. The check in `verify.py` confirmed that the response belonged to another customer; the record states that fact and leaves the personal data behind. That is minimisation in practice.

A professional record would also carry attribution (who ran the check, from which host) and a pointer to the full request and response capture.

## Timestamps that mean something

A timestamp is only useful if two people reading it later agree on what moment it refers to. Three rules achieve that.

**Use UTC and say so.** The lab records `2026-11-08T00:46:25Z`. This is the ISO 8601 / RFC 3339 format: date, the letter `T`, time, and `Z` meaning UTC (sometimes called "Zulu" time). Writing local time without a zone ("08/11/26 6:16") is ambiguous twice over: is it 8 November or 11 August, and which time zone? India Standard Time is UTC+05:30, so the lab's verification moment was 06:16:25 IST on 8 November 2026. If you must record local time for a client, include the offset, for example `2026-11-08T06:16:25+05:30`.

**Synchronise the clock.** A precise format on a clock that is ten minutes wrong produces precisely wrong evidence. Keep test workstations synchronised with NTP, and record the time source in the engagement notes. In India, the CERT-In Directions of April 2022 (Lesson 5) require the covered organisations to synchronise their system clocks with the NTP servers of the National Informatics Centre (NIC) or the National Physical Laboratory (NPL), or with servers traceable to them. A tester whose clock follows the same source makes correlation with the client's logs straightforward.

**Generate timestamps by machine.** Tools should stamp their own records at the moment of capture, as `verify.py` does with `time.strftime("%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ", time.gmtime())`. Times typed by hand later are reconstructions, not records.

> [!TIP]
> At the start and end of each testing session, write a line in the activity log with the time and your source address, and notify the client contact if the rules of engagement require it. These bracket entries make it easy for defenders to separate test traffic from everything else.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each downward arrow means the item passes to the next stage only when the current stage is complete. An item that has not been hashed should not enter storage, because from that point on nobody can prove it was not altered there. An item whose hash does not verify at transfer should not be used as proof until the discrepancy is explained.

**Execution flow.** Read the figure from top to bottom. Capture and hashing happen within seconds of each other; custody logging starts at the same time; storage, transfer and verification repeat as often as the item changes hands; retention and destruction happen after the report is delivered.

**Decision logic.** The figure is linear, but two decisions sit inside it. At capture, the tester decides what is the minimum that proves the finding. At transfer, the receiver decides whether the recomputed hash matches the manifest; a mismatch stops the flow.

**Technical interpretation.** Hashing early and storing the hash separately from the item is what turns "I am sure I did not change it" into something verifiable. The custody log supplies the human context that a hash cannot: a hash proves a file is unchanged, not who had it.

**Practical example.** In the course lab, `verify.py` performs Capture and writes `data/findings.json`. You then compute its SHA-256 (`4bd809c8...a590e9a4` for the saved kit file), record it with the time and your name, keep the file in the lab folder, verify the hash before `report.py` reads it, and finally delete `data/` as the kit's README instructs.

**Security implications.** The riskiest stages are storage and retention, because that is where evidence sits for longest and where breaches and forgotten copies happen. Encrypted storage and an enforced retention date reduce both risks.

**Key learning.** Every item of evidence is captured, hashed, logged, stored, verified at hand-off, retained only as agreed and then destroyed with a record. Skipping any step weakens all the later ones.

## Integrity: hashing evidence with SHA-256

### What a hash function guarantees

A cryptographic hash function takes input of any length and produces a fixed-length digest. SHA-256, specified in NIST FIPS 180-4, produces 256 bits, conventionally written as 64 hexadecimal characters. Three properties make it useful for evidence:

- **Deterministic.** The same bytes always produce the same digest, on any computer, with any correct implementation. That is why a client can verify your hash with a different tool from the one you used.
- **Sensitive to every bit.** Changing a single character anywhere in the file produces a completely different digest, with no visible relationship to the original (the "avalanche" behaviour). There is no such thing as a "nearly matching" hash.
- **Collision resistant.** It is computationally infeasible to find two different inputs with the same SHA-256 digest, or to construct a modified file that matches a given digest. Nobody can quietly edit the evidence and adjust it to keep the old hash.

Older functions lack the third property. Practical collisions were demonstrated for MD5 in 2004 and for SHA-1 in 2017, so neither should be used to protect evidence today. You may still see MD5 values in older tools and reports; treat them as identifiers, not integrity proof.

Two limits matter just as much. A hash proves a file is unchanged *since the hash was computed*, so you hash at capture. And it does not prove *who* produced the file: anyone who can change the file and the recorded hash together defeats it, which is why the digest is stored separately (in the custody record or a signed manifest).

### Computing the hash of the lab evidence

The kit's saved `data/findings.json` is a 1,109-byte file. Its SHA-256, computed with GNU coreutils in a Bash shell from the `LAB_ASSETS/C05` folder:

```terminal
$ sha256sum data/findings.json
4bd809c804aba2c39647eade0c3e012aad58315bffd66c9af13edc53a590e9a4 *data/findings.json
```

`sha256sum` takes one or more file names and prints, for each, the digest followed by the file name. The `*` before the name means the file was read in binary mode, which is how this Windows build reports it; on Linux you will usually see two spaces instead. The digest is what matters.

The same file in Windows PowerShell:

```terminal
PS> Get-FileHash -Algorithm SHA256 .\data\findings.json | Select-Object Algorithm, Hash | Format-List

Algorithm : SHA256
Hash      : 4BD809C804ABA2C39647EADE0C3E012AAD58315BFFD66C9AF13EDC53A590E9A4
```

`Get-FileHash` computes the digest; `-Algorithm SHA256` names the algorithm explicitly (also the default, but stating it removes doubt); `Select-Object` keeps two fields and `Format-List` prints one per line. PowerShell prints hexadecimal in upper case; hexadecimal is case-insensitive, so this is the same value.

Python's standard `hashlib.sha256` gives the same digest. Independent tools agreeing on one value is exactly what makes a hash a good integrity check.

### Verifying a set of files with a manifest

In a real engagement you hash many files, so you write a manifest once and check it at every hand-off. Running `sha256sum data/findings.json data/report_output.txt > evidence.sha256` writes the two lines, and `-c` checks them:

```terminal
$ cat evidence.sha256
4bd809c804aba2c39647eade0c3e012aad58315bffd66c9af13edc53a590e9a4 *data/findings.json
44fca1b8ecd5e6ac03f292cc6f5c2ac31128b81a40d3f5f8cfaa2f0616e0b04c *data/report_output.txt
$ sha256sum -c evidence.sha256
data/findings.json: OK
data/report_output.txt: OK
```

`-c` reads the manifest, recomputes each file's digest and reports `OK` or `FAILED`. To see what tampering looks like, a working copy of the folder was used and one character in `findings.json` was changed (invoice `1002` to `1003`):

```terminal
$ sha256sum -c evidence.sha256
data/findings.json: FAILED
data/report_output.txt: OK
sha256sum: WARNING: 1 computed checksum did NOT match
```

The command also exits with status 1, so a script can stop automatically. One changed digit, a completely failed check, and the untouched file still verifies: the manifest tells you exactly which item can no longer be trusted.

### Why rerunning a tool changes the hash

A subtle point catches many beginners. Running `verify.py` again in a copy of the kit on 14 November 2026 confirmed the same four findings, but the new `findings.json` hashed to `49ffc9cdbc11aaebe5dfcae04c85377b74a007a4e87f2696c63797345f735471`. Nothing about the findings changed; the `verified_utc` timestamps did (the new file records `2026-11-14T22:14:07Z`). A hash protects bytes, not meaning.

So every capture is a separate item of evidence with its own hash and custody entry. Because `verify.py` overwrites `data/findings.json`, copy and hash the earlier file before rerunning if anything cites it; professional tooling writes each run to a new, timestamped file.

## Chain of custody

A hash answers "has this changed?". A chain of custody answers "who has had it, and could anyone have changed it and the hash together?". It is a chronological log, kept from capture to destruction, in which every entry records:

- the item (an identifier, file name and SHA-256);
- the action (captured, copied, transferred, accessed, verified, destroyed);
- who performed it, and who received it at a hand-off;
- the date and time (UTC) and the location (system or storage path);
- the purpose (for example "peer review of finding F2").

A minimal custody log for the lab evidence might read as follows. The people are fictional; the hash is the real digest of the kit file.

| Time (UTC) | Item / SHA-256 (first 16) | Action | By | To / location | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2026-11-08 00:46 | EV-01 findings.json / 4bd809c804aba2c3 | Captured by verify.py; hashed | A. Tester | Lab workstation, encrypted volume | Engagement evidence |
| 2026-11-08 09:10 | EV-01 / 4bd809c804aba2c3 | Transferred and verified OK | A. Tester | R. Reviewer | Peer review |
| 2026-11-09 11:30 | EV-01 / 4bd809c804aba2c3 | Delivered with report; verified OK | R. Reviewer | Client CISO (fictional) | Report appendix |
| 2026-12-09 10:00 | EV-01 / 4bd809c804aba2c3 | Tester copy destroyed | A. Tester | Encrypted volume sanitised | Retention period ended |

The value of this log is that every gap is visible. If the item turns up somewhere not listed, or the hash at one entry differs from the one before, the log shows exactly where custody broke.

**Purpose.** The figure shows the message exchange when evidence passes from the tester to a reviewer, with the custody log as a third party in the exchange. It matters because a hand-off is the moment when evidence is most easily swapped, corrupted or lost, and the figure shows how hashing and logging make that detectable.

**Components.** There are three lanes. **Tester** ("captures and hashes") is the person who created the evidence. **Custody log** ("separate record") is where hashes and hand-offs are written, deliberately kept apart from the evidence files. **Reviewer** ("peer or client") is the person receiving the evidence.

**Relationships and arrows.** The first arrow, **Record SHA-256 at capture**, runs from the Tester to the Custody log: the hash is written down before the file goes anywhere. The second, **Evidence file + manifest**, runs from the Tester to the Reviewer and is the transfer itself. The third, **Fetch recorded hash**, runs from the Reviewer to the Custody log: the reviewer obtains the expected value independently. The dashed reply **Expected SHA-256** returns that value. The Reviewer then recomputes the digest, and the next arrow, **Log: received, hash OK (or FAILED)**, records the result in the custody log. The final dashed arrow, **Acknowledge receipt**, tells the Tester that custody has passed.

**Execution flow.** Record, transfer, fetch, compare, log, acknowledge. The order matters: the hash must be recorded before transfer, and the comparison must use the separately recorded value, not only the manifest that travelled with the file.

**Decision logic.** The reviewer's comparison is the decision point. A match means the item is accepted and custody passes. A mismatch means the item is quarantined, the custody log records `FAILED`, and the tester is asked to re-send from the original or explain the change; the item is not cited in the report until the discrepancy is resolved.

**Technical interpretation.** The manifest that travels with the files is convenient but not enough on its own, because anyone who could alter the file in transit could alter the manifest too. Fetching the expected hash from the custody log, a second channel, closes that gap. Organisations that need stronger proof sign the manifest, so that the signature proves who vouched for the hashes.

**Practical example.** In the lab, act as both roles. Record `4bd809c8...a590e9a4` for `data/findings.json` in a text file outside the lab folder, copy the folder elsewhere, run `sha256sum -c` there, and log the result with the time.

**Security implications.** Defenders reviewing a test benefit too: when evidence of a serious finding reaches the client with a verifiable hash and an unbroken log, nobody can later claim it was fabricated, and the tester cannot be accused of altering it.

**Key learning.** A hand-off is trusted only when the receiver recomputes the hash and compares it with a value recorded separately, and logs the result.

## Storing evidence safely

Evidence must be at least as well protected as the systems it came from.

- **Encrypt at rest.** Keep engagement data on an encrypted volume or in an encrypted container, so that a lost or stolen laptop does not become a data breach.
- **Control access.** Only the people named on the engagement should be able to read the evidence. Keep one folder per engagement.
- **Use client-approved locations.** The rules of engagement say where evidence may be stored and how it is transferred; personal cloud-sync folders, chat apps and personal email are out unless the client agrees.
- **Separate evidence from tools.** In the lab kit the scripts sit at the top of `LAB_ASSETS/C05/` and every output goes into `data/`.

## Minimisation and personal data

Testers routinely see data they were never meant to see; prove the exposure with the smallest possible sample and keep nothing more. For an insecure direct object reference, one record showing that a different customer's data is returned is enough.

Where the evidence contains personal data, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (Lesson 5) applies to how it is handled. In a typical engagement the client is the data fiduciary and the testing firm processes the data on the client's behalf, so the contract should say what may be collected, where it is stored and when it is erased. In the report, redact personal values (show "customer B" rather than a name), keep the unredacted original only if the client needs it, and record in the custody log that redaction was done on a copy.

> [!WARNING]
> Never take personal or sensitive data out of the client's environment "to analyse later" unless the rules of engagement explicitly allow it. Prove the finding where you found it, record the minimum, and move on.

## Retention and secure destruction

The contract or rules of engagement should state a retention period: long enough to answer questions and support a retest, and no longer. When the period ends, every copy held by the testers (working folders, backups, removable media, report drafts) must be destroyed, and the destruction recorded.

Deleting a file is not destroying it. On most file systems deletion only marks the space as free, and the data remains until it is overwritten. NIST SP 800-88, *Guidelines for Media Sanitization*, groups sanitisation into three levels:

- **Clear**: overwriting user-addressable storage with standard read and write commands, which protects against simple recovery tools.
- **Purge**: techniques that defeat laboratory-grade recovery, such as a drive's built-in secure-erase command or cryptographic erase (destroying the encryption key of an encrypted volume, which leaves the remaining data unreadable).
- **Destroy**: physical destruction of the media, such as shredding or disintegration.

For solid-state drives, overwriting a single file is unreliable, because the drive's wear-levelling may write the new data somewhere else and leave the old blocks intact. Keeping evidence in an encrypted container from the start means destroying the container and its key is an effective cryptographic erase.

The final custody entry for each item records what was destroyed, how, by whom and when. Many firms then send the client a short **certificate of destruction** listing the items and the method, which closes the custody chain from the client's side too.

## Evidence and the law in India

Electronic records were made admissible in Indian courts through section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, which required a certificate describing how the record was produced; the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced that Act from 1 July 2024, carries a similar certificate requirement for electronic records. The questions a court asks (how was this produced, by whom, has it been altered?) are the ones timestamps, hashes and a custody log answer.

If you find signs of a real intrusion by someone else, stop, inform the client contact, preserve what you have under custody, and let the client and counsel decide on reporting, for example to CERT-In (Lesson 5). This is not legal advice.

## Step-by-step: handling the lab evidence

The course lab is a safe rehearsal of the whole discipline. Work from `LAB_ASSETS/C05/` on your own machine; the only target is the loopback service you start yourself (Lesson 7).

1. **Note the retention rule.** Section 5 of `scope.md` says evidence in `data/` is lab-only and fictional and is deleted when finished.
2. **Start an activity log** outside `data/`, with the UTC time, your name and "source 127.0.0.1" as the first line.
3. **Hash before use.** Confirm `data/findings.json` hashes to `4bd809c8...a590e9a4` and record the digest in a separate custody file.
4. **Preserve before you rerun.** `verify.py` overwrites `findings.json`, so copy the kit's file to a new name first.
5. **Capture and hash new evidence.** Start `target/app.py`, run `verify.py`, and hash the new file at once; expect a new digest because your timestamps differ.
6. **Simulate a hand-off.** Write a manifest of `data/`, copy the folder elsewhere and check it there with `sha256sum -c`.
7. **Close out.** Stop the target with Ctrl-C, delete `data/`, and write the final custody entry.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"A screenshot is enough."** A screenshot without the request that produced it, a timestamp and the target's address cannot be reproduced and is easily disputed. Capture the text, and add the screenshot as illustration.
- **"I'll write up my notes at the end of the day."** Notes reconstructed later miss the exact parameters, the order of actions and the times. Log as you go, and let tools timestamp their own output.
- **"I hashed it, so it is safe."** A hash detects change; it does not prevent it, hide the data or prove authorship. It needs separate storage of the digest, access control and a custody log.
- **"More data makes a stronger finding."** It makes a bigger liability. One record proves an access-control failure as well as a thousand.
- **"Deleting the folder destroys the evidence."** Deletion leaves recoverable data and forgets backups and copies. Sanitise properly, cover every copy, and record it.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Evidence handling protects three parties. The **client**'s sensitive data is concentrated in the evidence: a leaked evidence folder exposes credentials, personal data and a map of weaknesses at once. The **tester** may need to show exactly what they did if a system failed or scope is disputed. And the **defenders** can match a timestamped activity log with source addresses against their alerts, measure which activities they detected and which they missed, and spot any activity that was *not* the tester's. A gap between the tester's log and the defenders' alerts is itself a useful detection finding.

## Best practices

- Agree evidence storage, transfer, retention and destruction in the rules of engagement before testing starts.
- Keep a running activity log with UTC times, source addresses and session start and end entries.
- Capture text unedited; use screenshots as supplements.
- Hash every item at capture with SHA-256, store digests separately, and verify at every hand-off.
- Minimise personal data, redact on copies, and destroy every copy at the end of retention with a recorded method.

## Key takeaways

- Evidence is every record that supports the report, including the authorisation, the activity log and negative results, not only screenshots of findings.
- Good evidence is reproducible, attributable, timestamped in UTC from a synchronised clock, minimal and integrity-protected.
- SHA-256 detects any change to a file; hash at capture, store the digest separately, and verify at every hand-off. MD5 and SHA-1 are no longer adequate.
- A chain of custody records who held each item, when, where and why, so that any break is visible.
- Store evidence encrypted and access-controlled, minimise personal data under the DPDP Act 2023, retain only for the agreed period, and destroy every copy with a recorded, appropriate sanitisation method.
