## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A scanner result, an advisory from a vendor and a line in a penetration-test report all need to refer to the same vulnerability in a way that everyone understands, and they all need to say how serious it is in a way that can be reproduced and argued about. Two public systems solve these two problems. **CVE** (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) gives each publicly disclosed vulnerability in a specific product a unique identifier. **CVSS** (the Common Vulnerability Scoring System) gives each vulnerability a numeric severity from 0.0 to 10.0, calculated from a small set of observable characteristics by a published formula.

Neither system is a risk assessment on its own, and confusing them with risk is one of the most common mistakes in vulnerability management (Lesson 6 deals with prioritisation). But you cannot do any serious assessment work without reading, writing and checking CVE identifiers and CVSS vectors fluently. This lesson explains both in depth, including the exact CVSS v3.1 base equations, and uses the course lab kit's offline calculator, `LAB_ASSETS/C05/cvss.py`, to score the four confirmed lab findings.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| CVE ID | An identifier of the form `CVE-YYYY-NNNN` (the sequence part has four or more digits) naming one publicly disclosed vulnerability. |
| CVE Record | The published entry for a CVE ID: description, affected products and versions, references, and optionally CVSS, CWE and other data. |
| CNA | CVE Numbering Authority: an organisation (often a vendor, a CERT or a research body) authorised to assign CVE IDs within its scope. |
| CVE Program | The programme that operates CVE, run by MITRE with sponsorship from the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). |
| NVD | The US National Vulnerability Database run by NIST, which enriches CVE Records with CVSS scores, CWE classifications and CPE applicability data. |
| CWE | Common Weakness Enumeration: a catalogue of *types* of weakness (for example CWE-639, Authorization Bypass Through User-Controlled Key), independent of any product. |
| CPE | Common Platform Enumeration: a structured name for products, for example `cpe:2.3:a:vendor:product:version:...`, used to state which products a CVE affects. |
| CVSS vector | A compact string recording the metric values used to compute a score, such as `CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H`. |
| Base score | The CVSS score computed from intrinsic characteristics of the vulnerability; it does not change over time or between environments. |

## CVE: naming a specific vulnerability

### What a CVE identifies

A CVE identifies *one vulnerability in one or more specific products and versions*. The well-known Log4Shell flaw in Apache Log4j 2, for example, is CVE-2021-44228. The year in the identifier generally reflects when the ID was reserved or when the vulnerability was made public, not when the flaw was introduced or discovered, so do not infer the age of the flaw from it. The sequence number carries no meaning beyond uniqueness.

A CVE does *not* identify a class of weakness. "SQL injection" is not a CVE; it is CWE-89. A SQL injection in a particular release of a particular product, once disclosed and assigned, is a CVE. This distinction matters every day in penetration testing: most of what you find in a client's custom application is a weakness *type* (name it with CWE), whereas most of what a network scanner reports on commercial and open-source software is a *known instance* (cite the CVE).

### How a CVE Record comes to exist

The lifecycle runs through several parties. A researcher or the vendor discovers a vulnerability and reports it to the appropriate CNA. The CNA reserves an ID, which can sit in the *Reserved* state while a fix is prepared under coordinated disclosure. When the CNA publishes the record, its state becomes *Published* and it appears in the CVE List. Downstream databases, chiefly the NVD, then enrich it: analysts or the CNA attach CVSS vectors, a CWE classification and CPE configurations that state precisely which product versions are affected. Scanner vendors write checks referencing the CVE, and advisories, patches and threat intelligence all cite it. A record found to be a duplicate or not a vulnerability is moved to the *Rejected* state.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure traces how a vulnerability becomes a CVE Record and then becomes usable data inside scanners and reports. It matters because the quality of everything a scanner tells you about a CVE depends on this chain, and gaps in the chain explain many confusing results.

**Components.** *Discovery and report* is the researcher or vendor finding the flaw. *CNA reserves an ID* is the numbering authority allocating a `CVE-YYYY-NNNN` identifier, which can stay *Reserved* during coordinated disclosure. *Record published* is the moment the description, affected products and references become public in the CVE List. *Enrichment (NVD and others)* adds CVSS vectors, CWE and CPE applicability. *Scanner feeds and advisories* are the consumers that turn the record into checks and patches. *Your finding* is the endpoint: a result in your assessment citing the CVE.

**Relationships and arrows.** The arrows run left to right because information flows downstream from discovery to consumers. The publication step is the gate: before it, almost nobody outside the vendor and reporter knows the flaw exists; after it, defenders and attackers learn about it at the same moment. The enrichment step is shown separately because it can lag publication by days or more.

**Execution flow.** Discover, report, reserve, fix and publish, enrich, consume. In a healthy coordinated disclosure, the vendor's patch and the CVE publication appear together so that defenders can act immediately.

**Decision logic.** At the CNA stage the decision is whether the issue qualifies as a vulnerability within the CNA's scope; if not, no ID is assigned. After publication, a record can still be rejected as a duplicate or invalid. During enrichment, analysts decide which CPE configurations apply, and that decision directly controls which products a version-based scanner check will flag.

**Technical interpretation.** Scanners match the CPE identity they fingerprint on your target against the CPE configurations in enriched records. If enrichment is missing or wrong, version-based checks may not fire (a false negative) or may fire on unaffected builds (a false positive). Distribution vendors also publish their own advisories mapping a CVE to fixed package versions, which is why authenticated, package-based checks are more precise than remote version guesses.

**Practical example.** Suppose a scan of a lab VM reports an OpenSSH CVE based on the SSH banner. Reading the CVE Record tells you the affected upstream versions; reading the distribution's own security tracker tells you which package release contains the backported fix. Comparing the installed package (Lesson 4) with that fixed release settles whether the result is real.

**Security implications.** The window between publication and patching is when exploitation risk is highest, because the record itself tells attackers where to look. Defenders who monitor new CVEs for the products in their inventory, rather than waiting for the next quarterly scan, shorten that window.

**Key learning.** A CVE is an identifier for a specific known flaw; its usefulness in a scan depends on enrichment data, especially CPE and the vendor's fixed versions.

### CVE, CWE and CPE together

The three enumerations answer different questions and are used together. CVE answers "which known flaw?", CWE answers "what kind of mistake?", and CPE answers "which product and version?". An enriched record for a SQL injection in a fictional product release would therefore carry a CVE ID, the classification CWE-89, and CPE entries listing the affected versions. When you report a flaw you found yourself in a custom application, there is usually no CVE: you name it with a CWE (Lesson 7) and describe the affected component in your own words.

## CVSS v3.1: scoring severity

CVSS is maintained by FIRST (the Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams). Version 3.1, published in 2019, is still the version most scanners, advisories and reports use, although version 4.0 (published in November 2023) is being adopted; a short comparison appears later in this lesson. CVSS v3.1 has three metric groups:

- **Base** metrics describe intrinsic characteristics that do not change: how the flaw is reached and what it does.
- **Temporal** metrics adjust for factors that change over time: Exploit Code Maturity (E), Remediation Level (RL) and Report Confidence (RC).
- **Environmental** metrics adjust for a particular organisation: Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability Requirements (CR, IR, AR) and modified versions of the base metrics.

Penetration-test reports almost always state the base score and vector; this lesson focuses on the base group.

### The eight base metrics

The **exploitability** metrics describe how hard it is to exploit the vulnerability:

- **Attack Vector (AV)**: how remote the attacker can be. Network (N) means reachable across routed networks, including the internet; Adjacent (A) means the same physical or logical segment (for example the same Wi-Fi or VLAN); Local (L) means the attacker needs local access or must trick a local user into running something; Physical (P) means physical contact with the device.
- **Attack Complexity (AC)**: conditions beyond the attacker's control that must exist. Low (L) means the attack works reliably at will; High (H) means it depends on circumstances such as winning a race or gathering target-specific secrets.
- **Privileges Required (PR)**: the privilege level the attacker needs *before* the attack. None (N), Low (L) or High (H).
- **User Interaction (UI)**: whether a user other than the attacker must take part. None (N) or Required (R), for example clicking a link.

**Scope (S)** asks whether a successful attack affects resources beyond the security authority of the vulnerable component. Unchanged (U) means the impact stays within the same authority; Changed (C) means it crosses a boundary, for example a flaw in a web application that executes script in a victim's browser, or an escape from a virtual machine into its hypervisor.

The **impact** metrics describe the consequence for the *impacted* component: **Confidentiality (C)**, **Integrity (I)** and **Availability (A)**, each High (H), Low (L) or None (N). High means total loss or a serious, direct consequence; Low means partial or limited loss.

### Metric weights

The specification assigns each metric value a numeric weight. These are the exact v3.1 values implemented in `cvss.py`:

| Metric | Values and weights |
| --- | --- |
| Attack Vector | N 0.85, A 0.62, L 0.55, P 0.20 |
| Attack Complexity | L 0.77, H 0.44 |
| Privileges Required (Scope Unchanged) | N 0.85, L 0.62, H 0.27 |
| Privileges Required (Scope Changed) | N 0.85, L 0.68, H 0.50 |
| User Interaction | N 0.85, R 0.62 |
| Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability | H 0.56, L 0.22, N 0.00 |

Notice that Privileges Required is the only metric whose weight depends on Scope: when an attack crosses a security boundary, needing privileges in the vulnerable component reduces the score less.

### The base equations

The calculation has four steps, defined in section 7 of the FIRST v3.1 specification.

First, the **Impact Sub-Score (ISS)** combines the three impacts so that they cannot exceed total loss:

$$\text{ISS} = 1 - (1 - C)(1 - I)(1 - A)$$

Second, the **Impact** is scaled according to Scope:

$$\text{Impact}_{S:U} = 6.42 \times \text{ISS}$$

$$\text{Impact}_{S:C} = 7.52 \times (\text{ISS} - 0.029) - 3.25 \times (\text{ISS} - 0.02)^{15}$$

Third, **Exploitability** multiplies the four exploitability weights:

$$\text{Exploitability} = 8.22 \times AV \times AC \times PR \times UI$$

Fourth, the **Base Score**. If Impact is zero or less, the base score is 0.0. Otherwise, for Scope Unchanged it is $\text{Roundup}(\min(\text{Impact} + \text{Exploitability}, 10))$, and for Scope Changed it is $\text{Roundup}(\min(1.08 \times (\text{Impact} + \text{Exploitability}), 10))$. *Roundup* returns the smallest number with one decimal place that is equal to or greater than its input; v3.1's Appendix A defines it with an integer step so that floating-point noise (a value such as 4.000000000000001) does not push a score up by 0.1. The lab calculator implements exactly this function.

The qualitative severity rating then follows from the score: None 0.0, Low 0.1 to 3.9, Medium 4.0 to 6.9, High 7.0 to 8.9, Critical 9.0 to 10.0.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the CVSS v3.1 base calculation as a pipeline from the eight metric values in a vector string to a numeric score and a severity rating. It matters because a score is only defensible if you can show how each metric produced it.

**Components.** *Vector string* is the input, for example `AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H`. *Impact Sub-Score* combines C, I and A. *Impact* scales the ISS according to Scope. *Exploitability* multiplies AV, AC, PR and UI with the constant 8.22. *Combine and Roundup* adds the two parts, applies the Scope Changed multiplier where relevant, caps at 10 and rounds up to one decimal. *Severity rating* maps the result into None, Low, Medium, High or Critical.

**Relationships and arrows.** The vector feeds two parallel branches, impact and exploitability, which meet at the combine step. Scope is drawn as a decision because it changes three things: the impact formula, the Privileges Required weight and the 1.08 multiplier.

**Execution flow.** Parse the vector, look up weights, compute ISS, compute Impact, compute Exploitability, check for zero impact, combine, cap, round up, then rate.

**Decision logic.** Two decisions matter. If Impact is zero (C, I and A all None), the score is 0.0 regardless of how easy exploitation is: a flaw that does nothing has no severity. If Scope is Changed, the Changed formulas and multiplier apply, which is how vectors can reach 10.0.

**Technical interpretation.** The impact formula for Scope Changed is non-linear, so impacts saturate near the top. The 8.22 constant makes the best-case exploitability (N, L, N, N) equal 3.887, and the best-case Scope Unchanged impact equal about 5.873, which together give 9.76 and round up to 9.8. That is why the most severe Scope Unchanged vector scores 9.8 and only Scope Changed vectors reach 10.0.

**Practical example.** For the lab's default administrator credential, the vector is `AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:H/A:H`. ISS is $1 - 0.44^3 = 0.914816$; Impact is $6.42 \times 0.914816 = 5.873$; Exploitability is $8.22 \times 0.85 \times 0.77 \times 0.85 \times 0.85 = 3.887$; the sum, 9.760, rounds up to 9.8, Critical.

**Security implications.** Because every step is deterministic, two analysts who agree on the eight metric values must agree on the score. Disagreements therefore move to the metrics themselves, which is where they belong: is the service really reachable from the network, or only from an adjacent segment?

**Key learning.** A CVSS base score is a deterministic function of eight metrics; always publish the vector so the score can be reproduced and challenged.

The README records that the calculator returns 7.5, 10.0 and 9.8 for its sample vectors. Running it on the Scope Changed reference vector:

```terminal
$ python cvss.py CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:C/C:H/I:H/A:H

impact SC   : 6.0
exploitability: 3.9
BASE SCORE  : 10.0 (Critical)
```

The single argument is the vector; the `CVSS:3.1/` prefix is optional. The output prints the vector back in canonical order, the Impact value (labelled `impact SC`, the impact sub-score after Scope scaling), the Exploitability value, both to one decimal for display, and the base score with severity. Here Impact is about 6.05 and Exploitability 3.887; $1.08 \times 9.935 = 10.73$, capped at 10.0.

The missing HTTP security headers:

```terminal
$ python cvss.py AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:R/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N
vector      : CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:H/PR:N/UI:R/S:U/C:L/I:N/A:N
impact SC   : 1.4
exploitability: 1.6
BASE SCORE  : 3.1 (Low)
```

Interpret each vector by reasoning about the metrics, not just the number.

1. **Default credential, 9.8.** Anyone who can reach the portal over the network (AV:N) can log in reliably (AC:L) without an account (PR:N) and without a victim's help (UI:N). The administrator role gives full control of the application's data and functions, so C, I and A are all High. Scope is Unchanged because the impact stays within the application's own authority.
2. **IDOR, 7.5.** The same easy exploitability, but the impact is reading another customer's invoice: Confidentiality High, no direct integrity or availability effect. Impact drops to 3.6, giving 7.5.
3. **Verbose error, 5.3.** Exploitability is identical; the leak is partial (internal query detail), so C:L. Impact 1.41 plus 3.887 gives 5.30, which is already exactly 5.3.
4. **Missing headers, 3.1.** The absence of headers only matters if another flaw (such as a script-injection weakness) is exploited and a user is involved, so AC:H and UI:R; impact is limited (C:L). Exploitability falls to $8.22 \times 0.85 \times 0.44 \times 0.85 \times 0.62 = 1.620$ and the total of 3.033 rounds up to 3.1.

These are the same scores `report.py` attaches in `data/report_output.txt`: 9.8, 7.5, 5.3 and 3.1. If you leave a metric out, the calculator refuses rather than guessing:

```terminal
$ python cvss.py AV:N/AC:L
missing metrics: PR,UI,S,C,I,A
```

## CVSS v4.0 in brief

CVSS v4.0 renames and extends the groups: Base, Threat (replacing Temporal, now mainly Exploit Maturity), Environmental and a new Supplemental group that carries extra information without changing the score. It adds an Attack Requirements (AT) metric, splits User Interaction into None, Passive and Active, and replaces Scope with separate impact metrics for the vulnerable system and for subsequent systems. It also introduces naming such as CVSS-B (base only) and CVSS-BT (base plus threat) so that readers know which groups contributed. Scores from v3.1 and v4.0 are not interchangeable, so a report must always state the version alongside each vector. This course uses v3.1 because it is what the lab calculator implements and what most scanner feeds still present.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Using a CVE number for a weakness class**, or a CWE for a specific product flaw.
- **Inferring the age of a flaw from the CVE year.** The year reflects reservation or publication, not when the bug was introduced or found.
- **Copying the NVD score without checking the vector.** The published vector reflects general conditions; your finding may differ (a service reachable only on an adjacent network, for example).
- **Scoring the worst imaginable chain.** Score the vulnerability as found. If it is only exploitable after another flaw, reflect that through Attack Complexity, Privileges Required or User Interaction, and describe the chain in the narrative.
- **Treating the base score as risk.** CVSS measures technical severity. Risk also depends on likelihood of exploitation, asset value and exposure (Lesson 6).
- **Dropping the vector from the report.** A bare "7.5" cannot be checked; `CVSS:3.1/AV:N/AC:L/PR:N/UI:N/S:U/C:H/I:N/A:N` can.

## Security implications, detection and defence

For defenders, CVE monitoring and CVSS scoring are the foundation of vulnerability management. An organisation that maintains an accurate inventory (with CPE-like product and version data) can match newly published CVEs to its assets on the day of publication. CVSS then gives a first ordering, refined by threat intelligence and business context. CERT-In regularly issues vulnerability notes and advisories referencing CVE IDs for products widely used in India, and security teams should subscribe to them alongside vendor advisories.

For testers, rigorous scoring is a matter of professional integrity. Inflated scores cause clients to spend money in the wrong place and erode trust; deflated scores leave real risk unaddressed. Publishing the vector, the version and a sentence justifying each metric is the defence against both.

## Best practices

1. Cite the CVE ID for known product vulnerabilities and the CWE ID for weakness types; use both when both apply.
2. Read the CVE Record and the vendor or distribution advisory, not just the scanner's summary.
3. Always state the CVSS version and full vector with each score.
4. Justify every metric in one short phrase in your working notes.
5. Re-score when the conditions on your target differ from the published vector, and explain why.
6. Validate any calculator you use against known reference vectors before relying on it.

## Key takeaways

- CVE names a specific disclosed flaw; CWE names a weakness type; CPE names products and versions.
- CVE Records are assigned by CNAs, published in the CVE List and enriched by the NVD and others.
- CVSS v3.1 base scores are a deterministic function of eight metrics: AV, AC, PR, UI, S, C, I, A.
- The equations combine an impact sub-score and an exploitability sub-score, apply Scope, cap at 10 and round up to one decimal.
- The lab findings score 9.8, 7.5, 5.3 and 3.1; always publish the vector with the score.
