## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Reconnaissance is the discovery phase of a penetration test: the work of learning what exists before you test how it behaves. Every later phase depends on it. You cannot scan a host you never found, you cannot test an endpoint nobody listed, and you cannot rank the risk of a service whose purpose you do not understand. Poor reconnaissance fails a test in two opposite ways. It can **miss assets**, so that a forgotten server or an old login page is never examined and the report gives the client false comfort. Or it can **act on bad information**, so that the tester spends a day attacking a stale record, a lookalike host or, worst of all, a system that belongs to someone else.

NIST SP 800-115 places this work in its **Discovery** phase, which sits between Planning and Attack and receives feedback from Attack whenever new information appears. PTES calls the same stage *Intelligence Gathering*. MITRE ATT&CK, which describes what real adversaries do, has a whole tactic for it: **Reconnaissance (TA0043)**, with techniques such as *Active Scanning* (T1595), *Search Open Websites/Domains* (T1593) and *Search Open Technical Databases* (T1596). The fact that adversaries spend serious effort here is the best argument for doing it well yourself: whatever an attacker can learn about your client from the outside, the client should learn first, from you, in a report.

The most important distinction inside reconnaissance is between **passive** and **active** collection. It decides how much risk an action carries, how visible it is to the target, and, above all, which rules govern it. This lesson defines the two modes precisely, explains the grey zone between them, and shows how the rules of engagement from Module 1 apply to each.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In this course every technique runs inside written authorisation and an isolated lab you own. "Passive" does not mean "permitted against anyone". It means "does not touch the target system". Collecting and using information about real people and real organisations is still governed by law, by your contract and by ethics.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Reconnaissance | Gathering information about an in-scope target to understand its attack surface before testing it. |
| Passive reconnaissance | Collection that never sends traffic to the target's own systems; the information comes from third parties or public records. |
| Active reconnaissance | Collection that sends traffic directly to the target's systems (connecting, requesting, probing) and can therefore be seen and logged by the target. |
| Semi-passive (low-touch) | Interaction that looks like ordinary use, such as requesting a public web page once as any visitor would. Still traffic to the target, so still active for scope purposes. |
| Attack surface | Everything an attacker could interact with: hosts, names, services, endpoints, people-facing channels and the data they expose. |
| Lead | A piece of information that suggests an asset or weakness but has not yet been validated. |
| OSINT | Open-source intelligence: information gathered from publicly available sources. |
| Footprint | The overall picture of an organisation's presence that reconnaissance builds. |

## Why the distinction matters

At first sight the difference between passive and active work looks like a matter of stealth: passive work is quiet, active work is noisy. Stealth is part of it, but for a professional tester there are three more important reasons the line matters.

### Risk to the target

Passive collection cannot break anything on the target because it never reaches the target. Reading a public DNS record from a resolver, searching a certificate log or reading a search engine's index puts load on third parties who exist to answer such questions, not on the client's production systems. Active work is different. Even a gentle request can trigger a bug, lock an account, fill a log partition or set off an incident response. That is why the rules of engagement specify windows, rates and stop conditions for active work, and why the tester's tooling should make out-of-scope action hard.

### Visibility and evidence

Active traffic leaves records in the target's logs: firewall entries, web server access logs, intrusion detection alerts. This is useful in an authorised test because it lets the client's defenders check whether they noticed you. It also means every active action must be traceable to an authorised source address and a time inside the agreed window, so that the client can distinguish your testing from a real attack. Passive collection leaves no trace on the target, which is precisely why defenders must assume adversaries are doing it all the time.

### Scope and legal footing

This is the decisive reason. Active work against a system is lawful only when someone with authority over that system has authorised it in writing (Module 1, lessons 2 to 5). Under India's Information Technology Act 2000, accessing a computer resource without the owner's permission can attract civil liability under section 43 and criminal liability under section 66. Sending probes to a host that is not in scope is not a "reconnaissance mistake"; it is unauthorised activity. Passive collection about an in-scope organisation, by contrast, generally relies on information the organisation or a third party has chosen to publish. It still has limits: terms of service of the sources, personal-data law (the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 governs processing of digital personal data), and the contract you signed. But it does not involve accessing the target's computer resources.

> [!WARNING]
> A tester who "just checks" an interesting related host with one request has crossed from passive into active work against an asset nobody authorised. The size of the action does not change its nature. When in doubt, stop and ask the client (Module 1, lesson 4).

## The interaction spectrum

It is more accurate to think of reconnaissance as a spectrum of interaction than as two boxes. At one end is information you already hold or can read from third parties without any query reaching the target. At the other end is systematic probing of the target's services. Between them sit actions that look like normal use of a public service.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram turns the vague words "passive" and "active" into an ordered scale of how much a reconnaissance action touches the target. It matters because the point on the scale, not the tool used, decides which rules apply.

**Components.** Five ticks run from left to right. **Prior knowledge** covers what the client or the engagement already gave you, such as the scope document and asset list. **Third-party records** covers public DNS answers served by resolvers, WHOIS/RDAP registration data, certificate transparency logs and search engine indexes. **Ordinary visit** is a single request to a public page, exactly as any visitor would make. **Targeted requests** are requests a normal visitor would not make, such as asking for `/robots.txt`, `/admin` or a list of candidate paths. **Systematic probing** is scanning ports, brute-forcing names or enumerating services.

**Relationships.** Three bands sit above the ticks. The green **passive** band spans the first two ticks: no packet reaches the target's infrastructure. The orange **low-touch** band covers the ordinary visit: it reaches the target but is indistinguishable from normal use. The red **active** band covers targeted requests and probing, where traffic is shaped by testing intent.

**Execution flow.** A disciplined engagement moves from left to right. You exhaust what prior knowledge and third-party records can tell you, then confirm a few facts with low-touch actions, and only then move into active work, each step inside the authorised window.

**Decision logic.** The key boundary is between the second and third ticks. Anything to the right of it sends traffic to the target, so it requires the target to be listed in scope and the activity to be permitted by the rules of engagement. The low-touch band is not a loophole: it is still traffic to the target and still needs authorisation.

**Technical interpretation.** When you look up `www.example.com` through a public resolver, the resolver may contact the domain's authoritative name servers on your behalf. Those name servers are often operated by a DNS provider rather than by the target itself. This is why DNS lookups are usually classed as passive, while sending an HTTP request to the resulting address is active.

**Practical example.** In the course lab, reading `scope.md` is prior knowledge; reading the saved file `LAB_ASSETS/C05/data/enumerate_output.txt` is analysing existing records; requesting `http://127.0.0.1:8099/` is an ordinary visit; requesting `/robots.txt` and the ten paths in `enumerate.py`'s word list is targeted, active work, which is authorised only because the target is your own loopback service.

**Security implications.** Defenders cannot detect the left half of the scale on their own systems, so they must reduce what they publish and monitor third-party sources such as certificate logs. They can detect the right half, so their logs should record it with enough detail to spot reconnaissance patterns.

**Key learning.** Classify an action by whether its traffic reaches the target. Everything that does is active and needs authorisation, however gentle it looks.

## Passive sources in outline

Later lessons in this module study each source in depth. Here is the map, so that you can see how they relate.

- **DNS.** The public naming system maps names to addresses, names mail servers and holds text records such as SPF and domain-verification tokens (lesson 2).
- **Registration data.** WHOIS and its successor RDAP describe who registered a domain or an address block, through which registrar, and when (lesson 2).
- **Certificate transparency.** Publicly trusted TLS certificates are recorded in append-only public logs defined by RFC 6962, which reveal the host names certificates were issued for (lesson 4).
- **Search engines and archives.** Indexes and historical snapshots of public pages, documents and error messages (lesson 3).
- **Published documents.** Brochures, reports and files on public websites, along with the metadata embedded in them (lesson 6).
- **Code and configuration in public repositories.** Accidentally published source code can expose host names, internal paths and sometimes credentials.
- **Organisational material.** Job advertisements often name the technologies a company runs; press releases name partners and suppliers.

Each of these is a record that somebody else holds. None of them requires you to send a packet to the target.

## Active techniques in outline

Active reconnaissance overlaps with the next module, *Scanning and enumeration*. In this module, active work is limited to light confirmation of what passive sources suggested:

- **Requesting public pages and well-known files** such as `/robots.txt` and `/.well-known/security.txt` (defined by RFC 9309 and RFC 9116 respectively).
- **Reading response headers** returned by those requests, such as `Server`.
- **Direct DNS queries to the target's own name servers**, including zone transfer attempts. A zone transfer (AXFR, RFC 5936) asks a name server for the whole zone; a correctly configured server refuses strangers. Because it is a deliberate request to the target's infrastructure, it belongs in the active column and needs authorisation.
- **Connecting to a service** to see whether it answers.

## Worked example: classifying actions in the lab

The course lab (`LAB_ASSETS/C05/`) contains a deliberately weak web application, the fictional *Nilgiri Parts Portal*, which binds only to `127.0.0.1:8099`. The scope document `LAB_ASSETS/C05/scope.md` states that the single host 127.0.0.1 running `target/app.py` on TCP 8099 is in scope, and nothing else is. Suppose you plan five actions and classify each before acting.

| Planned action | Reaches the target? | Class | Allowed here because |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Read `scope.md` and the kit `README.txt` | No | Prior knowledge | It is your engagement paperwork. |
| Read the saved `data/enumerate_output.txt` | No | Passive analysis of existing records | Reviewing evidence already collected. |
| `curl -s http://127.0.0.1:8099/` | Yes | Low-touch | Target is in scope (section 2 of `scope.md`). |
| `curl -s http://127.0.0.1:8099/robots.txt` | Yes | Active, targeted | Reconnaissance of the in-scope service is a permitted activity (section 4). |
| `python enumerate.py --host 192.0.2.10` | Would, but is refused | Out of scope | 192.0.2.10 is not listed; the tool refuses it. |

The last row is worth running, because it shows a good engineering habit. The lab's enumeration script encodes the scope in code, so a typing mistake or a stray lead cannot turn into out-of-scope traffic. This command was run against the lab kit on CPython 3.14.4:

```terminal
$ python enumerate.py --host 192.0.2.10
REFUSED: 192.0.2.10 is out of scope. This lab tool runs only against the loopback target you own (127.0.0.1).
$ echo $?
2
```

`--host` names the target to enumerate (the default is `127.0.0.1`). The script compares it against an allow-list containing only `127.0.0.1` and `localhost`, writes the refusal to standard error and exits with status 2 before opening any socket. The address 192.0.2.10 is from the documentation range 192.0.2.0/24 (RFC 5737), so even if the check were missing, no real system would be reached. Notice the order of operations in the script: the scope check runs before any network code, not after the first connection.

The first permitted low-touch request returns what any visitor would see:

```terminal
$ curl -s http://127.0.0.1:8099/
{"service": "Nilgiri Parts Portal", "banner": "AUTHORISED LAB TARGET - Nilgiri Parts Portal. Fictional and deliberately vulnerable. For use only on your own machine.", "endpoints": ["/login", "/invoice?id=", "/robots.txt"]}
```

`-s` (silent) suppresses curl's progress meter so only the response body is printed. Even this single ordinary visit yields three leads: a login function, an invoice endpoint that takes an `id` parameter, and a `robots.txt` file. That is the typical yield of good reconnaissance: not a vulnerability yet, but a short list of places worth testing.

## Planning the passive-to-active transition

Before moving from the passive band into active work, a professional pauses and checks four conditions. The figure shows them as a decision path that every candidate action must pass.

{{figure C05-M02-L01-F2}}

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** This graph is the gate every reconnaissance action passes through before it is executed. It converts the rules of engagement from a document into a repeatable decision, so that the tester never has to improvise under time pressure.

**Components.** The start node, **Planned recon action**, is any step you are about to take. Three violet decision nodes follow: **Sends traffic to the target?**, **Asset listed in scope?** and **Activity, time and rate permitted?**. Two green outcomes, **Proceed as passive** and **Proceed and log**, are the allowed endings. One red outcome, **Stop: park lead, ask client**, is the safe ending for everything else.

**Relationships and arrows.** Arrows point in the direction of the decision. The **no** branch from the first decision goes straight to *Proceed as passive*: if no traffic reaches the target, scope checks on the target's systems do not apply, although legal and ethical limits on the source still do. The **yes** branch leads to the scope question. A **no** at either the scope or permission question leads to the red stop node; only two **yes** answers lead to *Proceed and log*.

**Execution flow.** The questions are asked in order, cheapest first. Most passive actions leave the graph at the first question. Active actions must clear the scope check (is this exact host, address or name in the signed scope?) and then the rules-of-engagement check (is this kind of activity allowed, inside the window and at an agreed rate?).

**Decision logic.** There is deliberately no "probably in scope" branch. Ambiguity resolves to *no*, which sends the action to the stop node. The stop node does not delete the lead; it parks it in the documentation (lesson 7) and raises it with the client, who can extend the scope in writing.

**Technical interpretation.** In software terms the gate is an allow-list check executed before any network call. The lab's `enumerate.py` implements exactly the scope node: `in_scope()` returns true only for `127.0.0.1` and `localhost`, and `main()` returns exit status 2 if the check fails.

**Practical example.** A search result mentions `portal-old.nilgiriparts.example`. Looking at the search result is passive and proceeds. Resolving the name through a public resolver is passive and proceeds. Sending an HTTP request to it sends traffic to the target, and the name is not in `scope.md`, so the action stops; the lead is recorded with its source and raised with the client.

**Security implications.** The gate protects third parties, the client and the tester. It prevents collateral testing of shared hosting neighbours or cloud addresses reassigned to another customer, and it gives the tester a documented justification for every action.

**Key learning.** Two questions decide whether active reconnaissance may happen: is the asset in scope, and is the activity permitted now? Anything else resolves to stop and ask.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"Passive means legal everywhere."** Passive collection avoids touching the target's systems, but it does not override the law or the sources' terms. Downloading a breached credential dump, logging in to a private group, or scraping and profiling employees' personal social media may each be unlawful or unethical even though nothing touches the target. Personal data you encounter is governed by the DPDP Act 2023 and your contract.

**"One request does not count."** A single request is still access to the target's computer resource. Scope does not have a size threshold.

**"DNS lookups are always passive."** Queries through a public resolver usually are. A query sent directly to the target's own authoritative server, especially a zone transfer request, is a request to the target's infrastructure and should be treated as active.

**"Passive results are facts."** Passive data is often out of date: a DNS name might point to an address now used by another organisation, a cached page may describe a system decommissioned years ago. Every passive result is a lead until validated (lesson 7).

**"Active means scanning."** Active reconnaissance includes reading `robots.txt`, fetching an error page or checking a header. Scanning is only its noisiest form.

## Security implications, detection and defence

From the defender's side, the two modes call for different responses.

**Against passive reconnaissance**, detection on your own systems is impossible by definition, so defence is about **reducing and watching what you publish**. Review DNS for records that reveal internal naming or forgotten hosts. Monitor certificate transparency logs for certificates issued for your domains. Strip metadata from published documents. Search for your own organisation the way an attacker would, on a schedule, and fix what you find. MITRE ATT&CK lists *Pre-compromise* (M1056) as the mitigation for reconnaissance techniques, acknowledging that the main lever is limiting exposed information.

**Against active reconnaissance**, detection is possible. Web server logs show bursts of 404 responses for paths such as `/admin`, `/backup` and `/.git`; firewall logs show connection attempts across many ports; DNS server logs show refused zone transfers. A security operations team should alert on such patterns and, during an authorised test, compare what they saw with the tester's activity log. That comparison is often one of the most valuable outputs of a test.

## Best practices

- **Passive first, always.** It is lower risk, and it often answers questions that would otherwise need active probing.
- **Classify before acting.** Write the class (passive, low-touch, active) next to each planned action in your notes.
- **Encode scope in tooling.** Prefer tools and wrappers that refuse out-of-scope targets, as `enumerate.py` does.
- **Use documentation ranges in examples and tests.** Addresses from 192.0.2.0/24, 198.51.100.0/24 and 203.0.113.0/24, and names under `example.com` or `.example`, cannot hit a real system by accident.
- **Record the source of every lead.** A lead without a source cannot be validated or defended.
- **Respect people.** Collect information about roles and systems, not about individuals' private lives.

## Key takeaways

- Reconnaissance discovers what exists; poor reconnaissance either misses assets or acts on bad leads.
- Passive collection never sends traffic to the target; active collection does, however lightly.
- Active work needs the asset in scope and the activity permitted; ambiguity means stop and ask.
- Passive does not mean unrestricted: law, source terms, contracts and ethics still apply, especially to personal data.
- Defenders cannot see passive reconnaissance, so they must reduce and monitor what they publish; they can see active reconnaissance, so they should log and alert on it.
