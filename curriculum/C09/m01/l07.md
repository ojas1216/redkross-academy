## Introduction and conceptual foundation

The earlier lessons in this module dealt with what evidence is, how quickly it decays, how it is handled under Indian law, how custody is recorded and how an examination is documented. All of those are techniques. This lesson is about the person using them. A forensic examiner is given unusual access: an image of a laptop holds a person's mail, photographs, browsing, health searches, bank statements and private conversations, most of which has nothing to do with the question being asked. The examiner's findings may end someone's employment, support a prosecution, clear a suspect or decide an insurance claim. Very few people check the examiner's work as it is done, and many of the readers who rely on it cannot check it at all.

Ethics, in this setting, is the set of commitments that keeps that power tied to its purpose. It is not a separate topic from good technique. An examiner who exceeds scope, keeps quiet about a mistake, describes a tool as more trustworthy than the evidence shows, or writes a stronger conclusion than the artefacts support has produced bad forensic work, whatever the quality of their parsing. Most ethical failures in digital forensics are not dramatic. They come from small accommodations made under time pressure: a phrase softened for a client, a scope question left unasked, an awkward artefact left out because it "complicates the story".

The published guidance this course relies on links integrity and method in the same way. NIST SP 800-86, *Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response* (2006), organises forensic work into collection, examination, analysis and reporting, and advises organisations to have forensic policies in place, developed with their legal advisers, rather than improvise. It is practical guidance, not legal advice. The SWGDE *Best Practices for Computer Forensic Acquisition* treats acquisition as documented, verified work that another examiner can follow. The NIST Computer Forensics Tool Testing (CFTT) programme exists because claims about forensic tools need testing against defined specifications. All three assume an examiner who records honestly, stays within the task and does not overstate. This lesson makes that assumption explicit.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This lesson describes professional conduct. It is not legal advice and it does not guarantee that any action will be lawful or admissible in a particular case. Lesson 4 covers legal handling in India. When law, contract and conscience point in different directions, stop and get a written decision from the case lead and legal counsel.

## Key terminology

| Term | Meaning in this course |
| --- | --- |
| Authorisation | The written instruction that permits an examination: who asked, on what basis, which items, which data types and which time range. |
| Scope | The boundary set by the authorisation. Anything outside it is not examined without a new written decision. |
| Incidental finding | Material met during authorised work that is outside the question being asked, for example personal medical records in a case about a leaked price list. |
| Objectivity | Reaching conclusions from the evidence, testing alternatives, and reporting what does not fit as well as what does. |
| Exculpatory material | Evidence that weakens the case against a person or supports an innocent explanation. |
| Conflict of interest | A personal, financial or professional tie that could influence, or reasonably appear to influence, the examiner's judgement. |
| Competence | Doing only the work you are trained and equipped to do, and saying so when a task exceeds it. |
| Overclaiming | Stating a conclusion, a level of certainty or a tool's reliability more strongly than the evidence supports. |
| Data minimisation | Viewing, copying and retaining no more personal data than the authorised question requires. |

## Three layers that govern conduct

Before looking at individual duties, separate three things that are often blurred together.

**Law** sets the outer limit. In India the Information Technology Act 2000 and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 are directly relevant to examiners who handle other people's systems and personal data, and Lesson 4 explains how evidence is handled for legal proceedings. Law tells you what you must not do and some of what you must do. It rarely tells you how to word a finding.

**Policy and engagement terms** sit inside the law. An employer's investigation policy, a retainer letter or a court order says which items you may examine and for what purpose. These documents are the practical source of your authorisation. If the policy is silent on a situation, that silence is not permission.

**Professional ethics** sit inside both. They cover the judgements no document can fully anticipate: whether a sentence in your report is fair, whether you should disclose that you know a party, whether an artefact that cuts against the client's theory belongs in the findings. The ethical examiner does not treat "nobody told me not to" as a standard.

A client cannot authorise you to break the law, and an examiner cannot use personal ethics to widen their own authority, for example by "helping" with an investigation nobody requested. When the layers conflict, pause and escalate in writing rather than resolving the conflict privately.

## The core duties

### Stay within authorisation and scope

Every examination starts from a written authorisation naming the requester, the legal or policy basis, the evidence items (by receipt or exhibit number), the data categories, the date range and the question. Read it before you open the image, and again whenever you find yourself somewhere unexpected.

Scope is not only about devices. An authorisation to examine "the laptop" for evidence that a pricing spreadsheet was sent to a competitor does not automatically permit you to read the employee's personal chat history from three years earlier, even though it is on the same image. Searching is targeted: keyword lists, date filters and file-type filters should be chosen to answer the authorised question, recorded in your notes, and adjusted only with a documented reason.

When you meet something outside scope, the ethical response begins the same way whether the material is trivial or alarming. Stop viewing it, record where it is and why you stopped, and ask for a written decision. Figure 1 sets out that decision.

### Handle incidental findings with restraint

Incidental findings fall into two broad groups. The first is ordinary private material: health records, family photographs, religious or political content, personal finances. The duty here is restraint. Do not open it further, do not copy it into your working notes, do not mention it to colleagues who do not need to know, and do not let it colour your view of the person. The case notes should record that out-of-scope material was encountered at a stated location and was not examined, which is enough to show a later reviewer what happened.

The second group is material that suggests a risk of serious harm or a serious offence. Here restraint still applies: you do not continue browsing to "see how bad it is", you do not make your own copies, and you do not distribute anything. What changes is urgency. Your organisation's policy should name who is told and how quickly, and legal advice is needed about what happens next. Some kinds of material carry specific legal obligations and specific legal risks for anyone who handles them, which is exactly why the decision must not be made alone.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram is a decision graph for one recurring situation: during authorised work, the examiner meets an item and has to decide whether to examine it. It shows that the safe default for anything outside or uncertain against scope is to stop and ask, and that every path ends with a logged decision.

**Components.** The slate starting box, *Item met during examination*, stands for any file, message or artefact the examiner encounters. The two violet boxes are questions: *Inside written authorisation?* (checked against the case scope, data types and dates) and *Risk of serious harm or crime?*. The green box, *Examine and record*, is the normal path for in-scope material. The orange box, *Stop viewing the item*, is the restraint step: the examiner notes the path and views nothing further. The red box, *Escalate under policy*, involves the case lead and legal advice on the same day. The blue box, *Request a scope decision*, asks for a written answer before work on that item resumes. The dark box, *Decision logged in case notes*, is where every path finishes.

**Relationships and arrows.** Arrows run in the direction of the examiner's decisions. The edge labelled *yes* from the scope question leads to examination. The edge labelled *no or unsure* leads to the stop step; "unsure" is deliberately grouped with "no". From the stop step a single arrow leads to the harm question, whose *yes* edge goes to escalation and *no* edge goes to a scope request. Three arrows converge on the dark log box.

**Execution flow.** Check scope first, before reading the content in detail. If the item is out of scope or uncertain, stop, then judge the urgency, then escalate or request a decision, and log the outcome.

**Decision logic.** Only two questions are asked, and neither question is "is this interesting?" or "might the client want this?". Curiosity and helpfulness are not grounds for widening scope.

**Technical interpretation.** Stopping does not mean losing the item. The evidence image is unchanged and its verified digest still covers the item, so a later authorised examiner can locate it from the path in your notes. Restraint costs nothing in evidential terms.

**Practical example.** Examining the Saffron Ledger laptop image for the pricing-file leak, an examiner meets a folder of scanned hospital reports. The scope covers documents and mail relating to pricing between 1 and 15 October 2026. The answer to the first question is *no*. The examiner records the folder path, views nothing further, judges that there is no indication of harm, and asks the case lead whether the folder is relevant. The written reply ("not relevant, do not examine") goes into the case notes.

**Security implications.** The pattern protects the data subject's privacy, the examiner's credibility under cross-examination and the organisation against claims of misuse. Skipping the log box is the most common failure: the restraint happened, but nobody can show it did.

**Key learning.** Scope comes first, uncertainty counts as out of scope, and every decision about an out-of-scope item is written down.

### Be objective and report what does not fit

Objectivity means letting the evidence choose between explanations rather than choosing the explanation and collecting evidence for it. In practice it has three habits.

First, state the competing hypotheses at the start. In the Saffron Ledger case these might be: the employee sent the pricing file to a competitor; someone else used the employee's account; the file reached the competitor by another route. Each hypothesis predicts different artefacts, and an examination that only looks for the first one cannot rule out the others.

Second, record and report material that weakens the requester's theory. If the same pricing file was also on a shared drive readable by forty staff, that fact belongs in the findings, even if the client is paying for the examination and would prefer a single culprit. Omitting exculpatory material is not neutral. It changes the meaning of everything else in the report.

Third, keep facts, inferences and opinions visibly separate. "File `Q4_pricing.xlsx` is present in the user profile with a creation time of 09 Oct 2026" is an observation. "The file was probably copied from the shared drive" is an inference, and the report should say what it rests on. "The employee acted deliberately" is not a forensic conclusion: artefacts show that an account did something on a device, and linking that to a person's intent needs other evidence.

### Be honest about your own errors

Mistakes happen in every laboratory. The ethical question is never whether a mistake occurred but whether it was recorded and dealt with openly. The saved C09 acquisition transcript, supplied with this module, includes a deliberate one-byte change to a working copy, which shows what such a mistake looks like in the record. Its relevant lines are reproduced below exactly as recorded.

```text
== 3. verify image file
e3a74e55ea5520102b3b9458e4fd1eeec4c9a92958082c68953b754e44a60f21  evidence.dd
size 16777216 bytes
== 4. working copy for analysis; verify
e3a74e55ea5520102b3b9458e4fd1eeec4c9a92958082c68953b754e44a60f21  working.dd
== 5. one-byte change in working copy at offset 100000
16d816db2d0761c01f8a6d0e8d88870f98a1bbdbf5bae5c95d99a26c9bb47f2c  working.dd
evidence.dd working.dd differ: char 100001, line 1
== 6. md5 alongside sha256 (legacy compatibility only)
9330805010f0e235779d131b854f2295  evidence.dd
```

Read it as an examiner would. In step 4 the working copy has the same SHA-256 digest as the verified image. In step 5 a single byte at offset 100000 was changed, and the working copy's digest becomes completely different. The comparison line reports the first difference at "char 100001", because that comparison counts bytes from 1 while the offset in the heading counts from 0, so both describe the same byte. The change was made to `working.dd`; the SHA-256 value recorded for `evidence.dd` in step 3 is not the digest that changed.

Now imagine the change was an accident, such as a tool writing to a file it should only have read. Record the discovery with the time and both digests. Stop using the altered copy. Make a new working copy from the verified image and confirm that it matches `e3a74e55…0f21` before continuing. Check whether any finding drawn from the altered copy depends on the affected region, and say in the report what happened and what you did. Do not quietly make a new copy, delete the old notes and present the work as if nothing occurred. The original image was protected, so the honest account costs little; concealment, once discovered, casts doubt on every other statement you make.

Step 6 makes a related point. The transcript labels the MD5 value "legacy compatibility only". Reporting it as the primary integrity check would imply more assurance than the course's own record offers. Report the SHA-256 value you relied on and label the MD5 for what it is.

### Do not overclaim tools or certainty

Reports are often weakened by phrases that sound authoritative but cannot be supported: "a perfect copy", "forensically sound tool", "court-approved software", "proves that the user deleted the file". Each replaces a specific, checkable statement with a general one.

Tool claims need particular care. The NIST CFTT programme develops specifications and test methods for categories of forensic tools and publishes results for particular tool versions against particular test cases. A CFTT report is evidence about how that version behaved in those tests. It does not describe a later version, a different configuration or your own lab, and it is not a general approval for use in court. An ethical report therefore names the tool and version, states what checks you ran yourself (for example that an image's digest matched the source value), and, if you cite a published test report, says exactly which version and test cases it covers. This course does not claim validation for any tool, and neither should your report unless you can point to the evidence.

**Execution flow.** Use the diagram as a review pass. After drafting findings, search the draft for the red patterns: absolute words ("perfect", "proves", "clearly"), unnamed authority ("approved", "validated"), statements about people where the artefact describes an account, and any integrity claim not tied to a recorded value.

**Decision logic.** For each sentence, ask: could another examiner check this from my notes and the evidence? If not, rewrite it in the green style or remove it.

**Technical interpretation.** The green statements are narrower but stronger, because each is tied to a recorded value, a named version or a stated limitation. Cross-examination tends to dismantle broad claims and leave specific ones standing.

**Practical example.** A Saffron Ledger draft says, "The tool used is forensically validated and the image is a perfect copy." The reviewer replaces it with: "Imaging tool and version are recorded in the acquisition log. The SHA-256 digest of `evidence.dd`, `e3a74e55…0f21`, matched the digest recorded for the source before acquisition." Nothing true has been lost.

**Security implications.** Overclaiming harms the people named in a case, and it exposes the examiner and organisation when the claim fails. A single disproved absolute statement invites doubt about the rest of the report.

**Key learning.** Replace authority with evidence: a recorded value, a named version or an explicit limitation.

### Competence and honest limits

A confident report from an examiner working beyond their skill misleads as surely as a dishonest one. If a case needs mobile extraction, memory analysis or a file system you have not studied, say so and arrange for someone qualified, or state the limitation in writing. Competence also covers equipment: test tools on known data in your own lab before relying on them, and record versions so the work can be repeated.

### Independence and conflicts of interest

Disclose any tie to the people or organisations in a case before starting: a relative, a former employer, a financial interest, a dispute. The case lead then decides whether you continue. A requester may ask you to explain a finding more clearly or test another hypothesis. A requester may not tell you what the finding is, whatever the fee, deadline or seniority involved.

### Confidentiality and data minimisation

Everything you see is confidential to the case. Discuss it only with people who need to know, store working material only in the case's controlled storage, and export only what the authorised question requires. When the case closes, retain or dispose of material as the policy and any legal hold require, and record what you did. Where personal data is involved, the DPDP Act 2023 is part of the legal background, and minimisation is also simply good practice: material you never extracted cannot leak from your working files.

## Common pressure scenarios

| Situation | Ethical response |
| --- | --- |
| A manager asks you to remove the paragraph describing a working-copy alteration. | Decline. Offer to make the paragraph clearer and to show that the verified image was unaffected. Escalate if pressed. |
| The client asks for "a clear conclusion that the employee did it". | Report what the artefacts establish about accounts, devices and times, and state what would be needed to attribute action to a person. |
| You recognise the subject as a neighbour. | Disclose before starting and let the case lead decide whether you continue. |
| A colleague asks to see "the funny photos" on an exhibit. | Refuse. Viewing outside the case purpose is a breach of confidentiality whatever the content. |
| Counsel asks you to describe your tool as "court-validated". | Explain that you can state the tool, version and the checks you performed, and cite a specific test report only for what it covers. |
| You find that an artefact you relied on was misread. | Correct the record, notify the case lead in writing and issue a revised report that shows the change. |

## Worked example: checking a fictional receipt against the record

The following exercise uses only files you can read offline. No live system is touched. The receipt below is fictional, prepared for this lesson. The digest it should carry is the `evidence.dd` value from the saved transcript.

```text
SAFFRON LEDGER PVT LTD - EVIDENCE RECEIPT (fictional training record)
Receipt no.      : SL-RCPT-2026-014
Case ref.        : SL-INV-2026-031
Authorisation    : SL-AUTH-2026-031, signed by Head of Legal, 16 Oct 2026
Scope            : documents and mail relating to Q4 pricing, 01-15 Oct 2026
Item             : SL-EX-01, lab image file evidence.dd, 16777216 bytes
SHA-256 recorded : e3a74e55ea5520102b3b9458e4fd1eeec4c9a92958082c68953b754e44a60f12
Received by      : Examiner A. Rao, 17 Oct 2026 11:40 IST
```

**Step 1: compare the size.** The receipt says 16777216 bytes; the transcript records `size 16777216 bytes`. They agree.

**Step 2: compare the digest character by character.** The receipt's value ends `…0f12`. The transcript records `…0f21`. The final two characters are transposed. Everything else matches.

**Step 3: decide what the discrepancy means.** A transposition in a hand-copied digest most likely means a transcription error, not a changed image. But "most likely" is an inference. What settles it is a fresh verification of the image against the digest recorded at acquisition, performed and noted by the examiner.

**Step 4: correct openly.** Leave the original receipt intact. Add a dated, signed annotation giving the receipt value, the transcript value, the re-verification result and who made the note. Do not overwrite the receipt or issue a "clean" replacement. A later reviewer should see both values and why one is correct.

**Step 5: check the authorisation fields.** The receipt names the authorisation, the signatory and a scope with dates. Before examining SL-EX-01, an examiner should confirm that the authorisation document itself exists in the case file and says what the receipt says it says. A receipt that summarises an authorisation is not the authorisation.

> [!TIP]
> When comparing digests, compare the whole value, not the first and last few characters. Abbreviations such as `e3a74e55…0f21` are convenient in prose, but the check itself must cover every character.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"Ethics is about not breaking the law."** Law sets a floor. A slanted summary or an omitted artefact can be lawful and still wrong.
- **"If it is on the image, I may look."** Holding an image is not authorisation to examine everything on it.
- **"Reporting my mistake will ruin the case."** A recorded, corrected mistake on a working copy, with the verified image intact, is routine. A concealed one that surfaces later damages everything.
- **"The client is paying, so the client decides the conclusion."** The client sets the question and scope. The evidence decides the answer.
- **"Unsure means carry on carefully."** In this course, unsure means stop and ask.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Ethical controls are also security controls. An examiner who browses out-of-scope personal data is, to the data subject, an insider misusing access. Organisations reduce that risk as they reduce other insider risk: least privilege and separate folders on case storage, access logging on evidence repositories, and a route for raising concerns without retaliation. Access logs show who opened which exhibits and when, so a supervisor can detect browsing outside an assignment. Peer review is the main detective control for overclaiming and omissions, provided the reviewer checks the report against the notes and evidence rather than only for style.

## Best practices

1. Read the authorisation before opening evidence and record its reference.
2. Write down competing hypotheses and what would distinguish them.
3. Treat scope uncertainty as out of scope: stop, note the location, ask in writing.
4. Label observations, inferences and opinions separately.
5. Record, correct and report every error, working from the verified source.
6. Name tools and versions, state your own checks, and cite test reports only for what they cover.
7. Disclose conflicts, export only what the question needs and have a peer review findings against the notes.

## Practical applications: case exercises

Work through each case using only the information given. Write your answer as you would in case notes: what you do, who you tell, and what you record.

**Case 1, the shared drive.** In the Saffron Ledger case you find that `Q4_pricing.xlsx` was stored on a departmental share readable by about forty staff for the whole fortnight in question. The requester's theory names one employee. Decide where this belongs in the report and how you will phrase it so that it is neither buried nor overstated.

**Case 2, the softened paragraph.** Your supervisor reads your draft and asks you to delete the sentence recording that a working copy was altered at offset 100000 and replaced. Using the transcript lines above, draft a reply that explains why the sentence stays and how you would show that the verified image was not affected.

**Case 3, the receipt.** A second fictional receipt, SL-RCPT-2026-015, records an MD5 value for SL-EX-01 and no SHA-256 value. Explain what you would add to the record and how you would describe the MD5 value in your report.

**Case 4, the familiar name.** On opening the case file you see that the second custodian is a former flatmate. Write the disclosure note you would send before doing any work.

**Case 5, the request for certainty.** Counsel asks you to add "the tool is forensically validated and the findings are conclusive". Rewrite the sentence so that every part is supported by your record.

## Key takeaways

- Forensic access is privileged access; ethics keeps it tied to the authorised question.
- Law, policy and professional ethics are three layers. When they conflict, escalate in writing.
- Check scope before content, treat uncertainty as out of scope and log every decision.
- Report material that weakens the requester's theory, and separate facts, inferences and opinions.
- Record and correct your own errors openly; a verified original makes honesty cheap.
- Replace authority with evidence. Do not claim validation you cannot show.
