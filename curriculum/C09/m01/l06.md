## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Forensic work is judged long after it is done. A disciplinary hearing may sit months after an examination; a civil dispute may take years. By then the examiner has worked on dozens of other cases and cannot remember which folder they opened first or why they skipped a particular file. What survives is the documentation. If the notes are clear, complete and honest, the work can be explained, checked and, if necessary, repeated. If they are thin, the work is only as strong as the examiner's memory under questioning.

Practitioners often repeat the maxim "if it isn't written down, it didn't happen." It overstates the point slightly, since unrecorded work did happen, but it captures what matters in practice: work that cannot be shown to have happened, in a known order, on a known item, with known tools, is very hard for anyone else to rely on.

This lesson is about **ordinary case documentation**: the everyday records an examiner keeps while working, as opposed to the final report written at the end. The report, covered later in the course (Module 6 reporting and the Module 12 capstone), is a polished account for a reader. The documentation is the working record from which the report is built and against which it can be checked. A good report rests on good notes; a report that cannot be traced back to notes has nothing underneath it.

Documentation serves four purposes.

1. **Reproducibility.** Another competent examiner, given the same evidence and the notes, should be able to repeat each step and reach the same observations. This is the scientific core of forensic work.
2. **Accountability.** The notes show who did what and when, and that the work stayed within its authorised scope.
3. **Review.** A second examiner or supervisor can check the reasoning, spot gaps and challenge conclusions before they reach a decision-maker.
4. **Memory.** The examiner can pick up a paused case, write the report, and answer questions accurately months later.

Lesson 5 covered the custody record, which tracks the *item*. This lesson covers the records that track the *work*: what was done to the item's data, what was seen, what was decided and why. The two are kept together in the **case file**.

We continue with fictional internal case **KF-2026-031** at Kestrel Freight Pvt Ltd (fictional). The laptop `KF-031-E01` and USB drive `KF-031-E02` have been collected and imaged. Examiner S. Menon now begins work on the working copy of the USB image, `KF-031-E02-D1`. All names, file names, times and values are invented.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Documentation records facts and reasoning. It does not decide guilt, responsibility or legal consequences. An examiner who writes "the employee leaked the file" in a note has moved from observation into a conclusion that belongs to the decision-maker, and that sentence will be quoted back to them.

## Key terminology

| Term | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Case file | The complete, organised collection of records for one matter: authorisation, custody, notes, logs, communications, drafts and reviews. |
| Contemporaneous notes | Notes written at the time of the activity, or as soon after as practical, by the person doing it. |
| Examination log | A structured record of each analytical step: date and time, item, tool and version, action, result, output location. |
| Decision log | A record of significant choices, including what was decided, by whom, why, and what alternatives were rejected. |
| Observation | Something directly seen in the evidence ("a file named `pricing_Q1.xlsx` is present in folder `Exports`"). |
| Inference | A reasoned interpretation of observations ("the file was probably copied to the drive on 9 March, based on its timestamps"). |
| Opinion | A professional judgement on what the evidence supports, given only where the examiner is qualified to give it, and labelled as such. |
| Working hypothesis | A provisional explanation being tested, recorded with the evidence for and against it. |
| Peer review | Independent checking of the notes, logs and findings by another qualified person, itself recorded. |
| Version control | Keeping every draft and change with dates and authors, so the history of a document can be shown. |

## The structure of a case file

A case file is more than a folder of notes. It is organised so that a reviewer can find any record quickly and see how the records relate. Organisations differ in the exact sections, but the parts below appear in almost every mature practice, whether the file is on paper, on a case-management system or in a controlled share.

**Authorisation and scope.** The written instruction that started the work, the questions the examiner was asked to answer, any limits (date ranges, accounts, file types), and every later change to scope with its approval. This is the first thing a reviewer reads, because everything else is judged against it.

**Evidence register.** A list of every item and derived item with its number, description, parent item and current location. In the Kestrel case it lists `E01`, `E02`, `E01-D1`, `E02-D1` and any exports made later.

**Custody records.** The receipts and logs from Lesson 5, one set per item.

**Contemporaneous notes.** The examiner's running, dated, first-person account of the work.

**Examination log.** The structured version of the analytical work: one row per significant action, with the tool and version used, the input, the output and the result. Some teams combine this with the notes; many keep it separate because it is easier to review as a table.

**Decision log.** Significant choices and their reasons: why one item was prioritised over another, why a search was limited, why a line of enquiry was dropped.

**Communications record.** Who was told what, when and by whom: briefings to the case lead, requests for information, scope changes agreed by phone and then confirmed in writing.

**Drafts and review records.** Successive drafts of findings and the report, with version numbers, and the peer reviewer's comments and the examiner's responses.

**Key learning.** A case file is a structured set of eight record types under one case number; a missing type is a gap a reviewer will notice.

## Contemporaneous notes

Contemporaneous notes are the heart of ordinary documentation. They are written by the person doing the work, at the time, in their own words. They are not polished. They are trustworthy precisely because they were written before anyone knew which details would matter.

### What makes a good note

**Timestamped.** Every entry begins with the time, and the date and time zone appear at the start of each session. Times let the notes be correlated with tool logs, custody entries and system activity.

**Specific.** Item numbers, file paths, tool names and versions, settings, counts. "Searched the image" is not useful; "keyword search across `E02-D1` working copy for the terms in authorisation annex A, approved search tool (version in tool register), 3 hits, exported list as `E02-D1-S1.csv`" is.

**Factual and first-person.** "I opened", "I observed". The notes record what the examiner did and saw, not what someone else told them unless that is recorded as such ("M. Kulkarni told me by phone at 15:02 that...").

**Observation separated from inference.** The single most important habit. Observations go down as plain facts. Inferences are marked ("Inference:" or "Interpretation:") and state what they rest on. This lets a reader see exactly where the evidence stops and reasoning begins.

**Complete, including failures.** If a step failed, a tool crashed, a file would not open or an export was saved to the wrong place, that goes in the notes. Omitting mistakes makes the notes less credible, not more.

**Never erased.** Paper notes are kept in a bound, page-numbered notebook in ink; errors are struck through once and stay readable. Electronic notes are kept in a system or format that preserves history, such as an append-only log, a case-management system with an audit trail, or a document under version control.

**Attributable.** Each session is signed or initialled, and the notes identify the examiner, the case and the item at the top of each page or entry.

### Paper or electronic?

Both are acceptable when the properties above are preserved. Paper notebooks are simple, hard to edit invisibly, and familiar to reviewers. Electronic notes are searchable, easier to share with a reviewer, and can embed screenshots and hash values without transcription errors. The weakness of electronic notes is that ordinary documents can be edited silently, so they need a system that records history. A plain word-processor file saved over itself every evening has no history and is the weakest option.

### Screenshots and exports

Screenshots and exported files are part of the documentation, and each needs to be traceable. A practical scheme names each one with the case, item and a sequence number (`KF-031-E02-D1-SS004.png`), records it in the notes at the moment it is taken ("14:33 screenshot SS004 of file properties"), and stores it in the case file with a recorded hash. Exports that might be relied on later are treated as derived items and entered in the evidence register.

## Observation, inference and opinion

A reviewer reading Kestrel's notes should never have to guess which statements are facts and which are interpretations. Consider three statements about the same file.

| Statement | Type | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A file named `pricing_Q1.xlsx` is present in folder `Exports` on `E02-D1`. | Observation | Directly seen in the evidence and repeatable by anyone. |
| The file's recorded timestamps are consistent with it being placed on the drive on 9 March. | Inference | Depends on how the file system and copying tool set timestamps, which later modules cover; the basis must be stated. |
| The employee sent the file to a personal account. | Not a note at all | It joins several facts to a conclusion about a person. If supported, it belongs to the decision-maker, informed by the report. |

Inference is not forbidden; it is the purpose of analysis. The discipline is to label it, state what it rests on, and record what would contradict it. A useful pattern for each inference is: *what I observed, what I infer, what would change my view*. For the timestamp example: "Observed: timestamps listed in SS004. Inferred: file placed on drive on or about 9 March. Would change view: evidence of clock error on the source system, or a copying method known to preserve original timestamps."

Opinion is narrower still. An examiner may be asked whether the evidence is consistent with a particular explanation. That is an opinion, given within the examiner's competence, labelled as such, and usually expressed as a degree of support rather than a certainty. It belongs in the report after review, not scattered through working notes.

## The examination log and the decision log

The examination log is the table version of the work. Each row records the date and time, the item worked on (with its number), the tool and version, the action, the result and where the output was saved. It allows a reviewer to see the whole examination at a glance and to repeat any step. The tool version matters because tools change: a parser update can extract more or fewer records, and a reviewer repeating the step with a different version needs to know that.

The decision log records the reasoning that a table of actions does not show. Typical entries include: prioritising the USB drive over the laptop because the complaint concerned a file on removable media; limiting a keyword search to the terms approved in the authorisation; deciding not to examine a personal folder that fell outside scope; asking the case lead to approve extending the date range. Each entry gives the date, the decision, who made or approved it, the reason and the alternatives considered.

Decisions not to do something are as important as decisions to act. A reviewer who sees that browser history was not examined will ask why. "Out of scope per authorisation amendment of 15 March" is a complete answer. Silence invites the suggestion that relevant evidence was ignored.

**Components.** There are two columns separated by "vs". The left column, titled "Weak note" in the red tone, holds five short entries. The right column, titled "Strong note" in the green tone, holds five corresponding entries. A note under the diagram states the takeaway.

**Relationships.** Each item in the left column corresponds to the item in the same position on the right. Reading across a row shows one property being fixed. "Looked at the USB image" becomes a timestamped hash verification of the working copy. "Opened it, all fine" becomes a timed record of opening it with the approved viewer, version logged. "Found the spreadsheet, he copied it" becomes an observation of the file and its path with an explicit statement that no inference about who copied it is made. "Some time in the afternoon" becomes a timed, hashed screenshot entry, and exact times appear on every right-hand entry. "Messed up export, redid it" becomes a record that the first export was saved to the wrong folder, kept rather than deleted, and both versions logged.

**Execution flow.** The right column runs in time order, from 14:12 to 14:40 IST, which is itself a property the left column lacks: without times, the order of the weak entries cannot be confirmed.

**Decision logic.** The diagram has no branches. Its implicit test for each entry is whether a reviewer could repeat the step and check the result from the note alone. Every left-hand entry fails that test; every right-hand entry passes.

**Technical interpretation.** The strong column records five things the weak column omits: time, item number, tool identity and version, the verification result, and the handling of a mistake. It also removes a conclusion about a person, which is the most damaging line in the weak column.

**Practical example.** At a later hearing, S. Menon is asked whether the copy examined was verified. With the weak note, the answer depends on memory. With the strong note, S. Menon reads the 14:12 entry and points to the matching hash in the evidence register for `KF-031-E02-D1`.

**Security implications.** Deleting a mistaken export, as the weak note implies, destroys a record and makes the examiner's output folder inconsistent with the notes. Keeping both exports and logging them preserves the integrity of the case file.

**Key learning.** A strong note is timed, specific, repeatable and honest about mistakes, and it records observations without jumping to conclusions about people.

## Worked example: rewriting a session's notes (offline)

This exercise uses only a text editor. The fictional notes below were written by a trainee examiner during a two-hour session on the Kestrel case. They contain at least six problems.

```text
KF case - USB
Looked at the image, hash fine.
Searched for pricing - found it in Exports. Suspect copied it on 9th.
Exported results.
Called Kulkarni, he says the drive is shared by the team.
Laptop browser looked boring so skipped it.
Tool crashed, restarted, carried on.
Done.
```

A reviewer's annotated rewrite might read as follows. Bracketed text marks what the reviewer added from other records or flagged as missing.

```text
Case KF-2026-031  Item KF-031-E02-D1 (working copy)  Examiner: [name]
Date 21 Mar 2026, times IST (UTC+05:30)

[time] Verified working-copy SHA-256 against evidence register value
       for E02-D1. Result: [match / mismatch - not recorded originally].
[time] Keyword search, terms per authorisation annex A, approved search
       tool [name and version not recorded]. Hits: [count not recorded].
[time] Observed: file pricing_Q1.xlsx in folder Exports.
       [Inference removed from note: "suspect copied it on 9th" is not
       an observation. If the timestamps support an inference, record
       them first, then label the inference and its basis.]
[time] Exported hit list as [file name, hash, register entry missing].
[time] Phone call to M. Kulkarni (Operations Manager), who stated the
       drive is used by several team members. [Record as information
       received, not as a finding; confirm in writing.]
[time] Laptop browser history NOT examined. [Reason required. If out
       of scope, cite the authorisation amendment; "looked boring" is
       not a reason and suggests an unrecorded look at the laptop.]
[time] Search tool stopped responding; restarted. [Record whether the
       search was rerun from the start and whether results differed.]
[time] Session ended. Initials: [ ]
```

Work through the problems the reviewer found.

1. **No identifiers or times.** The original names neither the item number nor the date, and gives no times, so nothing can be correlated with the custody log or tool logs.
2. **Unverifiable verification.** "Hash fine" does not say which value was compared with which.
3. **Tool and version missing.** The search cannot be repeated exactly.
4. **An inference about a person, written as fact.** "Suspect copied it" mixes an assumption about identity with an unstated reading of timestamps.
5. **Hearsay not marked.** Kulkarni's statement is useful context, but it is information received by phone, not an observation of the evidence.
6. **An unexplained omission that may hide an unrecorded action.** "Looked boring" implies the laptop was looked at, but there is no record of when, how, or on which item.
7. **A failure without consequences recorded.** After a crash, the reviewer needs to know whether results are complete.

The rewritten version is longer but not complicated. Most of the added detail would have taken seconds to write at the time and is now difficult or impossible to recover.

## Communications, privacy and access

Investigations involve conversations, and conversations change cases. A case lead narrows scope on a call; a manager mentions that a drive is shared; a colleague suggests another source of logs. Each of these belongs in the communications record with the time, the people, the substance and any follow-up, and significant ones are confirmed in writing (an email summarising the call, filed in the case file).

Case files contain personal data about employees, customers and third parties. Organisations in India will consider their obligations under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and their own policies with their legal team; this lesson does not interpret those obligations. From a documentation standpoint, sound practice is the same everywhere: record only the personal data the work needs, keep the file on access-restricted storage, log who views it, and avoid copying extracts into email or chat where they escape the file's controls.

## Review, retention and closure

Before findings leave the examination team, a second qualified person reviews the case file. The reviewer checks that the authorisation covers the work done, that the notes and examination log support each finding, that inferences are labelled and grounded, and that decisions not to examine something are explained. A practical test is to pick two or three findings and trace each back through the notes to a specific observation on a specific item. The review itself is documented: who reviewed, when, what was checked, what comments were made and how each was resolved.

At closure, the file records the outcome of the work (not the outcome of any disciplinary or legal process, which belongs to others), the final disposition of each evidence item from the custody records, and the retention period set by the organisation's policy. The file is then locked against further editing; if anything must be added later, it is added as a dated addendum.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"I'll write it up properly at the end."** Notes reconstructed after the fact lose detail and are less trustworthy. Write as you go, even briefly.

**Recording only successes.** Omitting failed steps and crashes makes the record look cleaner but leaves a reviewer unable to judge completeness.

**Conclusions about people in working notes.** Phrases such as "he leaked it" are opinions about responsibility, not observations of evidence.

**Missing tool versions.** "Used the usual tool" cannot be reproduced once the tool has been updated.

**Unexplained omissions.** Not examining something is often correct, but only if the reason is recorded.

**Editable notes with no history.** An ordinary document overwritten daily cannot show what it said last week.

**Scattering records.** Notes in a personal notebook, screenshots on a desktop and decisions in chat messages are not a case file. Bring everything into the file as it is created.

## Security implications, detection and defence

The case file is both evidence of good practice and a sensitive asset. Threats include unauthorised disclosure (findings about named staff leaking to colleagues), alteration (notes changed after the fact to fit a conclusion) and loss.

- **Access control:** restrict the file to the case team and named reviewers; log access.
- **Integrity:** use append-only or audit-trailed systems, version control for drafts, and recorded hashes for screenshots and exports.
- **Separation:** keep working copies and outputs in case-specific locations, not on shared desktops.
- **Detection:** periodic comparison of the examination log against tool logs and storage access logs reveals unrecorded work; review sign-off reveals unsupported findings.
- **Minimisation:** fewer copies of personal data mean fewer places it can leak.

## Best practices

- Open the case file before any work begins, starting with the authorisation.
- Head every session with date, time zone, case, item and examiner.
- Timestamp every entry and write it at the time.
- Name tools and versions; record inputs, outputs and their locations.
- Separate observation from inference, and label inference with its basis.
- Record failures, mistakes and omissions with reasons.
- Log significant decisions, including what was not done and who approved it.
- Record communications and confirm important ones in writing.
- Name and hash screenshots and exports; register exports that may be relied on.
- Keep notes in a medium that preserves history; never erase.
- Have the file peer-reviewed and record the review.
- Close the file with an addendum rule: later additions are dated and attributed.

## Key takeaways

- Ordinary documentation is the working record of the examination; the report is built from it and checked against it.
- Its purposes are reproducibility, accountability, review and reliable memory.
- A case file brings together authorisation, evidence register, custody, notes, examination log, decision log, communications and review.
- Good contemporaneous notes are timed, specific, first-person, complete including failures, never erased and attributable.
- Observations, inferences and opinions are kept distinct; inferences are labelled with their basis and what would change them.
- Decisions not to examine something must be recorded with reasons and approvals.
- The case file is sensitive: restrict and log access, preserve history and keep personal data to what the work needs.
