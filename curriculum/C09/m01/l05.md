## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every forensic finding eventually reduces to the same claim: *this result came from that item, and that item is the one that was collected*. A timeline, a recovered document or a login record has no value if nobody can show where the data came from and what happened to it between collection and examination. The **chain of custody** is the record that supports that claim. It is a chronological, documented account of every person who held an evidence item, when and where they held it, why they held it, and what they did with it, from the moment it was identified until it is returned, retained or destroyed.

The idea is older than computers. Police property rooms, hospital specimen labs and food-safety inspectors have long used signed transfer forms and sealed containers for the same reason: an item that has passed through many hands is only trustworthy if every hand-over is accounted for. Digital evidence adds two complications. First, the thing that matters is usually the data, not the container, and data can be copied perfectly, so the record has to follow both the physical device and every copy made from it. Second, data can change silently. A drive that is powered on, a mailbox that keeps receiving messages or a cloud folder that syncs can alter its own contents without anyone touching it. The custody record therefore works together with integrity controls such as write blocking and cryptographic hashing, which Module 2 covers in depth.

A chain of custody does two jobs. It establishes **continuity**: there is no period in which the item's location and holder are unknown. It supports **integrity**: anyone who held the item can show that it was not altered, or that any change was deliberate, necessary and recorded. Neither job is about proving the case. A perfect custody record says nothing about what the evidence means; it only removes doubt about whether the item is what it claims to be.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This lesson teaches the practitioner's record-keeping. Whether a particular record is sufficient for a particular court, tribunal or disciplinary panel is a legal question for counsel and the decision-maker. Lesson 4 introduced the Indian legal framework, including the certificate that accompanies electronic records. Nothing here is a legal opinion on admissibility.

Custody records matter outside courtrooms too. An internal investigation into misuse of company data may end in a disciplinary hearing where the employee challenges the evidence. An insurer may ask how a ransomware loss was established. A regulator may ask an organisation to show how it preserved logs. In each setting, the organisation that can produce a clean custody record spends its time discussing findings rather than defending its handling.

Throughout this lesson we follow one fictional case. Kestrel Freight Pvt Ltd (a fictional logistics company in Pune) opened internal case **KF-2026-031** after a manager reported that a shipment-pricing spreadsheet appeared on a personal email account. The investigation team has written authorisation from Kestrel's legal head to collect a company laptop and a company USB drive from the employee's desk. All names, serial numbers, seal numbers and hash values in this case are invented for teaching.

## Key terminology

| Term | Meaning in practice |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence item | A single thing taken into custody: a laptop, a USB drive, a printed log, an exported mailbox file. Each gets its own identifier. |
| Item number (exhibit number) | A unique, permanent identifier such as `KF-031-E01`. It never changes and is never reused, even if the item is returned. |
| Custodian | The person responsible for the item at a given moment. There is always exactly one. |
| Transfer | A hand-over of responsibility from one custodian to another, recorded with both names, signatures, date, time and purpose. |
| Evidence receipt | The document created when an item is first taken into custody, describing the item and signed by the person collecting it and, where possible, the person it was collected from. |
| Custody log | The running list of every transfer and access for an item, usually on the back of the evidence bag label and in a register or case-management system. |
| Tamper-evident seal | A numbered seal or bag that cannot be opened without visible damage. Its number is written in the log every time it is applied or checked. |
| Derived item | Something created from an original item, such as a forensic image, a logical export or a printout. It gets its own number linked to the parent. |
| Working copy | A copy of a derived item that an examiner analyses, so that the master copy stays untouched. |
| Hash value | A fixed-length digest (for example SHA-256) computed over data. Matching values at two times show the data was bit-for-bit identical at both times. |
| Continuity gap | Any interval in which the record cannot say who held the item or where it was. |

International guidance uses closely related vocabulary. ISO/IEC 27037 sets out guidelines for identifying, collecting, acquiring and preserving digital evidence, and distinguishes the first responder who collects evidence from the specialist who examines it. RFC 3227, an older internet guideline on evidence collection, also asks collectors to be able to describe how evidence was found, how it was handled and everything that happened to it. NIST SP 800-86 places these practices inside incident response. Your organisation's own procedure will name the exact forms; the concepts here map onto all of them.

## What a custody record must answer

A useful test for any entry is whether it answers six questions without the reader having to ask the author.

**Who.** The full name and role of everyone who held the item, not initials alone. Initials are acceptable on a bag label only if a signature register elsewhere maps them to names.

**What.** A description precise enough that the item could not be confused with another: make, model, serial number, colour, visible damage, stickers, asset tag, capacity. "Silver laptop" is not enough in an office where forty identical laptops sit on desks.

**When.** Date and time, with the time zone. In India, local time is IST (UTC+05:30). Many teams record UTC throughout because log sources and tools usually report UTC. Whichever you choose, state it once at the top of every form and do not mix them.

**Where.** The location of collection (building, floor, desk), every location the item moved to, and where it is now. Storage locations should be specific: the store, the shelf or locker, not just "evidence room".

**Why.** The purpose of each transfer: lodging in storage, imaging, review by a second examiner, return to the owner. A transfer with no purpose is a red flag for a reviewer.

**How.** The condition of the packaging and seals at each step, and anything done to the item. "Seal KS-4471 intact on receipt, bag opened by cutting the base, resealed in bag with seal KS-4490" is a good entry; "checked" is not.

## The custody lifecycle

Custody is not a single form; it is a sequence of stages, and each stage produces records. The order matters because the earliest records cannot be recreated later. Photographs of a desk taken after the laptop has been moved do not show where it was.

**Identify and document in place.** Before anything is touched, the collector photographs and notes the scene: the item, its position, what is connected to it, whether it is powered on, what is on the screen. Decisions about powered-on systems and volatile data are covered in Lesson 3 (order of volatility); the custody point is that those decisions and their timing are written down.

**Collect and label.** The item receives its number. The label records the number, case, description, serial, collector, date, time and location. Accessories such as chargers are either separate items or explicitly listed as part of the item.

**Package and seal.** The item goes into suitable packaging (anti-static bags for drives, padded boxes for laptops) and a tamper-evident seal is applied. The seal number is written on the receipt and the log. A photograph of the sealed bag showing the seal number is cheap and valuable.

**Issue the evidence receipt.** The receipt is the birth certificate of the item in custody. Where the item was taken from a person, that person (or their manager, or a witness) is asked to sign that the description is accurate. They keep a copy.

**Transfer and log each hand-over.** Every time responsibility changes, the releasing and receiving custodians both sign, with the date, time, purpose and seal condition. A courier counts as a custodian; so does a locked transport case if its key holder is recorded.

**Secure storage.** Between uses the item sits in an access-controlled store. The store keeps its own access register, so that the custody log and the store log can be cross-checked.

**Examination on a verified copy.** The examiner works from a forensic image or export, not the original device, and checks the hash of the copy against the value recorded at acquisition before and after work. Module 2 explains imaging and verification in full; for custody purposes the key is that each hash check is recorded with its result.

**Return, retain or dispose.** At the end of the matter, an authorised person decides what happens to each item. The decision, its authority and its execution (returned to whom, wiped by whom, retained until when) close the record.

"Return, retain or dispose" (dark) is the authorised close of the record. Each box carries a short sub-label naming the record it produces.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each arrow runs from one stage to the next and represents the item, or responsibility for it, moving forward. Every arrow is effectively a hand-over or a change of state, which is why the note under the diagram says each one must appear in the log.

**Execution flow.** The stages run in the order drawn. Storage and examination can repeat: an item may be signed out for imaging, returned, then signed out again for a second examiner. Each repetition adds a transfer entry; the order of the stages does not change.

**Decision logic.** The diagram has no branching boxes. The one decision point is the final stage, where an authorised person chooses between return, retention and disposal. That choice is recorded with the name of the person who authorised it.

**Technical interpretation.** The orange stages are the ones where custody failures most often begin: receipts with vague descriptions and transfers recorded without seal checks. The green examination stage is the only one where the data is analysed, and it uses a copy, so the original remains in the state recorded at collection.

**Practical example.** In case KF-2026-031, the laptop on desk 3B is photographed in place (slate), labelled `KF-031-E01` (blue), sealed in a bag with seal KS-4471 (blue), and receipted with the employee's manager as witness (orange). It is carried to the evidence store and lodged (orange, then violet). Two days later the examiner signs it out, images it and analyses the verified working copy (green). When the matter closes, the legal head authorises return to IT for reissue (dark).

**Security implications.** The arrows are where an insider could substitute or alter an item. Seal numbers checked and written at every arrow, and a store register that can be compared with the custody log, are what make substitution detectable.

**Key learning.** Chain of custody is a chain of records, one per stage and per hand-over; a missing record at any arrow is a continuity gap.

## The evidence receipt

The evidence receipt is the first custody document and the one most often written in a hurry. A practical receipt is short, structured and complete. Below is the fictional receipt for the laptop in case KF-2026-031, as it would be typed up from the paper original. The receipt records that no hash was taken. The collector documented the physical item and its condition without powering it on to read the drive.

```text
EVIDENCE RECEIPT                         Kestrel Freight Pvt Ltd (fictional)
Case:            KF-2026-031  Internal data-handling review
Authority:       Written instruction, Legal Head, ref LH/2026/112, 13 Mar 2026
Item number:     KF-031-E01
Description:     Laptop, Dell Latitude, silver, asset tag KF-IT-0388,
                 serial 7XQ2K93 (label under base), small dent on lid corner
Condition:       Powered off on arrival (screen dark, no LED); lid closed;
                 charger attached and collected as part of this item
Location:        Building B, floor 2, desk 3B, Pune office
Date and time:   14 Mar 2026 10:42 IST (UTC+05:30)
Collected by:    A. Rao, Investigator, Information Security
Witness:         M. Kulkarni, Operations Manager
Packaging:       Padded evidence bag, tamper-evident seal KS-4471
Photographs:     IMG 0141-0149 (desk in place, labels, sealed bag)
Hash:            None taken at collection (device not powered on)
Signatures:      A. Rao ............  M. Kulkarni ............
Copy given to:   M. Kulkarni
```

Each line earns its place. **Authority** ties the collection to the written instruction, which a later reviewer will ask for first. **Description** includes two independent identifiers (asset tag and serial) and a physical mark (the dent), so the laptop could be recognised even if a label fell off. **Condition** records the power state, which shapes what the examiner can and cannot later say about volatile data. **Packaging** and **Photographs** link the paper to the physical bag. **Hash** honestly records that none was taken, which is better than leaving the field blank: a blank field invites the question "did you forget?", whereas "none taken, device not powered on" answers it.

A second item, the USB drive, gets its own receipt as `KF-031-E02`. It is not added as a line on the laptop receipt, because the two items may follow different paths: the drive may be imaged on a different day by a different examiner.

## Physical items, data and derived items

The laptop is a physical object; the evidence that matters is the data on its drive. When the examiner images the drive, the image is a new thing that did not exist at collection. Good practice treats it as a **derived item** with its own number, for example `KF-031-E01-D1`, linked to its parent. Its custody record begins at the moment of creation and states which item it was made from, by whom, with what tool and version, and the hash value computed over the image at that time.

From then on there are two chains. The laptop continues in the evidence store with its physical seal. The image lives on evidence storage, and its "seal" is its hash value combined with the access controls of the storage. A working copy made from the image for analysis is either a further derived item or, under many procedures, simply recorded in the examination notes as "working copy verified against D1 hash, result: match".

Some evidence never has a physical form. An exported mailbox, a cloud audit-log download or a set of screenshots from a web console begins life as data. Its custody record starts with the export: the source system, the account used, the person who ran the export, the time, the export method and the hash of the resulting file computed immediately. Without that first entry, nobody can later show that the file on the evidence share is the one that came out of the system.

> [!NOTE]
> A matching hash shows that two copies of data are identical at the two moments the hash was computed. It does not show that the acquisition captured everything, that the device was unaltered before it was collected, or that the data means what someone says it means. The custody record and the examiner's notes cover those questions.

## Transfers and the custody log

The custody log is where continuity is either proved or lost. Each entry records the releasing custodian, the receiving custodian, date and time, purpose, and seal condition. Both people sign. Most teams keep the log in two places that can be compared: a label or form that stays with the item, and a register in the evidence store or a case-management system.

A reviewer reads the log by following the receiving custodian of each entry into the releasing custodian of the next. If entry 3 says the item went to S. Menon, entry 4 must show S. Menon releasing it. If the seal number changes, there must be an entry explaining when and why the bag was opened and resealed. If time passes between entries, the location during that time must be clear, either because the item was in the store (and the store register agrees) or because the custodian recorded where they kept it.

**Execution flow.** Read the rows top to bottom in time order. Entry 4 returns the laptop to the store but records seal KS-4471, the seal that entry 3 says was cut and replaced. Entry 5 signs the laptop out to S. Menon again. Entry 6, five days later, shows J. Das returning it with a seal, KS-4502, that no earlier entry mentions.

**Decision logic.** For each red row the reviewer asks whether the defect is clerical or substantive. Entry 4 may be a copying error, which inspecting the bag would settle: if KS-4490 is on it and intact, the error is in the writing. Entry 6 is more serious: there is no transfer from S. Menon to J. Das, no record of where the laptop was for five days, and no record of the bag being opened to explain the new seal.

**Technical interpretation.** Neither defect necessarily means the evidence was tampered with. Both mean the record cannot show that it was not. Because the drive was imaged in entry 3, the image's hash still lets the examiner show that the analysed data matches what was acquired, but anything later done with the physical laptop is now open to question.

**Practical example.** The Kestrel reviewer raises both entries in writing, asks S. Menon and J. Das for statements about 18 to 23 March, checks the store register and badge records for those dates, and inspects the bag. The findings go into a dated memorandum attached to the log. Nobody edits entries 4 or 6.

**Security implications.** Logs that are corrected silently are worse than logs with acknowledged errors, because a later discovery of the correction destroys trust in every entry. Paired records (bag label and store register) make silent edits detectable.

**Key learning.** Read a custody log as a chain of links: each receiver must become the next releaser, and each seal change must have its own explained entry.

## Seals, packaging and storage

Packaging protects the item physically and makes interference visible. Tamper-evident bags have a printed, numbered seal strip and usually show "VOID" or tear patterns if peeled. Drives go into anti-static bags first. Laptops go into padded bags or boxes, with the lid closed and, where the procedure requires it, the battery state noted. Mobile phones need packaging decisions about network isolation that belong to the collection plan; the custody record simply notes what was done and when.

Every time a bag is opened, the examiner cuts it away from the existing seal (so the old seal stays intact and can be kept with the new bag), records the old seal number as found, records what was done, and reseals with a new numbered seal. The log entry for that access names both seal numbers.

The evidence store is a locked room, safe or locker with limited key or badge holders. It keeps its own register of every item in and out and every person entering. Digital evidence storage follows the same logic: a dedicated share or object store with named accounts, access logging, and where available write-once or retention-locked settings, so that a derived image cannot be overwritten. The store register and the system access log are the independent records against which a reviewer checks the custody log.

## Handling gaps and errors honestly

Mistakes happen. A transfer is signed without a time; a seal number is miscopied; an item sits on a desk over lunch. What matters is how the mistake is handled.

The rule is simple: **never alter an earlier entry so that it looks as though the mistake did not happen.** On paper, a wrong entry is struck through with a single line so it stays readable, initialled and dated, and the correct information is written alongside or in a new entry. In a system, corrections are new records that reference the original. Where a gap cannot be closed from memory, the custodian writes a dated memorandum explaining what is known, what is not, and what checks were made (store register, badge logs, statements). The memorandum is attached to the log.

An honest, explained gap is a weakness that the decision-maker can weigh. A concealed gap that is later found is a credibility problem that spreads to everything else the examiner has written. That is why many procedures require custody defects to be reported to the case lead the same day they are noticed.

## Worked example: reviewing a custody packet offline

This exercise uses only paper or a text editor. You are the second reviewer on case KF-2026-031 and receive a custody packet for item `KF-031-E02`, the USB drive. The packet contains the receipt, the custody log and the examiner's imaging note.

```text
RECEIPT  KF-031-E02  USB flash drive, black, "KF-IT" sticker
         Collected 14 Mar 2026 10:55 by A. Rao, desk 3B drawer
         Anti-static bag, seal KS-4472. Witness: M. Kulkarni

LOG      1  14 Mar 10:55  site          A. Rao     collected, KS-4472
         2  14 Mar 17:05  A. Rao        P. Iyer    lodged, KS-4472 intact
         3  17 Mar 10:00  P. Iyer       S. Menon   imaging, KS-4472 intact
         4  17 Mar 12:40  S. Menon      P. Iyer    returned, KS-4495

IMAGING  17 Mar 2026 10:20-11:05 IST, S. Menon. Opened bag below KS-4472.
         Write blocker in use. Image KF-031-E02-D1 created.
         SHA-256 of image recorded (illustrative): 3c9a...e1f0
         Resealed in new bag, seal KS-4495. Old seal retained in new bag.
```

Work through the questions a reviewer would ask.

1. **Is the item identified uniquely?** Partly. "Black, KF-IT sticker" may match many drives. There is no capacity, brand or serial. Record this as a weakness and check whether the photographs show more detail.
2. **Do the log entries link?** Yes. A. Rao releases to P. Iyer, P. Iyer to S. Menon, S. Menon back to P. Iyer.
3. **Is the seal change explained?** Yes. The imaging note says the bag was opened below KS-4472, resealed with KS-4495, and the old seal kept. Entry 4's seal number matches.
4. **Is there a derived item with a starting record?** Yes. `KF-031-E02-D1` has a creation time, creator and hash. The reviewer would next check that the image's own custody record (on the evidence share) starts with the same hash.
5. **Are times consistent?** Imaging ran 10:20 to 11:05; the drive was signed out at 10:00 and returned at 12:40. That is consistent, though the 95 minutes after imaging should be accounted for in S. Menon's notes (for example, verification and resealing).

Your written review would list one weakness (description lacks a serial or capacity) and one query (activity between 11:05 and 12:40), and confirm that continuity is otherwise shown. It would not state whether the drive "proves" anything; that is outside the custody review.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"The hash is the chain of custody."** A hash is an integrity check on data. It cannot show who held a device, whether a seal was intact or where an item spent a weekend. The custody record and the hash work together.

**One receipt for several items.** Bundling a laptop, a drive and a phone on one receipt creates trouble the moment they follow different paths. One item, one number, one receipt.

**Initials without a register.** "AR" on a bag means nothing a year later if nobody can say who AR was.

**Mixed time zones.** A log in IST and an imaging tool reporting UTC will appear to disagree by five and a half hours. Record the zone on every form.

**Backfilling.** Completing missing entries days later from memory, without saying so, turns an honest gap into a misleading record.

**Forgetting derived items.** Images, exports and printouts are evidence too. An image with no record of when and from what it was made is hard to rely on.

**Leaving fields blank.** A blank "hash" or "witness" field invites speculation. Write "none taken" or "no witness available" with the reason.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Custody controls are also security controls. The threats are substitution (swapping an item for another), alteration (changing data on an item or a copy), loss, and quiet removal of an item that someone wants to disappear. The defences are layered.

- **Physical layer:** numbered tamper-evident seals, photographs of sealed packaging, locked storage with limited access, and store registers.
- **Data layer:** write blocking during acquisition, hashes computed at creation and verified at each use, read-only or retention-locked evidence storage, and working copies for analysis.
- **Record layer:** paired records that can be compared (bag label and store register, custody log and storage access log), sequential numbering that makes a missing item number visible, and corrections that add rather than overwrite.
- **People layer:** separation of duties, so the person who collects is not the only person who can access the store, and same-day reporting of defects.

Detection comes from comparison. A reviewer who cross-checks the custody log against the store register and the storage system's access log will find most substitution or removal attempts, because an attacker would need to falsify several independent records consistently.

## Best practices

- Assign the item number at collection and never reuse it.
- Describe items with at least two independent identifiers plus visible condition.
- Record time zone on every form; prefer one zone for the whole case.
- Photograph items in place and again when sealed, showing the seal number.
- Use one receipt and one log per item; give derived items their own linked numbers.
- Record the seal number on every opening and resealing, and keep old seals.
- Have both custodians sign every transfer, with purpose stated.
- Verify and record hash values each time a derived item is used.
- Correct by adding, never by erasing; explain gaps in a dated memorandum.
- Have a second person review the full custody packet before findings are reported.

## Key takeaways

- Chain of custody is a chronological record of who held each item, when, where, why and what they did, from identification to final disposition.
- Its purpose is continuity and integrity, not proof of any finding; legal sufficiency is decided by others.
- Every stage of the lifecycle and every hand-over produces a record, and records created at collection cannot be recreated later.
- Physical items and data follow linked but separate chains; derived items such as images need their own numbers and starting records.
- A custody log is read as linked entries: each receiver becomes the next releaser, and each seal change is explained.
- Mistakes are corrected by adding records and explained in writing, never by altering earlier entries.
- Comparing independent records (bag label, store register, storage access log, hash values) is how substitution and alteration are detected.
