## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Kestrel Freight Pvt Ltd, the fictional Pune logistics company from this module, opened internal case **KF-2026-031** after a pricing spreadsheet appeared on a personal email account. For two weeks it is an internal matter: an employer examining its own laptop and USB drive under its own policies. Then the legal head decides to file a police complaint. The same laptop image, the same custody log and the same case notes are now headed for a criminal process, and possibly a court, governed by statutes that the examiners may never have read.

This lesson is about that moment. It explains the Indian legal framework that applies to electronic evidence, at the level a forensic practitioner needs: which laws exist, what each one asks of the evidence and its records, and how a lab's ordinary work (hashes, custody logs, contemporaneous notes) maps onto those requirements. It does not teach law, and it does not decide what is admissible in any case. Those are questions for counsel and, finally, for the court. A practitioner who understands the framework knows which records will be asked for and keeps them from the first day, instead of trying to reconstruct them when a lawyer calls.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> This lesson is a practitioner's orientation, not legal advice. Statutes, rules and court interpretations change, and new criminal laws came into force in India on 1 July 2024. Before relying on any provision in a real matter, confirm its current text and interpretation with the organisation's legal adviser.

Two ideas run through everything that follows. First, **the law asks about the record as much as the data**. Courts rarely examine raw disk images. They examine certificates, witness statements, custody records and expert reports that vouch for the data. Second, **evidence should be handled from the start as if it may reach a court**, because internal matters often become legal ones, and nobody can go back and create a contemporaneous record afterwards.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| BSA | Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023: the evidence law that replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, in force from 1 July 2024. |
| BNSS | Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023: the criminal procedure law that replaced the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, in force from 1 July 2024. |
| IT Act | Information Technology Act, 2000: defines electronic records and signatures, cyber offences, and bodies such as CERT-In. |
| Electronic record | Data, record or data generated, image or sound stored, received or sent in an electronic form, as defined in the IT Act. |
| Section 63 certificate | The certificate, in the form set out in the BSA Schedule, that accompanies an electronic record produced as evidence; the BSA successor to the earlier Section 65B certificate. |
| Proper custody | Custody in the place where, and under the care of the person with whom, a record would naturally be kept; relevant to whether a record is treated as primary evidence. |
| Examiner of Electronic Evidence | A body or department notified by the Central Government under Section 79A of the IT Act to give expert opinion on electronic evidence. |
| CERT-In Directions | The directions issued by CERT-In on 28 April 2022 under Section 70B(6) of the IT Act, covering incident reporting, log retention and clock synchronisation. |
| DPDP Act | Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023: governs the processing of digital personal data, including data handled during an investigation. |

## The framework at a glance

Four bodies of law matter most to a forensic practitioner in India.

The **IT Act, 2000** is the foundation. It gives electronic records legal recognition, defines cyber offences (for example unauthorised access and data theft under Sections 43 and 66), creates CERT-In as the national incident response agency, and, through Section 79A, lets the Central Government notify Examiners of Electronic Evidence whose opinion courts may rely on.

The **BSA, 2023** governs how evidence, including electronic records, is proved in court. It carries forward the earlier evidence law's approach to electronic records with important changes, described below.

The **BNSS, 2023** governs criminal procedure: how police search, seize, record and forward evidence, and when a forensic expert must be involved.

The **DPDP Act, 2023** governs personal data. Forensic work almost always touches other people's personal data, such as colleagues' emails and customers' records in a database, so it shapes how much is collected, who may see it and how long it is kept.

Alongside these, sector regulators (for example the Reserve Bank of India for regulated financial entities) issue their own directions on incident reporting and evidence preservation. An organisation's legal adviser decides which apply.

## Electronic records under the BSA

The BSA treats electronic records as documents. Its definition of "document" expressly includes electronic and digital records, and Section 61 states that the admissibility of a record shall not be denied on the sole ground that it is electronic or digital.

Two further provisions matter to practitioners. The first concerns **primary evidence**. The explanations to Section 57 include rules for electronic records. Among them, an electronic or digital record produced from proper custody is treated as primary evidence unless it is disputed. This is one reason custody records matter so much: they are how a party shows that a record comes from proper custody.

The second is **Section 63**, which deals with the admissibility of electronic records produced as evidence and is accompanied by a certificate in the form set out in the BSA's Schedule. Section 63 is the successor to Section 65B of the old Evidence Act, under which the Supreme Court held, in *Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer* (2014) and again in *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal* (2020), that the certificate was a condition for admitting secondary electronic evidence. How courts apply the corresponding BSA provisions in particular situations is a question for counsel.

The Schedule's certificate has two parts. **Part A** is completed by the party producing the record, typically the person responsible for the computer or device. **Part B** is completed by an expert. Both describe the device and the record, and the form asks for the **hash value of the electronic record and the algorithm used**. For a practitioner, that single field is where months of good practice either pay off or collapse. The hash must be the one recorded at acquisition, with its algorithm, traceable through the custody log and case notes to the item it describes.

**Technical interpretation.** Each legal instrument checks the evidence indirectly, through records. A strong technical acquisition with a weak record is legally weaker than a modest acquisition with a complete one.

**Practical example.** A defence lawyer asks how the hash in Part B of the certificate was obtained. The examiner turns to the acquisition notes for KF-031-E01-D1, which record the tool, its version, the time and the computed SHA-256, and to the custody log showing the image's storage since then.

**Security implications.** Records are evidence too and must be protected: a custody log stored on a shared drive that anyone can edit invites the question of whether it was altered.

**Key learning.** Each legal stage relies on a specific practitioner record. Keep them from the first day, and make sure the hash on the certificate is the hash from the acquisition.

## Search, seizure and the BNSS

When an internal matter becomes a police case, police procedure applies to anything the police seize. The BNSS introduced two changes that directly affect digital evidence.

**Section 105** requires the process of conducting a search, or taking possession of property, including preparing the list of seized items and its signing by witnesses, to be recorded through audio-video electronic means, preferably a mobile phone, and the recording to be forwarded without delay to the appropriate magistrate. For digital evidence this means a visual record of exactly what was seized and in what state, which complements the paper seizure memo.

**Section 176(3)** provides that, for offences punishable with imprisonment of seven years or more, a forensic expert shall visit the crime scene to collect forensic evidence, with the process videographed. The BNSS allows states time to put the necessary facilities in place, so practice may vary between states at present.

The BNSS also continues the power, under Section 94, for a court or police officer to require a person to produce a document or other thing, and it expressly includes electronic communication and digital evidence. Organisations receiving such a requirement should route it through their legal adviser and produce a verified, documented copy.

A private lab does not exercise these powers, but its work often precedes or supports them. Kestrel's internal collection receipt, photographs and custody log are what the police and the prosecution will look at to decide whether the evidence handed over is reliable. Handling internal evidence to the same standard as a police seizure makes that hand-over straightforward.

## Expert opinion and Section 79A

Courts often need expert help to understand electronic evidence. Under the BSA, the opinion of an expert is relevant when the court has to form an opinion on a technical matter, and Section 79A of the IT Act lets the Central Government notify bodies or departments as Examiners of Electronic Evidence. Opinions from such notified examiners carry a recognised status. Other experts may also give opinion evidence, but their competence, method and independence are open to examination.

For a practitioner, the lesson is that an expert's report is only as strong as the records under it. Lesson 1's principles become legal requirements in practice: the method must be explained, the tools named with versions, the limits stated, and facts kept separate from inferences and opinions. An expert who cannot show a repeatable method will struggle under cross-examination, whatever their reputation.

## Readiness: the CERT-In Directions

The CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022, issued under Section 70B(6) of the IT Act, are not evidence law, but they shape what evidence will exist. Among other things, they require covered entities to report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them, to enable logs of their ICT systems and keep them securely for a rolling period of 180 days within Indian jurisdiction, and to synchronise system clocks with the Network Time Protocol servers of the National Informatics Centre or the National Physical Laboratory, or with servers traceable to them.

Each requirement supports forensic work directly. Retained logs are the remote-logging layer from Lesson 3. Synchronised clocks make timelines from different systems comparable without guesswork. A six-hour reporting window means that preservation decisions and the first contemporaneous notes happen early, under time pressure, which is exactly when the habits in Lessons 5 and 6 matter. Which entities are covered and how each direction applies to a given organisation is a matter for its legal and compliance teams.

## Personal data and the DPDP Act

A laptop image holds far more than the evidence of one event. It contains the user's personal messages, colleagues' data, perhaps customers' records. The DPDP Act, 2023 governs the processing of digital personal data, and its rules are being brought into effect in phases. The Act includes exemptions, including for processing necessary for the prevention, detection, investigation or prosecution of offences, but whether and how an exemption applies to an organisation's internal investigation is a legal question.

What a practitioner can do regardless is practise **data minimisation**: examine what the authorised scope requires, restrict access to the people working on the case, avoid copying personal data into reports where a reference will do, and record the case's retention and disposal decisions. Lesson 6 showed how the case file records these controls. They protect the people whose data is in the image, and they protect the investigation from the challenge that it went beyond its authority.

## Worked example: the Kestrel hand-over to police (offline)

This is a fictional, paper-only review. Kestrel's legal head decides to file a police complaint about case KF-2026-031. Before the hand-over, the lab lead reviews the case file against what will be asked for.

1. **Authority and scope.** The written authorisation to collect the laptop and USB drive is on file, with the scope change that added the firewall export. Counsel will need it to explain the basis of the internal collection.
2. **Collection.** The evidence receipts for E-01 and E-02 record date, time with time zone, location, collector, witness, seal numbers and photographs. These are not BNSS seizure records, because Kestrel is not the police, but they will be compared with whatever the police record when they take possession.
3. **Custody.** The custody log for each item has no unexplained gaps since collection. The two defects identified in Lesson 5 are documented in a dated memorandum, not edited away.
4. **Acquisition.** The image KF-031-E01-D1 has a SHA-256 value recorded at acquisition, with the tool and version, and it was re-verified before examination.
5. **Certificate support.** The lab prepares a summary that lets the responsible person complete Part A and the expert complete Part B truthfully: the device, the record, the method, the hash value and the algorithm. The lab does not complete the legal certificate on its own initiative. It supplies facts to the people who sign it.
6. **Personal data.** The hand-over includes the items and records within scope. Unrelated personal data found during examination is listed in the case file's minimisation record and is not copied into the report.

The review finds one gap: the firewall export E-04 arrived with no manifest, so its integrity anchor is the lab's intake hash (Lesson 2). The lab records that limitation in the hand-over note so that nobody later assumes the export was verified at source.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"The certificate is a formality the lawyer fills in."** It states facts about the device, the record and the hash. If the lab's records cannot support those facts, nobody can complete it truthfully.
- **"A hash on the certificate is enough."** The hash must trace back, through custody and notes, to the item and moment it describes.
- **"Internal investigations do not need legal-grade records."** Internal matters regularly become police complaints, civil suits or labour disputes. Records cannot be made contemporaneous afterwards.
- **"Old procedures are still fine."** Templates that refer only to repealed provisions may omit fields the current forms ask for.
- **"Data protection does not apply to investigations."** Exemptions exist, but their scope is a legal question. Minimisation is good practice either way.
- **"Lab staff decide admissibility."** Courts decide admissibility. Labs make the evidence and its records as reliable and as well documented as they can.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Legal readiness is part of security readiness. An organisation that retains logs for the required period, synchronises its clocks, escrows encryption keys and trains staff to write receipts and notes will produce evidence that survives legal scrutiny. One that does not may know exactly what happened and still be unable to prove it. Equally, evidence records are attractive targets: an insider who can edit a custody log or a case note can undermine a whole case. Access controls, append-only logs, and hashes on the records themselves defend the investigation against that risk.

## Best practices

- Handle every internal matter as if it may reach a court.
- Keep a current, counsel-approved reference of the provisions your lab relies on, dated and reviewed after any legal change.
- Record hashes with their algorithms at every change of form, so that a certificate can cite them.
- Supply facts for certificates; let the responsible person and the expert sign what they can personally vouch for.
- Synchronise clocks and retain logs as the CERT-In Directions require where they apply to you.
- Apply data minimisation and record access, retention and disposal decisions in the case file.
- Route production requests, police requests and regulator requests through the legal adviser.

## Key takeaways

- India's new criminal laws, the BSA and BNSS, came into force on 1 July 2024. The IT Act and DPDP Act remain central to electronic evidence.
- Under the BSA, electronic records are documents. Section 63 and its two-part Schedule certificate, which asks for the hash value and algorithm, govern how they are produced as evidence.
- The BNSS requires audio-video recording of search and seizure and, for serious offences, forensic experts at the scene.
- Section 79A of the IT Act provides for notified Examiners of Electronic Evidence. Every expert's opinion depends on documented method.
- The CERT-In Directions on reporting, log retention and clock synchronisation determine what evidence will exist.
- The lab's job is records that let the legally responsible people state the facts truthfully. Admissibility is for counsel and the court.
