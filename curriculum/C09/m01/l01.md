## Introduction and conceptual foundation

At 06:10 one Saturday morning, the on-call engineer at the fictional Tamarind Logistics notices that a backup directory on the web server `web01` holds a copy of the customer list that nobody remembers putting there. The questions come quickly. Was the file copied out of the organisation? By which account? From where, and when? Did anything else leave with it? Every one of these questions is about the past, and the past cannot be observed directly. It can only be reconstructed from the traces it left behind: log lines, file timestamps, network packets, the contents of memory. **Digital forensics** is the discipline of reconstructing past events from such traces in a way that other people can check and rely on.

A working definition, consistent with the course library, is this: digital forensics applies scientific methods to identify, preserve, collect, examine, analyse and present digital evidence, so that its conclusions can withstand scrutiny, including in court. Each verb in that definition is a separate duty. **Identify** means knowing where relevant evidence might exist. **Preserve** means stopping it from changing or disappearing. **Collect** means acquiring it with its integrity provable. **Examine** and **analyse** mean extracting what is relevant and drawing reasoned conclusions. **Present** means explaining those conclusions, and their limits, to someone who did not do the work.

The words "scientific" and "withstand scrutiny" carry most of the weight. A clever analyst can usually produce *an* answer to "what happened on web01?". The forensic question is whether that answer would survive a second competent examiner repeating the work, a lawyer cross-examining every step, or a disciplinary panel deciding an employee's future on it. The principles in this lesson exist because forensic findings have consequences for people. An overstated conclusion can cost someone their job or their liberty; a carelessly handled disk can make true evidence unusable. The UK Post Office Horizon litigation, examined in Module 8, is the starkest modern reminder of what happens when computer evidence is presumed reliable rather than tested.

### Forensics and incident response

This course is called *Digital Forensics and Incident Response* (DFIR) because the two disciplines meet in almost every real case, but they pull in different directions. **Incident response** prioritises stopping harm quickly: contain the compromised host, disable the stolen account, restore service. **Forensics** prioritises accuracy and defensibility: preserve what happened before it is lost, and make sure every conclusion can be traced to its source. A responder who reboots `web01` to stop a suspicious process has done something sensible for the business and has destroyed the contents of memory. An examiner who insists on a full disk image before anyone may touch a ransomware-infected server may watch the encryption spread to the next twenty.

Neither extreme is right. Mature teams take IR decisions with evidence in mind (Course 08 Module 7 introduced this) and let forensic findings drive scoping and eradication. The principles in this lesson are what allow the two to coexist: they tell a responder which actions are acceptable on a live system, what must be recorded when an action changes evidence, and how much intrusion is justified by the urgency of the harm.

### What forensics can and cannot answer

Forensic evidence is good at answering **what**, **when**, **where** and **how**: which file was read, at what time, from which address, by which mechanism. It is weaker at **who**, and usually silent on **why**.

The weakness on "who" is fundamental and is the single most common source of overstated findings. A log line that reads `Accepted publickey for deploy from 172.31.0.20` proves that a client holding the private key for the `deploy` account authenticated from that address. It does not prove which human was at the keyboard. The key may be shared by a team, copied to a compromised laptop, or used by an automated job. The course library puts it in one sentence worth memorising: "Account divya did X" is not the same as "Divya did X". Moving from an account to a person requires additional, independent evidence (badge records, CCTV, device possession, an admission), much of which is collected by other teams, as Lesson 2 discusses.

"Why" is usually beyond forensic reach altogether. Intent is a matter for investigators, managers and courts weighing all the evidence, not something a timestamp can establish. An examiner who writes "the user deliberately exfiltrated the file" has moved from evidence to speculation; one who writes "the file was downloaded by a client using the `deploy` credentials at 05:51:40 UTC" has stayed within it.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Digital evidence | Information stored or transmitted in digital form that may be relied on to establish a fact in an investigation or proceeding. |
| Artefact | A specific trace left by activity on a system, such as a log entry, a timestamp, a registry value or a cache record. |
| Original evidence | The device, medium or data source as it existed when the investigation began. |
| Forensic image | A complete, bit-for-bit copy of a storage medium or volume, verified against the original by hash. |
| Working copy | A further verified copy of the image on which examination and analysis are performed, so the image itself is never altered. |
| Hash value | A fixed-length digest (for example SHA-256) computed from data; any change to the data changes the digest. Used to prove that a copy is identical to its source. |
| Integrity | The property that evidence has not been altered since it was collected, or that every alteration is known and recorded. |
| Repeatability | Another competent examiner applying the same documented method to the same data obtains the same result. |
| Footprint | The changes an examiner's own actions make to a live system (new processes, memory allocations, log entries, altered access times). |
| Exculpatory evidence | Evidence that tends to show that a suspected person or account was not responsible, or that an alternative explanation is true. |
| Defensible | Able to withstand challenge: every conclusion traces to evidence, every step is documented, and the limitations are stated. |

## The core forensic principles

Several bodies have codified good practice for digital evidence. In the United Kingdom, the ACPO *Good Practice Guide for Digital Evidence* set out four principles that are still widely quoted: no action should change data that may be relied on in court; where a person must access original data, they must be competent to do so and able to explain the relevance and implications of their actions; an audit trail of all processes applied to the evidence must be created and preserved, so that an independent third party can repeat them and achieve the same result; and the person in charge of the investigation is responsible for ensuring that the law and these principles are followed. Internationally, ISO/IEC 27037 gives guidance on the identification, collection, acquisition and preservation of digital evidence, and NIST SP 800-86 describes how forensic techniques fit into incident response.

This course uses seven working principles that cover the same ground in practical terms. Every later module applies them; most mistakes in forensic casework are a failure of one of them.

### Integrity

**Do not change original evidence.** Work on verified copies, and prove the copies are faithful by hashing the source before acquisition and the copy afterwards. If the digests match, the copy is bit-for-bit identical; if they differ, something changed and the examiner must find out what. Where integrity cannot be absolute (a live system changes every millisecond whether or not you touch it), the requirement becomes: know and record every change you introduce. Module 2 teaches acquisition and hashing in depth; the integrity model is shown in Figure 2 later in this lesson.

### Documentation

**Record every action, tool and tool version, time and person, contemporaneously**, meaning at the time, not reconstructed afterwards from memory. Documentation is what turns private skill into checkable method. A finding with no record of how it was produced is an opinion. Lesson 6 covers case notes, forms and reports in detail.

### Repeatability

**Another competent examiner using your method on the same data should get the same result.** Repeatability is the scientific heart of forensics. It is why examiners prefer documented tools with known behaviour, why they record exact commands and versions, and why they work from images whose hashes anyone can check. It does not mean every examiner will reach the same *interpretation*; it means that the factual observations (this file, this timestamp, this hash) can be reproduced by anyone who follows the record.

### Minimal intrusion

**Where live interaction is unavoidable, choose the least-changing method and record its expected footprint.** Capturing memory from a running server, querying a cloud API or running a live-response script all change the system: the tool occupies memory, creates processes and may write log entries. These actions are often justified, because the alternative (switching off the system) destroys more evidence than it preserves. The principle is not "never touch a live system" but "touch it deliberately, minimally and on the record". Lesson 3 explains how the order of volatility decides what to collect first.

### Competence

**Work only within your skill, and validate tools before relying on them.** Forensic tools are software, and software has bugs and undocumented behaviour. In one widely reported US murder trial, two browser-history tools disagreed about how often a search term had been visited, and the figure presented in court was later found to be wrong. The lesson, developed in Module 3, is that an examiner must understand what a tool actually measures, test it against data whose correct answer is known, and cross-check important findings with a second method or source.

### Objectivity

**Seek evidence that could disprove your hypothesis, and report exculpatory findings.** The examiner's duty is to the facts, not to the client, the incident lead or the investigator who commissioned the work. Confirmation bias is the occupational hazard: once an examiner believes the `deploy` account is guilty, every ambiguous artefact starts to look like proof. Objectivity is practised, not felt; the method section below shows how.

### Chain of custody

**Account for the possession of evidence continuously**, from the moment it is collected to the moment it is produced or destroyed. Who held it, when, where it was stored and why it moved. A gap in custody invites the question "could it have been altered during that time?", and hashes alone cannot always answer it. Lesson 5 is devoted to custody records.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The principles reinforce each other. Hashes prove integrity only if the hash values themselves are documented and custody is continuous; repeatability depends on documentation; objectivity depends on competence to recognise evidence that contradicts the working theory. Treat them as one system, not seven separate rules.

## Method: forensics as hypothesis testing

The seven principles describe how evidence must be handled. The method describes how conclusions are reached. A forensic examination is not a search for proof of something already believed. It is a cycle of forming explicit hypotheses and testing them against the evidence, including tests that could show a hypothesis is wrong.

For the `web01` case, an honest starting position lists several hypotheses at once. H1: an outside party used the `deploy` credentials to copy the customer list. H2: an employee with legitimate access copied the file for a business reason and left the copy in the backup directory. H3: an automated job wrote the file there as part of a misconfigured backup, and nothing left the organisation. Each hypothesis predicts different traces. H1 predicts an authentication from an address outside Tamarind's normal ranges and an outbound transfer of roughly the file's size. H2 predicts activity from a known workstation during working hours, perhaps with a change ticket. H3 predicts a scheduled-task or cron entry whose run time matches the file's creation time, and no outbound transfer.

The examiner's job is to look for the traces that separate the hypotheses, not just the traces that fit the favourite one. If the file's creation time matches a backup job to the second and the web server's outbound traffic shows nothing of the right size, H3 has survived a test that could have failed. H1 then needs positive evidence before anyone writes it in a report. This is what objectivity looks like in practice: written alternative explanations, and a deliberate search for the observation that would rule each one out.

**Components.** Six boxes run from left to right: **Identify** (where might relevant traces exist), **Preserve** (stop them changing or disappearing), **Collect** (acquire with provable integrity), **Examine** (extract the relevant artefacts), **Analyse** (test hypotheses against them) and **Present** (report findings and their limits). The labelled loop returns from Analyse to Examine.

**Relationships and arrows.** The forward arrows mean that each stage depends on the one before it. Nothing can be examined that was not collected, and nothing collected is trustworthy if it was not preserved first. The loop means that analysis regularly finds that the examined material is not enough to separate the hypotheses, so the examiner goes back and extracts more.

**Execution flow.** In the `web01` case, identification lists the server disk, the authentication log, the backup job configuration and the perimeter flow records. Preservation stops log rotation from deleting older entries. Collection produces verified copies. Examination extracts the file's timestamps, the relevant log lines and the cron schedule. Analysis compares them with the three hypotheses. If the cron schedule did not explain the copy, the loop would send the examiner back to examine shell history and flow records for that time window.

**Decision logic.** The loop ends when the remaining hypotheses have been tested as far as the evidence allows, not when one hypothesis "looks right". If a question cannot be answered from the available evidence, the report says so.

**Technical interpretation.** The left-hand stages decide what the right-hand stages can ever say. Evidence lost at preservation cannot be recovered at analysis, which is why preservation decisions belong in the first hour of an incident, not after the scoping meeting.

**Practical example.** An examiner who skips the loop writes "data exfiltrated by the deploy account" after seeing one log line. An examiner who follows it writes "the file was created by the nightly backup job at 02:00:03 UTC; no outbound transfer of comparable size appears in the monitored flow records for the following 48 hours".

**Security implications.** Attackers rely on responders acting before they think: rebooting, deleting and restoring before anything is preserved. The left side of the figure is where defenders win or lose the ability to explain an intrusion.

**Key learning.** Process stages are dependent, and analysis loops back to examination until every hypothesis has met a test that could have failed.

## The integrity model

Integrity is often summarised as "never touch the original", but the practical model has layers, and each layer has its own check. The **original evidence** is the source as it was found. It is handled as little as possible and kept under custody. The **forensic image** is a bit-for-bit copy whose hash, computed at acquisition, matches the hash of the source. The **working copy** is a further copy whose hash matches the image. Examination happens only on working copies, and every **finding** records which working copy, identified by its hash, it came from.

The model gives the examiner freedom to make mistakes safely. If an analysis tool modifies a working copy (some tools write index files or update access times), a new working copy is made from the image, and the hash proves it is a faithful starting point again. The image is never examined directly, so it remains a fixed reference that anyone can re-verify. The original is still there, sealed, if a court or the other side's expert wants their own image.

**Purpose.** The figure shows the four layers of the integrity model and the check that connects each layer to the one above it. It exists so that learners can say precisely which object a hash protects and which object a finding came from.

**Components.** The top layer, **Original evidence**, is the seized disk or the source system, kept sealed and under custody. The second layer, **Forensic image**, is the acquired bit-for-bit copy, whose hash must equal the source hash recorded at acquisition. The third layer, **Working copy**, is the copy actually examined, whose hash must equal the image hash. The bottom layer, **Findings**, is the analyst's output, each item citing the working copy and the artefact it was drawn from.

**Relationships and arrows.** The arrows run downwards because each layer is derived from the one above. Each arrow carries a check: the image is verified against the original's recorded hash, and the working copy against the image's hash. No arrow runs upwards. Nothing is ever written back to a higher layer.

**Execution flow.** At acquisition the examiner hashes the source, images it and hashes the image. Before analysis the examiner copies the image, hashes the copy and records the match in the case notes. After analysis, re-hashing the working copy shows whether the tools changed it, which matters when explaining any unexpected artefact.

**Decision logic.** If the image hash does not match the source hash, the acquisition is not a faithful copy and must be investigated before any analysis, as Module 2 explains. If a working copy's hash no longer matches, it is discarded and a fresh one is made. The image itself is never repaired.

**Technical interpretation.** Each hash proves identity only between the two objects it compares, at the moments it was computed. The chain of matches proves that the findings came from data identical to what was acquired. It does not prove that the acquisition captured everything, or that the source was unaltered before collection.

**Practical example.** A defence expert questions a timestamp in the `web01` report. Because the report cites the working copy's hash, and the case notes show that hash equals the image hash, the expert can request the image, verify it and look at the same bytes.

**Security implications.** The model also protects the organisation from its own mistakes. An examiner who analyses the original disk directly may alter timestamps, and then cannot tell their own changes from an intruder's.

**Key learning.** Examine working copies, keep the image as the fixed reference, keep the original sealed, and make every finding cite the copy it came from.

## Facts, inferences and opinions

A forensic report contains three kinds of statement, and confusing them is how reasonable findings become indefensible ones. A **fact** is a direct observation that another examiner can reproduce: "the authentication log on the working copy contains the line `Accepted publickey for deploy from 172.31.0.20` at 05:51:40 UTC". An **inference** is a conclusion drawn from facts by stated reasoning: "because 172.31.0.20 is the address assigned to the build server in the network inventory, the connection came from the build server". An **opinion** is a judgement that weighs several inferences and depends on the examiner's expertise: "in my opinion, the copy is more consistent with the backup job than with an external actor".

Each kind has a place, but each must be labelled. Facts need their source. Inferences need the reasoning and any assumption that would break them: here, that the inventory was accurate on that date. Opinions need the alternatives that were considered and the level of confidence. Module 8 introduces a certainty scale for wording findings consistently. For now, the discipline is simpler: before writing any sentence, decide which of the three it is.

## Worked example: applying the principles to web01

This is an offline review of decisions in the fictional `web01` case. No systems are touched.

At 06:10 the on-call engineer wants to delete the stray copy of the customer list "to reduce exposure". Under the **integrity** and **documentation** principles, the better action is to restrict access to the directory, record the file's path, size and timestamps, and leave the file in place until it is preserved. Deleting it would remove the file's own metadata, the most direct evidence of when and how it was created.

At 06:25 a team lead proposes rebooting the server because "something odd is running". The **minimal intrusion** principle asks what the reboot would destroy: memory contents, network connections and logged-in sessions. If memory may matter to H1, a memory capture by a competent responder comes first, with its footprint recorded. If the odd process is actively causing harm, containment can still come first, but the decision and its reason go in the notes.

At 09:00 the examiner receives the server image and finds that the backup job ran at 02:00. Under **objectivity**, the examiner still checks the flow records for an outbound transfer, because H1 has not yet been ruled out. The flow records show none of comparable size. Under **competence**, the examiner confirms what the flow source records and what it does not (for example, traffic that bypasses the monitored gateway), and states that limit in the report.

The report's conclusion stays within the evidence: the file was written by the scheduled backup job, a misconfiguration placed the backup in a web-accessible directory, and no outbound transfer of comparable size appears in the monitored flow records for the period examined. It names the account, the times, the sources and the limits, and it makes no claim about any person.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"A matching hash proves nothing happened."** A hash proves that two copies were identical at the moments it was computed. It says nothing about what happened to the source before acquisition.
- **"The account did it, so the person did it."** An account is a credential, not a human. Attribution to a person needs independent evidence.
- **"Live systems should never be touched."** Switching off a system destroys memory and connections. The principle is to act deliberately and record the footprint.
- **"The tool says so."** A tool's output is a claim about what the tool parsed. Important findings need validation or a second method.
- **"Documentation can be written up afterwards."** Notes reconstructed from memory are weaker and are easy to challenge. Record actions as they happen.
- **"Exculpatory findings belong to the other side."** They belong in your report. Leaving them out undermines the whole examination when they are discovered.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Forensic principles are not only for investigators; they shape how an organisation prepares. Log retention that outlasts typical attacker dwell times, synchronised clocks, documented backup jobs and an incident runbook that says "preserve before you clean" are what make the `web01` question answerable at all. Attackers who understand forensics clear logs, use valid accounts and rely on built-in tools precisely because those actions leave traces that are ambiguous or short-lived. The defensive response is to collect the traces that survive those tactics, such as centralised logs, flow records and authentication telemetry, before an incident happens, and to train first responders in the preservation decisions shown in Figure 1.

## Best practices

- Write the hypotheses down at the start, including the innocent ones, and record the test that would rule each one out.
- Preserve before you remediate wherever the harm allows. When it does not, record what was lost and why.
- Hash at every change of form: source, image, working copy and export.
- Cite the evidence item and copy for every fact in a report.
- Label each statement as fact, inference or opinion, and give the reasoning for every inference.
- Validate tools against data with a known answer before relying on them, and record versions.
- Report exculpatory findings and limitations with the same care as incriminating ones.

## Key takeaways

- Digital forensics reconstructs past events from traces in a way others can check, and its findings have consequences for people.
- The seven working principles (integrity, documentation, repeatability, minimal intrusion, competence, objectivity and chain of custody) work as one system.
- Forensics answers what, when, where and how well, who less well, and why rarely. Accounts are not people.
- Examination is hypothesis testing: list the alternatives and look for the observation that could rule each one out.
- The integrity model separates the sealed original, the reference image, the working copy and the findings, each linked by a recorded hash check.
- Label every statement as fact, inference or opinion.
