## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every indicator in a SOC's intelligence platform was, at some point, somebody else's bad day. The command-and-control domain that a feed lists with confidence 85 exists in that feed because another organisation saw it in its own telemetry, investigated, and decided to tell others. Threat intelligence is therefore not only something a SOC consumes; it is an exchange. The quality of what you receive depends on how many organisations are willing to share, and how willing they are depends on whether the people they share with can be trusted to handle the information properly.

That last point is the subject of this lesson. Sharing is easy to do badly in two opposite ways. A SOC that shares nothing becomes a free rider: it takes indicators from communities and vendors but never reports a sighting, never corrects a false positive and never contributes the first-party indicators its own incidents produce. A SOC that shares carelessly is worse: it pastes a partner's restricted indicator into a public chat group, forwards a client's incident details to a vendor without permission, or publishes a note containing a customer's name and a staff member's user name. The first failure starves the community; the second destroys the trust that makes sharing possible at all, and in India it may also breach the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, a contract or a sector regulator's expectations.

Responsible sharing sits between those failures. It asks five questions before anything leaves the SOC:

1. **Why are we sharing, and with whom?** A sighting report back to the source, an early warning to sector peers and a mandatory incident report to CERT-In are different acts with different audiences.
2. **What restrictions did the information arrive with?** A Traffic Light Protocol label, a feed licence, a non-disclosure agreement or a client contract can each forbid onward sharing.
3. **What must be removed?** Personal data, internal identifiers and anything that would identify a victim who has not agreed to be identified.
4. **Under what label and through which channel?** The outbound label tells recipients how far they may pass the information on; the channel must be one that can honour it.
5. **Who approves it, and how is it tracked?** Outbound intelligence is a release of company information and needs an owner, an approval and a record.

Earlier lessons in this module dealt with the inbound direction: indicators, confidence, enrichment, MISP, feeds and their limits. This lesson covers the outbound direction: the labels, communities, legal limits and hygiene that let a SOC give intelligence back safely. Lesson 8 applies it to the course dataset.

> [!NOTE]
> This lesson describes rules and institutions at the level of stable, published facts. Sharing agreements, sector regulations and CERT-In directions are updated over time; before relying on any specific obligation in practice, read the current text from the issuing body and involve your legal or compliance team.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Traffic Light Protocol (TLP) | A set of labels, maintained by FIRST, that a source attaches to information to tell recipients how widely they may share it. Version 2.0 is current. |
| FIRST | Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams, the global association of incident response teams that publishes the TLP standard. |
| Source | In TLP terms, the person or organisation that labels and shares the information. Only the source decides its label. |
| Recipient | Anyone who receives labelled information and must respect the label. |
| Community | A group who share goals, practices and informal trust; in TLP 2.0, the default community is the cybersecurity or cyber defence community. |
| ISAC | Information Sharing and Analysis Center: a membership organisation through which organisations in one sector exchange threat information. |
| CERT-In | Indian Computer Emergency Response Team, the national agency for incident response under section 70B of the IT Act 2000. |
| Sighting | A report that an indicator was observed in your environment, sent back to the source or community to confirm or refute it. |
| First-party intelligence | Indicators and observations produced from your own telemetry and investigations, as opposed to ones received from others. |
| Personal data (DPDP) | Under the DPDP Act 2023, any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to that data. |
| Sanitisation | Removing or generalising sensitive content (personal data, internal identifiers, victim identity) before release. |

## Why SOCs share at all

A single organisation sees a tiny slice of any campaign. The phishing domain that delivered a macro document to one employee at a microfinance company may have been sent to staff at twenty others in the same week. If the first organisation to investigate publishes the domain to its sector within hours, the other nineteen can block it, search their proxy logs for earlier visits and alert their users before the attacker reaches the next stage. That is the core economic argument for sharing: an indicator is cheap to share and expensive to discover, so a community that shares spreads the cost of discovery across its members.

Sharing also improves the intelligence itself. Lesson 2 showed that confidence rises when independent sources corroborate a claim. A sighting report, even one sentence saying "we saw this domain from one workstation, beaconing every five minutes", is exactly that corroboration. Reports of false positives are how feeds get cleaned. The community feed is only as good as the feedback its members send.

There are also obligations. Some reporting is mandatory: Indian organisations covered by the CERT-In directions of April 2022 must report specified types of cyber incident to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them. Regulated sectors may have their own reporting duties to their regulator. Contracts with clients, especially for managed security service providers, often require prompt notification. Mandatory reporting is not "sharing" in the voluntary sense, but it uses the same skills: deciding what to send, stripping what must not go, and recording what was sent and when.

Finally, sharing has costs and risks that explain why organisations hesitate. Sharing can reveal that you were breached, which has reputational and sometimes legal consequences. It can tip off an attacker who watches public sources and sees their infrastructure listed. It can expose personal data. Good sharing practice exists to reduce those risks to a level where the benefit clearly wins, and the most important tool for that is a shared vocabulary for "how far may this go?": the Traffic Light Protocol.

## The Traffic Light Protocol, version 2.0

The Traffic Light Protocol was created to make person-to-person sharing of sensitive information fast and predictable. Instead of negotiating terms each time, the source attaches one of a small set of labels, and every recipient knows what they may do with it. FIRST maintains the standard. Version 2.0, which replaced version 1.0 and has applied since August 2022, made three changes worth knowing: TLP:WHITE was renamed **TLP:CLEAR**, a new label **TLP:AMBER+STRICT** was added, and the wording was rewritten for clarity, with explicit definitions of the terms the labels depend on, such as "community", "organisation" and "clients".

The labels are written in capitals with the prefix and no space: `TLP:RED`, `TLP:AMBER+STRICT`, `TLP:AMBER`, `TLP:GREEN`, `TLP:CLEAR`. The label should appear where a recipient cannot miss it: at the top of an email or document, in the subject line, on each slide, or as a tag on the object in a sharing platform. In MISP, for example, TLP labels are commonly attached as tags from the TLP taxonomy, which is separate from MISP's own distribution setting (the setting that controls which organisations and instances the event is synchronised to). Lesson 4 described the distribution levels; the point here is that the two must agree. An event tagged TLP:RED with distribution set to "all communities" contradicts itself, and the platform will happily send it everywhere.

It answers the two questions every analyst must be able to answer instantly when information arrives or leaves: "who may I pass this to?" and "why would a source choose this label?" **Components.** There are three columns. **Label** names the TLP marking exactly as it must be written. **Recipient may share with** states the audience to which a recipient may pass the information onwards. **Source uses it when** gives the typical reason a source picks that label. There are five rows, ordered from most restrictive at the top to least restrictive at the bottom, and each row carries a coloured marker and label that follows the traffic-light idea: red for **TLP:RED**, orange for **TLP:AMBER+STRICT** and **TLP:AMBER**, green for **TLP:GREEN** and slate for **TLP:CLEAR**.

**Relationships.** Reading down the middle column, each row's audience contains the one above it. Named recipients are part of their own organisation; the organisation is part of the set "organisation and its clients"; all of those sit within the wider community; and the community is part of "anyone". That nesting is why the order matters: moving down a row always widens distribution and never narrows it.

**Execution flow.** In practice the table is used twice. On receipt, you find the label on the incoming item and read across to see what you may do with it: TLP:AMBER+STRICT from an ISAC means your SOC and the rest of your organisation's need-to-know staff, but not your organisation's clients. On release, you decide who needs your information and read the table in reverse to find the most restrictive label that still reaches them.

**Decision logic.** Two rules sit behind the rows. First, the label is set by the source and only the source can change it; the note under the table repeats this. Second, when you combine items, the combined product inherits the most restrictive label among its parts. A case note that quotes one TLP:AMBER indicator becomes TLP:AMBER as a whole, even if everything else in it is TLP:GREEN.

**Technical interpretation.** TLP is a human-readable promise, not an access-control mechanism. Nothing in the label encrypts a file or stops a forward button. Platforms can help, for example by mapping TLP tags to distribution settings, but enforcement rests on the recipients' discipline and on the community's willingness to exclude members who break it.

**Practical example.** The course feed in `ioc_match.py` carries labels on each indicator: `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` from ISAC-A is TLP:AMBER, `203.0.113.66` from ISAC-A is TLP:GREEN and the old scanning address from an open feed is TLP:CLEAR. A Banyan analyst may discuss the AMBER domain with Banyan's own incident responders, but may not post it to a sector-wide chat group without ISAC-A's permission. The GREEN address may go to sector peers; the CLEAR address may be quoted in a public blog post.

**Security implications.** Over-labelling is a real failure too: marking everything TLP:RED means nobody can act on it, so recipients either ignore the information or quietly break the label. Choose the least restrictive label that keeps the risk acceptable.

**Key learning.** Read across the row before you forward anything, and when items are combined, the strictest label wins.

### Using the labels correctly

A few details of the standard trip up even experienced analysts.

**The audience is defined by the label, not by convenience.** TLP:GREEN allows sharing within the recipient's community, which by default in TLP 2.0 means the cyber defence community, but it explicitly excludes publicly accessible channels. A public social media post, a public paste site, a public code repository and a public online sandbox are all public channels. Submitting a TLP:GREEN file sample to a public analysis service that shares submissions with its users is a breach of the label, even though the motive was good.

**Sources may add limits.** TLP 2.0 lets a source state additional intended limits, such as "for incident responders only", and those limits must be respected alongside the label.

**Permission widens a label; time does not.** Information does not become TLP:CLEAR because it is old. If a recipient needs to share more widely, the correct path is to ask the source. Many sources will agree once the immediate risk has passed or once their own investigation has ended, and a short written request with the reason is usually enough.

**Unlabelled information is not automatically CLEAR.** If something arrives without a label, treat it according to the context and the agreement under which it was received, and ask the sender. Do not assume the absence of a label is permission.

**TLP is not a classification scheme.** It does not replace an organisation's own information classification, legal privilege, or contractual confidentiality. A document can be TLP:GREEN for sharing purposes and still be "Confidential" under the company's internal policy; the stricter of the two applies to what you do with it.

## Sharing communities and ISACs

Labels only work inside a community whose members know and honour them. Several kinds of community exist, and a SOC typically belongs to more than one.

**Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs).** The ISAC model originated in the United States in the late 1990s as a way for critical-infrastructure sectors to exchange threat information, and it has since spread internationally. An ISAC is a membership organisation for one sector: financial services, health, energy, aviation and so on. Members share indicators, incident summaries, phishing samples and analysis, usually through a sharing platform such as MISP alongside mailing lists and member calls. The value of an ISAC comes from the similarity of its members: they face the same adversaries and the same lures, so a warning from one is directly relevant to the others. Membership usually involves an agreement covering confidentiality, use of shared material and expected participation.

**National CERTs and critical-infrastructure bodies.** In India, CERT-In publishes advisories and vulnerability notes, receives incident reports and coordinates response. Critical information infrastructure is the concern of the National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre (NCIIPC), designated under section 70A of the IT Act 2000. These bodies are both sources of intelligence for a SOC and recipients of its reports.

**Vendor and product communities.** Security vendors run communities around their products and share threat research, often under their own terms of use. Those terms frequently limit redistribution of the vendor's feed content.

**Trusted informal groups.** Many analysts belong to closed, vetted groups of peers that share quickly and informally. They can be very effective, but their trust rests entirely on members' behaviour, and their membership rules must still be honoured.

**Open communities and public sources.** Public blocklists, open-source intelligence projects and public reports are TLP:CLEAR by nature. They are useful context, but anything you send to them is public.

When choosing where to send something, match the community to the purpose. A sighting of an ISAC-A indicator goes back to ISAC-A. An early warning about a lure aimed at microfinance staff fits a financial-sector ISAC or peer group. A reportable incident goes to CERT-In through its published reporting channels, and to a sector regulator if one requires it. A public blog post is for material that is already TLP:CLEAR and approved for publication.

## CERT-In and mandatory reporting

CERT-In is the national agency for incident response under section 70B of the Information Technology Act 2000. On 28 April 2022, CERT-In issued directions under section 70B(6) that apply to service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations. Three requirements in those directions shape SOC practice directly:

- **Reporting within six hours.** Specified types of cyber incident must be reported to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them or being brought to notice of them. The directions list the incident types, which include, among others, compromise of systems, unauthorised access to IT systems and data, and data breaches.
- **Log retention.** Logs of ICT systems must be maintained securely for a rolling period of 180 days within Indian jurisdiction, and provided to CERT-In when required.
- **Time synchronisation.** ICT systems' clocks must be synchronised to the Network Time Protocol servers of the National Informatics Centre or the National Physical Laboratory, or to servers traceable to them.

For a SOC, the six-hour clock is the most demanding. It means the decision "is this reportable?" must be made early, with incomplete information, and the initial report contains what is known at that point, with updates following. It also means the SOC needs the same outbound discipline as for voluntary sharing: a named person who prepares the report, a template that captures the required fields, a step that removes anything that should not be sent, an approval, and a record of what was submitted and when. The report itself goes through the channels CERT-In publishes on its website; use those current details, not a contact copied from an old document.

Mandatory reporting and TLP interact in one specific way. A report to CERT-In is not a community release: you are required to provide the information, and the TLP label you attach describes how you wish it to be handled onward. If your report contains a partner's TLP:AMBER indicator, tell the partner that you included it in a mandatory report; well-drafted sharing agreements anticipate legal obligations of this kind.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The six-hour window, the 180-day log retention and the clock-synchronisation requirement come from the CERT-In directions of 28 April 2022. Check the current directions and any FAQs CERT-In has issued before building them into a runbook, and confirm with your legal team which of your organisation's systems they cover.

## The outbound pipeline

Everything that leaves the SOC, whether a one-line sighting or a formal incident report, should pass through the same sequence of checks. The checks are quick for a small item and more involved for a large one, but none of them is optional.

**Purpose.** The flow is the release procedure for outbound intelligence. It turns the five questions from the introduction into an ordered set of checks, so that a tired analyst at the end of a long incident does not skip the one that matters. Its message is that sharing is a controlled release of company information, not a forward button.

**Components.** Six numbered steps run from top to bottom. **1 Purpose and audience** (blue) names why the item is being shared and who should receive it: a sighting back to a source, a peer warning or a mandatory report. **2 Inherited limits** (violet) gathers every restriction the content arrived with: the source's TLP, a feed licence, a non-disclosure agreement or a client contract. **3 Strip and minimise** (orange) removes personal data, internal identifiers and the identity of any victim who has not agreed to be named. **4 Label and channel** (green) assigns the outbound TLP and picks a channel able to honour it. **5 Approve and record** (violet) gives the release an owner and an approver and records what went to whom. **6 Share and follow up** (dark) is the release itself and the work afterwards: tracking sightings, sending corrections and retiring indicators when they expire.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each solid arrow means "only proceed when the previous step is complete". The order is deliberate. Inherited limits (step 2) come before stripping (step 3) because some content cannot be shared at all, and there is no point sanitising it. Labelling (step 4) comes after stripping because the label depends on what remains: removing a partner's AMBER indicator may allow a GREEN release. The dashed arrow labelled **feedback and corrections** runs from step 6 back to step 2: replies from recipients, a source's permission to widen a label, or a correction that an indicator is benign change the limits for the next release.

**Execution flow.** For a one-line sighting back to the source, steps 1 to 5 take minutes: the audience is the source, the inherited label is theirs, little needs stripping, the label stays the same and the shift lead approves. For a community early warning built from an incident, each step may take real work, especially step 3.

**Decision logic.** Step 2 can end the process: if a contract or the source's label forbids the intended audience, the answer is either a narrower audience or a request for permission. Step 3 can change the audience too: if the content cannot be made safe without destroying its value, share less, or share with fewer people.

**Technical interpretation.** In a MISP-based workflow, step 2 corresponds to reading the tags and distribution of the events you are drawing on, step 4 to setting the outbound event's TLP tag and distribution level, and step 6 to sightings and updates on the event. A home-grown workflow needs the same fields in a ticket.

**Practical example.** In Lesson 8 you will draft a note about the Banyan Microfinance incident. Step 2 finds the ISAC-A AMBER domain; step 3 finds a user name, a host name and an internal address in the first draft; step 4 produces a TLP:GREEN community note without the AMBER domain, plus a separate TLP:AMBER sighting back to ISAC-A asking permission to share it.

**Security implications.** Most sharing incidents happen at steps 2 and 3: a restricted item forwarded too widely, or personal data left in an attachment. A record from step 5 is what lets you respond if a recipient later asks what was sent.

**Key learning.** Check limits, strip, then label; never label first and hope the content fits.

## What to strip before sharing

The goal of sanitisation is to keep everything a recipient needs to detect or block the threat and remove everything that identifies people inside your organisation or exposes it unnecessarily. Recipients need **attacker-side** facts: the infrastructure, the files, the lures, the techniques and the timings. They almost never need **victim-side** facts: who clicked, which workstation, which internal address, which customer records.

| Keep (usually) | Strip or generalise (usually) |
| --- | --- |
| Attacker domains, URLs and IP addresses | Staff names, user names and email addresses |
| File names and hashes of malicious files | Internal host names and internal IP addresses |
| Lure themes and sender patterns (without the recipient list) | Customer data, account numbers, transaction details |
| Techniques, with MITRE ATT&CK IDs | Full email bodies addressed to named staff |
| Detection ideas: event IDs, process relationships | File paths containing user profile names |
| Timing patterns (beacon interval, spray rate) | Screenshots that show internal systems |
| Validity period and confidence | The victim organisation's identity, unless agreed |

### Personal data under the DPDP Act 2023

India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 defines personal data broadly as any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to that data. In the context of a security incident that includes staff names and user names, email addresses, phone numbers, and potentially an IP address or device identifier where it can be linked to a person, such as a staff member's home address in VPN logs. The Act applies to the processing of digital personal data, and sharing it with an outside party is processing.

The organisation that decides why and how personal data is processed is the **Data Fiduciary**, and the individual is the **Data Principal**. The Data Fiduciary must take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach, and in the event of a breach must inform the Data Protection Board of India and each affected Data Principal. Failure to take reasonable security safeguards can attract a penalty of up to 250 crore rupees under the Act's schedule. Note the double edge for a SOC: an incident may itself be a personal data breach with its own notification duties, handled by the organisation's privacy and legal teams, while careless intelligence sharing could create a new disclosure of personal data.

The practical rule follows from the Act's purpose limitation: share only the personal data that the purpose requires, and for threat-intelligence sharing the purpose almost never requires any. The attacker's domain protects the next organisation; your employee's user name does not. When personal data is genuinely necessary, for example a mandatory report that must identify affected accounts, it goes only to the body that requires it, through its official channel, with the approval of the privacy or legal team.

Two kinds of personal data deserve special care because they look technical. **Attacker-side data can be personal data too.** A spray source address may be a compromised home router belonging to an innocent person; label it as "observed source", not as "attacker", and prefer short validity. **Lures often contain victims' data.** A phishing email that greets the recipient by name, or an invoice lure that quotes a real customer, must be redacted before the sample is shared.

## Legal and contractual limits

Beyond TLP and data protection, several other limits can stop or narrow a release:

- **Sharing agreements and membership terms.** ISACs and closed groups have rules on how shared material may be used and passed on. Breaking them can end your membership.
- **Feed licences.** Commercial feeds are licensed for the buyer's own defensive use, and licences commonly forbid redistribution. Re-publishing a vendor's indicators in your community note, even unchanged, may breach the licence. Share your own sightings instead and name the vendor as the source only if the licence allows.
- **Client contracts.** A managed security service provider sees many clients' incidents. A client's incident details belong to that client; using them to warn another client needs permission, or must be stripped to the point where the client cannot be identified.
- **Non-disclosure agreements and legal privilege.** Incident investigations are sometimes run under legal counsel so that work products are privileged. Sharing outside the privileged group may waive that protection; ask counsel before sharing anything from such an investigation.
- **Law enforcement and regulators.** An ongoing investigation may require that certain details are not disclosed. Equally, CERT-In or a regulator may require you to provide information; tell the source when its material is included in such a report.
- **IT Act 2000.** Section 72A makes it an offence, in defined circumstances, to disclose personal information obtained while providing services under a lawful contract without the person's consent. It is another reason why a service provider must not pass on clients' personal data.
- **Attribution and accusation.** Naming a company, hosting provider or person as malicious when they are themselves a victim (for example, a compromised legitimate website) can cause harm and legal risk. Describe observations ("served the lure on 3 November"), not accusations.

> [!CAUTION]
> When a limit is unclear, the default is not to share widely. Share narrowly (back to the source, or within your organisation) while you ask. A late warning is better than a breach of trust that closes the channel for good.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"Old information is public."** Age does not change a TLP label. Ask the source.
- **"TLP:GREEN means I can post it on social media."** GREEN excludes public channels.
- **"Anonymised means removing the company name."** Combinations of details (sector, city, date, lure theme quoting a customer) can still identify a victim.
- **"We must share everything to be good members."** Quality beats volume. One accurate sighting with timing and confidence is worth more than a dump of unvetted indicators.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Sharing is itself an attack surface. Attackers monitor public repositories and paste sites for their own infrastructure, so publishing too early or too widely can prompt them to rotate domains and tools before others have searched for them. Shared samples can leak a lure's internal details, which helps the next attacker craft a better one.

Defensively, treat outbound sharing like any other privileged action: restrict who can publish to external platforms, log every release, review the logs, and include "intelligence release" in data-loss-prevention policies for obvious cases such as internal address ranges and staff email domains appearing in outbound sharing-platform traffic.

## Best practices

- Write a short sharing policy: who may release, to which communities, under which labels, with which approvals.
- Keep a register of your communities and agreements, with each one's rules and contacts.
- Carry labels forward automatically in your tooling; flag any product whose content mixes labels.
- Send sightings and false-positive reports back to sources routinely. They are the cheapest and most valued contribution.
- Use a fixed template for outbound notes with explicit fields for label, confidence, validity and "not included".
- Run a pre-share check for internal identifiers, and still read the note yourself; a tool finds patterns, not judgement errors.
- Record every release and every permission to widen a label.

## Key takeaways

- Sharing makes everyone's intelligence better, but only inside a community that honours its labels; careless sharing destroys the trust it depends on.
- TLP 2.0 has five labels: TLP:RED, TLP:AMBER+STRICT, TLP:AMBER, TLP:GREEN and TLP:CLEAR. Only the source sets a label, combined products take the strictest label, and wider sharing needs the source's permission.
- ISACs, CERT-In, NCIIPC, vendor communities and closed peer groups each have a role; match the community to the purpose.
- The CERT-In directions of April 2022 require reporting of specified incidents within six hours, 180-day log retention in India and synchronised clocks.
- Strip victim-side data, especially personal data under the DPDP Act 2023; recipients need attacker-side facts.
- Feed licences, client contracts, NDAs, legal privilege and section 72A of the IT Act can all limit a release; when unsure, share narrowly and ask.
