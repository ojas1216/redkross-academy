## Lab objective and scope

This lab takes the course feed's indicators through the full intelligence cycle that this module has described, from the moment they match your telemetry to the moment you give something back to the community. You will match the feed against the Banyan Microfinance events, verify each sighting in the raw data, grade each indicator for source reliability and information credibility, age it against its validity date, derive new first-party indicators from the incident, and write a short intelligence note that is safe to share. A pre-share check catches internal identifiers left in the draft before the note is released.

The Module 5 triage lab used the same matcher to *enrich alerts*; there, the question was "does intelligence make this alert more or less likely to be real?" Here the question is reversed: "what do our alerts tell us about the intelligence, and what can we tell others?" The skill being practised is turning a list of matches into graded, dated, labelled intelligence that another SOC could act on without ever learning who clicked what.

By the end of the lab you should be able to say, from outputs you produced yourself: which indicators are confirmed by your own detections and which are not, which ones are no longer valid and what you will do about them, what new indicators the incident produced, and exactly what you shared, with whom and under which label.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Authorisation and scope.** Everything in this lab runs on your own computer against a synthetic dataset about a fictional organisation, Banyan Microfinance, and a fictional feed. All names, hosts and addresses are fictional; IP addresses use private ranges and the RFC 5737 documentation ranges, and domains end in `.example`. No network connection is made, no indicator is submitted anywhere and nothing is shared outside your machine. "Releasing" a note in this lab means saving a file. Work only in your own copy of the lab kit, never inside the course library folder.

## Key terminology

| Term | Meaning in this lab |
| --- | --- |
| As-of date | The date the matcher treats as "now": `AS_OF = 2026-11-03` in `ioc_match.py`, the day of the incident. |
| Sighting | An event in the Banyan data that contains the indicator's value. |
| Source reliability | A letter A-F for how trustworthy the source has been, assigned by the analyst per source (Lesson 2). |
| Information credibility | A digit 1-6 for how well this particular item is supported, here derived from local sightings and detections. |

| First-party indicator | An indicator the incident itself produced, not received from any feed. |
| Pre-share check | A scan of an outbound note for internal identifiers before release. |

## How the lab works

The lab is a pipeline of seven stages. The first two use the kit as shipped: `ioc_match.py` compares five feed indicators with the events in the SQLite database built by `minisiem.py`, and `minisiem.py search` lets you look at the raw events behind each match. Two short scripts that you write yourself then do what the kit leaves to the analyst: `intel_grade.py` grades and ages each indicator by reusing the kit's feed and correlation rules, and `scrub_check.py` searches an outbound note for user names, host names and internal addresses taken from the dataset itself. Writing them yourself is deliberate. Both encode judgements (which source deserves which letter, what counts as an internal identifier) that you must be able to read and defend, not accept from a black box.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The graph is the route through the lab. It shows that a feed match is only the first of seven stages, and that nothing reaches the community until every indicator has been verified, graded and aged and the outbound note has passed a check for internal identifiers.

**Components.** Nine rounded boxes run from top to bottom. **Match** (slate) is `ioc_match.py` against `minisiem.db`. **Verify sightings** (blue) is `minisiem.py search` on the raw events. **Grade** (violet) is `intel_grade.py`, combining reliability and credibility. **Age** (violet) checks validity at three check dates. **Derive first-party IOCs** (orange) extracts new indicators from the R3 and R4 hits: a domain and an account name. **Draft note** (blue) assembles attacker-side facts with a TLP per item. **Scrub check** (red) is `scrub_check.py`. At the bottom sit the two outputs: **TLP:GREEN peer note** (green), holding first-party and GREEN items, and **TLP:AMBER sighting to ISAC-A** (dark), which asks the source for permission to widen its label.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each solid arrow means "the output of this stage feeds the next". Verified sightings feed the credibility half of the grade; the grade and age decide which indicators are worth sharing; derived indicators join them in the draft. The dashed arrow labelled **findings > 0** runs from **Scrub check** back up to **Draft note**: any finding sends the draft back for editing. The two solid arrows labelled **0 findings** are the only way to the outputs.

**Execution flow.** You run stages one to five once each, in order. The draft and scrub stages form a loop you repeat until the checker prints zero findings. The two outputs are written last and released separately, because they go to different audiences under different labels.

**Decision logic.** There are two gates. The first is inside **Grade** and **Age**: an indicator that is expired or ungraded does not go into the note as an actionable item. The second is **Scrub check**: a non-zero count blocks release. A zero count does not approve the note on its own; it only means the tool found nothing, and you still read the note before release.

**Technical interpretation.** Every stage except the two outputs is backed by a command. `intel_grade.py` imports `FEED` and `AS_OF` from `ioc_match.py` and `DB` and `RULES` from `minisiem.py`, so it grades exactly the indicators and detections the kit defines. `scrub_check.py` builds its list of user names from `SELECT DISTINCT user FROM events`, so it stays correct if the dataset changes.

**Practical example.** The beacon domain `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` matches 18 events, is named in an R6 hit, grades B1 HIGH and is valid, but it arrived at TLP:AMBER, so it leaves the pipeline through the dark box, not the green one.

**Security implications.** The route places the identifier check after drafting, where leaks actually happen. Skipping it is how a user name or internal address reaches a sector mailing list.

**Key learning.** Match, verify, grade, age, derive, draft, check: share only what survives all seven, and send each item to the audience its label allows.

### Step 1: Match the feed

Run the kit's matcher. It needs no arguments: the feed and the as-of date are defined at the top of the script.

Each line shows the status, the indicator type and value, the feed's confidence on a 0-100 scale, the number of matching events, the first matching timestamp, the source and the TLP label. ### Step 3: Grade each indicator

Lesson 2 introduced two-part grading: a letter for the **source's reliability** (A completely reliable, B usually reliable, C fairly reliable, D not usually reliable, E unreliable, F cannot be judged) and a digit for the **credibility of the item** (1 confirmed by other sources, 2 probably true, 3 possibly true, 4 doubtful, 5 improbable, 6 cannot be judged). The letter is a property of the source, the digit of the particular claim. Create `intel_grade.py` in your working folder:

```python
"""Grade and age the ioc_match.py feed against minisiem detections (C08 M6 lab).

Run from the lab working copy after: python minisiem.py load
Usage: python intel_grade.py
"""
import sqlite3
from datetime import date

from ioc_match import FEED, AS_OF
from minisiem import DB, RULES

# Source reliability (Admiralty-style letter) assigned by the analyst for this lab.
SOURCES = {"ISAC-A": "B", "internal": "B", "vendor": "B", "open feed": "C"}
CHECK_DATES = [AS_OF, date(2026, 11, 16), date(2027, 1, 1)]


def rule_hits(con):
    """Return {rule name: flattened text of every hit row}."""
    out = {}
    for name, sql in RULES.items():
        out[name] = " ".join(str(v) for row in con.execute(sql).fetchall() for v in row)
    return out


def sightings(con, ioc_type, value):
    if ioc_type == "domain":
        sql, arg = "SELECT COUNT(*) FROM events WHERE url LIKE ?", f"%://{value}/%"
    elif ioc_type == "ipv4":
        sql, arg = "SELECT COUNT(*) FROM events WHERE src_ip = ?", value
    else:
        return None  # no hash telemetry in this dataset
    return con.execute(sql, (arg,)).fetchone()[0]


def grade(rel, hits, rules, valid):
    if hits is None:
        cred = "6"          # cannot be judged locally: no telemetry of this type
    elif hits and rules:
        cred = "1"          # sighted AND an independent detection names it
    elif hits:
        cred = "3"          # sighted, nothing else corroborates
    else:
        cred = "6"
    if not valid:
        verdict = "LOW (expired)"
    elif rel in "AB" and cred in "12":
        verdict = "HIGH"
    elif rel in "ABC" and cred in "123":
        verdict = "MEDIUM"
    else:
        verdict = "UNGRADED (keep feed score)"
    return cred, verdict


def main():
    con = sqlite3.connect(DB)
    hits_by_rule = rule_hits(con)
    print(f"as-of {AS_OF}; ageing checked at {', '.join(str(d) for d in CHECK_DATES)}")
    for ioc_type, value, conf, until, src, ctx, tlp in FEED:
        until_d = date.fromisoformat(until)
        hits = sightings(con, ioc_type, value)
        rules = [n.split()[0] for n, text in hits_by_rule.items() if value in text]
        rel = SOURCES[src]
        cred, verdict = grade(rel, hits, rules, until_d >= AS_OF)
        print(f"\n{value}  [{ioc_type}, {src}, {tlp}]")
        print(f"  feed conf {conf}; sightings {'n/a' if hits is None else hits}; "
              f"rules {','.join(rules) or '-'}; grade {rel}{cred} -> {verdict}")
        ages = []
        for d in CHECK_DATES:
            left = (until_d - d).days
            ages.append(f"{d}: {'valid ' + str(left) + 'd' if left >= 0 else 'EXPIRED'}")
        print("  ageing  " + " | ".join(ages))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

How it works, part by part:

- **`SOURCES`** is your judgement of each source, written down where a reviewer can challenge it. ISAC-A, your own analysts ("internal") and the commercial vendor are given B: usually reliable, with a track record but not infallible. The open feed gets C: it aggregates unvetted submissions. Nobody receives A in this lab; reserve it for sources with a long, verified record.
- **`rule_hits()`** runs each of minisiem's six correlation rules (R1-R6) and flattens their result rows into text, so the script can ask "does any detection name this value?"
- **`sightings()`** counts events containing the value, using the same matching logic as `ioc_match.py`, and returns `None` for hash indicators because the dataset has no hash field.
- **`grade()`** turns those facts into a credibility digit and a verdict. A sighting that an independent detection also names is treated as confirmed (1): the rule fired on behaviour, without knowing about the feed. A sighting alone is "possibly true" (3). No telemetry of that type means "cannot be judged" (6). Expired indicators are LOW whatever their grade.
- **`CHECK_DATES`** drives the ageing line: the as-of date, the day you are writing the note (16 November 2026 in this lab) and the start of next year.

Run it:

```terminal

cdn-telemetry-sync.example  [domain, ISAC-A, TLP:AMBER]
  feed conf 85; sightings 18; rules R6; grade B1 -> HIGH
  ageing  2026-11-03: valid 58d | 2026-11-16: valid 45d | 2027-01-01: EXPIRED

banyan-payroll-update.example  [domain, internal, TLP:GREEN]
  feed conf 70; sightings 1; rules -; grade B3 -> MEDIUM
  ageing  2026-11-03: valid 27d | 2026-11-16: valid 14d | 2027-01-01: EXPIRED

203.0.113.66  [ipv4, ISAC-A, TLP:GREEN]
  feed conf 60; sightings 9; rules R2; grade B1 -> HIGH
  ageing  2026-11-03: valid 7d | 2026-11-16: EXPIRED | 2027-01-01: EXPIRED

198.51.100.104  [ipv4, open feed, TLP:CLEAR]
  feed conf 40; sightings 1; rules -; grade C3 -> LOW (expired)
  ageing  2026-11-03: EXPIRED | 2026-11-16: EXPIRED | 2027-01-01: EXPIRED

9f2c...e1a0  [sha256, vendor, TLP:AMBER]
  feed conf 90; sightings n/a; rules -; grade B6 -> UNGRADED (keep feed score)
  ageing  2026-11-03: valid 89d | 2026-11-16: valid 76d | 2027-01-01: valid 30d
```

**Interpreting the grades.** The beacon domain and the spray address both reach **B1 HIGH**: a usually reliable source, and your own behavioural rules (R6 for the beacon, R2 for the spray) independently fired on the same values. Note that the spray address scores HIGH although the feed gave it only 60; local corroboration has raised your confidence above the source's.

The look-alike domain stays at **B3 MEDIUM** because no rule row contains its name. Step 2 showed why: R1 detects Word starting PowerShell, and its output lists the host and time, not the URL. You know from the raw events that the download and the PowerShell launch happened on the same host two minutes apart. That supports upgrading the credibility to **2, probably true**, but it is your inference, not the script's. Record the override with its reason, for example "B3 upgraded to B2: same host, 06:12Z download of .docm, 06:14Z WINWORD spawns PowerShell (R1)". Do not edit the script to make the number come out higher.

The old address is **C3 LOW (expired)**: a fair source, a single unremarkable sighting and an expired validity date. The hash is **B6 UNGRADED**: a usually reliable vendor, but you cannot judge the claim because you have no hash telemetry. The script keeps the feed's 90 rather than inventing a grade.

> [!TIP]
> Grading is not a one-off. When a new rule hit or a new sighting arrives, rerun the script. Credibility should move with the evidence; reliability should move only when the source's track record changes.

### Step 4: Age the indicators and decide

The ageing line is where the as-of date of the incident meets the date on which you are acting. Indicators decay: attackers move infrastructure, addresses are reassigned and domains lapse. The `valid_until` field is the source's estimate of how long the indicator stays useful, and a SOC should respect it in its blocking and alerting while keeping expired items available for searches of historical data.

Read across each ageing line and decide:

- **`cdn-telemetry-sync.example`**: valid until 31 December 2026, 45 days left on 16 November. Keep it in blocking and alerting. Search retained proxy logs back to the start of the retention period for earlier contacts from other hosts.
- **`banyan-payroll-update.example`**: valid 14 more days. Keep blocking; because it is your own first-party indicator, you may extend its validity yourself if the domain is still live when it expires.
- **`203.0.113.66`**: valid on the incident day but **expired on 16 November**: its validity ended on 10 November. An IP address used for password spraying is often a rented or compromised machine that changes hands, so a short life is reasonable. Retire it from blocking and alerting, keep it for retrospective searches and in the case record, and do not share it as a live block recommendation.
- **`198.51.100.104`**: expired before the incident. No action on the strength of this indicator. Tell the open feed's maintainers that it is stale if their process allows feedback, and keep the 13:33Z session as a question in the case.
- **`9f2c...e1a0`**: valid into January, but untestable. The action is not about the indicator but about your telemetry: endpoint file-hash logging (for example Sysmon process events with hashes) would let you test it. Record "no match" as "no data", never as "clean".

### Step 5: Derive first-party indicators

The incident did more than confirm feed indicators. It produced new ones that no feed holds yet, and those are the most valuable things you can share. Look at the raw events behind the R3 and R4 hits:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT url, COUNT(*) AS hits, COUNT(DISTINCT host) AS hosts, MIN(ts) AS first, MAX(ts) AS last FROM events WHERE url LIKE '%://files-share-drop.example/%' OR url LIKE '%://banyan-payroll-update.example/%' GROUP BY url"
url | hits | hosts | first | last
https://banyan-payroll-update.example/invoice.docm | 1 | 1 | 2026-11-03T06:12:00Z | 2026-11-03T06:12:00Z
https://files-share-drop.example/upload | 1 | 1 | 2026-11-03T08:12:00Z | 2026-11-03T08:12:00Z
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, event_id, target_user FROM events WHERE target_user IS NOT NULL"
ts | event_id | target_user
2026-11-03T07:02:00Z | 4720 | svc_update
2026-11-03T07:03:00Z | 4732 | svc_update
```

Two new indicators emerge. **`files-share-drop.example`** received one upload from one host; `minisiem.py rules` reports it under R4 with `bytes_out` of 48,200,000, against ordinary proxy requests of a few thousand bytes. **`svc_update`** is the name of the local account created (event 4720) and added to a local group (event 4732; the raw event names the group as Administrators) on the compromised workstation. An account name is a weak indicator on its own, because attackers choose names that look routine, but it is a useful hunting lead for peers who search their own 4720 events.

Give each a validity period. These are your own decisions, so write them down with the reasoning: 30 days for the upload domain (until 3 December 2026), after which you re-check whether it still resolves; no expiry for the account name as a hunting lead, since it describes past behaviour rather than live infrastructure.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The table is the decision record for the feed after the lab. It joins what the script computed (sightings, rule names, grade, validity) to what the analyst decided, so that a reviewer or the next shift can see why each indicator is being blocked, retired, ignored or used to justify a telemetry change. Five rows, one per feed indicator, each with a coloured marker: red for `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, orange for `banyan-payroll-update.example`, violet for `203.0.113.66`, slate for `198.51.100.104` and blue for the truncated hash `9f2c...e1a0 (sha256)`.

**Relationships.** Read each row from left to right as an argument. For the beacon domain, 18 sightings plus rule R6 give B1 HIGH; it is still valid with 45 days left; so the decision is "Block, hunt; AMBER sighting". For the look-alike domain, one sighting and no rule give B3 MEDIUM, raised by the analyst to B2 with the reason recorded in Step 3; valid for 14 days; "Block; share first-party". For the spray address, 9 sightings plus R2 give B1 HIGH, but it is **expired**, so the decision is "Retire block; keep for retro". The old address is C3 LOW and expired: "No action; flag as stale". The hash has no telemetry ("n/a / none"), grade B6, valid 76 days: "Close the telemetry gap".

**Execution flow.** The left four columns came from commands in Steps 1 to 4, the right column from your judgement. The note under the table says this explicitly: the script supplies grades and ages, and the analyst supplies decisions with reasons.

**Decision logic.** Two rows show the most important distinctions. The spray address is the strongest-graded item in the table and yet it is retired, because confidence and validity are different questions: a HIGH grade says the indicator *was* attacker infrastructure, the expiry says it may no longer be. The hash is the opposite: a high feed confidence that you cannot test, which makes its row a to-do for your telemetry rather than for your blocklist.

**Technical interpretation.** Grade, validity and TLP are three independent fields. The table shows two of them; the third, TLP, does not appear because it does not change the decision to block, only the decision about who may be told. That is why the beacon domain's decision mentions an AMBER sighting rather than the peer note.

**Practical example.** Imagine a teammate asks why the spray address was removed from the VPN blocklist "even though it hit nine times". The row answers in one line: graded HIGH, expired on 10 November, kept for retrospective searches.

**Security implications.** Indicators that never expire fill blocklists with addresses that have changed hands, and eventually block legitimate users; indicators that expire silently lose history. Recording the retirement decision avoids both.

**Key learning.** Grade tells you how much to believe an indicator, age tells you whether it is still worth acting on, and the label tells you who you may tell. Keep the three apart.

### Step 7: Draft the note and run the pre-share check

Write a first draft as an analyst often does, straight from the case notes. Save it as `note_draft.txt`:

```text
TLP:GREEN  Banyan SOC intel note 2026-11-03-01
Phishing lure hosted at banyan-payroll-update.example/invoice.docm opened by divya on WS-104
at 06:12Z; WINWORD.EXE started hidden PowerShell at 06:14Z.
Beacon every 300 s from 10.40.0.23 to cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping.
Local account svc_update created and added to Administrators; 48,200,000 bytes
uploaded to files-share-drop.example/upload; Security log cleared at 08:17Z.
VPN password spray from 203.0.113.66: 8 accounts in 49 s, then success for gopal.
```

Now create the checker, `scrub_check.py`:

```python
"""Pre-share check: flag internal identifiers left in an outbound intel note.

Usage: python scrub_check.py note.txt
Looks for dataset user names, internal host names and private 10.x addresses.
"""
import re
import sqlite3
import sys

from minisiem import DB

con = sqlite3.connect(DB)
users = {u for (u,) in con.execute("SELECT DISTINCT user FROM events") if u}
text = open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8").read()
findings = []
for n, line in enumerate(text.splitlines(), 1):
    for word in re.findall(r"[A-Za-z_][A-Za-z0-9_.-]*", line):
        if word.rstrip(".-").lower() in users:
            findings.append((n, "user name", word.rstrip(".-")))
    for host in re.findall(r"\bWS-\d+\b", line):
        findings.append((n, "internal host", host))
    for ip in re.findall(r"\b10\.\d+\.\d+\.\d+\b", line):
        findings.append((n, "internal IP", ip))
for n, kind, val in findings:
    print(f"line {n:3}: {kind:13} {val}")
print(f"{len(findings)} finding(s) in {sys.argv[1]}" + ("" if findings else " - clear to review for release"))
```

The user list comes from the dataset itself (`SELECT DISTINCT user FROM events`), so the checker knows Banyan's staff without you typing their names. Words are compared after stripping a trailing full stop or hyphen, because a name at the end of a sentence ("for gopal.") would otherwise slip past; an early version of this checker missed exactly that case, which is a good reminder that pattern checks have blind spots. Host names follow Banyan's `WS-` convention, and internal addresses are its `10.` range. In a real SOC you would load these lists from the CMDB and the identity provider.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause and fix |
| --- | --- |
| `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'ioc_match'` | Your script is not in the same folder as the kit files. Move it there and run from that folder. |
| `sqlite3.OperationalError: no such table: events` | The database has not been built in this folder. Run `python minisiem.py load`. |
| `loaded` count is not 340 | The dataset was modified or regenerated. Re-copy the kit. |
| Grade shows no rules for the beacon domain | `RULES` was edited, or `minisiem.db` is stale. Reload and rerun `python minisiem.py rules`. |
| Ageing line differs from the lesson | You changed `CHECK_DATES`. The lesson uses 2026-11-03, 2026-11-16 and 2027-01-01. |
| `scrub_check.py` misses a name | The name is not in the dataset's `user` field, or is joined to other characters. Read every note yourself as well. |
| `no such column` in a search | The column is not one of minisiem's normalised fields (for example `status`); query the `raw` column instead. |

## Key takeaways

- A feed match is the start of the work: verify each sighting in raw events before you trust or share it.
- Grade with two independent parts: source reliability (a property of the source) and credibility (a property of the claim, raised by your own independent detections).
- Ageing is separate from confidence: a HIGH-graded indicator can be expired and should then leave blocking while staying available for retrospective searches.
- "No match" on an indicator type you do not collect is a telemetry gap, not a clean result.
- Your incidents produce first-party indicators that no feed holds; they, plus techniques and detection ideas, are the most valuable things to share.
- Run a pre-share check for internal identifiers, read the note yourself, keep restricted items out of wider releases, and send sightings back to the source under its own label.
