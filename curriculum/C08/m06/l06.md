## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Threat intelligence is evidence about adversaries, and like all evidence it has a scope, an age and a source, each of which limits what it can prove. The previous lessons treated intelligence as something to collect, score and match. This lesson asks the opposite question: **when an indicator matches, or fails to match, what does that actually tell you, and what does it not?**

The question matters because intelligence is persuasive. A match against a feed arrives with a label such as "malicious", a confidence number and often the name of a threat actor, and it is tempting to read that label as a verdict about your own environment. It is not. A feed entry is a statement by someone else, about activity they saw, at some time in the past, through their own telemetry. A match tells you only that a value in your logs equals a value in that statement. Everything else (whether the activity in your logs is malicious, whether it is the same actor, whether you are compromised) is an inference that the analyst must make, and which the match alone cannot make for you.

Experienced SOC analysts hold two ideas at once. Intelligence is enormously useful: in the lab dataset of this course, the ISAC indicators turned a handful of ambiguous events into a confirmed phishing, beaconing and password-spraying case within minutes. And intelligence is routinely wrong for your environment: the same feed matched an ordinary employee's VPN session through an address that had been listed months before. The skill is telling the two situations apart, and saying how sure you are.

The limitations fall into a small number of families, which this lesson takes in turn:

1. **Perishability:** indicators go stale as infrastructure is abandoned and reassigned.
2. **Shared infrastructure:** one address or domain can serve thousands of unrelated users.
3. **Absence of evidence:** "no match" is often "no data".
4. **Circular reporting:** feeds copy each other, so one observation can look like many.
5. **Attribution limits:** naming who is behind activity is hard, contested and usually unnecessary for defence.
6. **Bias:** analysts and producers see what they expect, and vendors have incentives.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> An indicator match is a lead, not a finding. It becomes a finding only when the events it points to make sense in your own context, and the conclusion you write should say how confident you are and why.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Stale indicator | An indicator that no longer reliably identifies malicious activity because the infrastructure has been abandoned, reassigned or changed. |
| Expired indicator | An indicator whose stated validity period (`valid_until`) has passed; a formal marker that it should no longer drive action. |
| Shared infrastructure | Addresses, domains or services used by many unrelated parties at once: cloud and CDN ranges, shared web hosting, VPN providers, Tor exit relays, mobile carrier gateways. |
| Collateral match | A match caused by legitimate activity that happens to use the same shared value as listed malicious activity. |
| Circular reporting | The same original observation reaching an analyst through several sources that copied it, so that it falsely appears independently confirmed. |
| Attribution | Assigning responsibility for activity to a named actor, group or state. |
| Cognitive bias | A systematic error in judgement, such as confirmation bias (favouring evidence that fits an existing belief) or anchoring (over-weighting the first information received). |
| Estimative language | Standardised wording ("low", "moderate", "high confidence") that tells the reader how strongly the analyst holds a judgement. |

## Perishability: stale and expired indicators

An indicator describes infrastructure at a moment in time, and attackers change infrastructure constantly. Some rotate domains and addresses per campaign, some per victim. Cheap cloud servers are rented for hours and returned; the address then goes back into the provider's pool and is handed to the next customer, who may be a bakery, a university or your own payroll provider. Domains expire and are re-registered by someone else, sometimes by a security researcher who sinkholes them, so that connections to the "malicious" domain are now connections to a defender's server. A file hash cannot be reassigned, but it decays in a different way: the attacker recompiles or changes one byte, the old hash stops matching anything new, and the feed keeps it forever.

Two terms are easily confused. An indicator is **expired** when its producer's stated validity has passed; that is a formal, checkable property, and Lesson 5 showed how the lab matcher evaluates it against a date. An indicator is **stale** when it no longer reliably identifies malicious activity, whatever its label says. Many stale indicators have no expiry at all, because the producer never set one; and an indicator can expire while it is still correct, as the lab's spraying address showed when the date moved to 15 November. Expiry is a policy tool for removing indicators from automatic action. Staleness is a judgement the analyst makes about a specific match.

The practical consequence is that the age and validity of an indicator must be visible at the moment an analyst reads a match. A match line that says only "malicious IP" invites the wrong action. The lab matcher prints confidence, source and TLP on every line, and puts `EXPIRED` in the status column where nobody can miss it.

## Shared infrastructure and collateral matches

Most internet infrastructure is shared. A single cloud address may host many customers' services over a year; a single CDN edge address serves thousands of websites at the same moment; a shared web-hosting server holds hundreds of small sites, one of which may be compromised and used for phishing; commercial VPN services and Tor exit relays carry traffic for large numbers of users, a few of whom misbehave; mobile carriers place many subscribers behind a small number of public addresses using carrier-grade NAT. When any of these addresses appears in a feed, every legitimate user of the same infrastructure becomes a potential collateral match.

Shared infrastructure explains why IP addresses and domains sit low on the Pyramid of Pain from Lesson 1, and why they produce most false positives in indicator matching. It also explains why the context field matters so much. An entry that says "scanning (old, shared hosting)" is warning you, in its own words, that the address was shared and the observation was old: two separate reasons to doubt any match.

Defences against collateral matches are partly technical and partly analytical. Technically, the allowlist from Lesson 5 removes known shared ranges from automatic action, and IP indicators should carry shorter lifetimes than domains and hashes. Analytically, an analyst seeing an IP match should ask what kind of address it is (cloud, hosting, VPN, residential, mobile) before asking what it did, and should look for corroboration from a different kind of evidence: a process, a user behaviour, a second indicator that is not on the same infrastructure.

## Worked example: an expired indicator matching routine activity

The lab kit contains a deliberately awkward match. In a temporary copy of `LAB_ASSETS/C08` (never inside the course library), load the dataset and run the matcher:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python ioc_match.py
MATCH    domain cdn-telemetry-sync.example       conf= 85 hits= 18 first=2026-11-03T06:17:00Z src=ISAC-A TLP:AMBER
MATCH    domain banyan-payroll-update.example    conf= 70 hits=  1 first=2026-11-03T06:12:00Z src=internal TLP:GREEN
MATCH    ipv4   203.0.113.66                     conf= 60 hits=  9 first=2026-11-03T06:42:00Z src=ISAC-A TLP:GREEN
EXPIRED  ipv4   198.51.100.104                   conf= 40 hits=  1 first=2026-11-03T13:33:00Z src=open feed TLP:CLEAR
         expired indicator still matches: review before acting
no match sha256 9f2c...e1a0                      conf= 90 hits=  0 first=- src=vendor TLP:AMBER
```

The output is identical to the kit's saved `ioc_match_output.txt`. Focus on the fourth line. The feed entry behind it, in `ioc_match.py`, reads:

```python
("ipv4", "198.51.100.104", 40, "2026-06-30", "open feed",
 "scanning (old, shared hosting)", "TLP:CLEAR"),
```

Everything about the indicator argues for caution: an open feed, confidence 40 out of 100, validity that ended on 30 June 2026, more than four months before the lab date, and a context string that itself says the observation is old and the address is shared hosting. The matcher has already done its part, labelling the line `EXPIRED` and printing the warning instead of treating it as a detection.

Now look at the event it matched. The kit's `minisiem.py search` command runs one read-only SQL query against the loaded table and prints the columns and rows:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, user, src_ip, outcome FROM events WHERE source='vpn' AND src_ip LIKE '198.51.100.%' ORDER BY ts"
ts | user | src_ip | outcome
2026-11-03T13:33:00Z | gopal | 198.51.100.104 | success
```

One successful VPN session for the user gopal at 13:33 UTC. The raw event in `banyan_events.jsonl` adds one more field, `"country": "IN"`, consistent with an employee of an Indian microfinance company connecting from home. The dataset generator, `make_dataset.py`, confirms what this event is. In its "Routine activity" section, each user has a chance of an evening VPN session from a random address in the documentation range:

```python
if random.random() < 0.4:
    t = start + timedelta(hours=10)
    add(t, source="vpn", user=u, src_ip=f"198.51.100.{random.randint(10, 200)}",
        outcome="success", country="IN")
```

So the match is a collateral match: ordinary activity that happens to come from an address an open feed listed months earlier for something unrelated. In a real SOC you would not have the generator's source code, but you would have equivalent evidence: the user's normal working pattern, the address's ownership and type, and the absence of any other suspicious behaviour linked to that session.

### The right action, for the right reason

Acting on this indicator, for example by disabling gopal's account or blocking the address because "threat intelligence flagged it", would be wrong. It would disrupt a legitimate user on the strength of stale, low-confidence, shared-infrastructure evidence, and it would teach the business that the SOC's alerts are unreliable.

There is a twist that makes this example worth studying. Query all of gopal's VPN activity:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, source, src_ip, outcome FROM events WHERE user='gopal' AND source='vpn' ORDER BY ts"
ts | source | src_ip | outcome
2026-11-03T06:42:42Z | vpn | 203.0.113.66 | failure
2026-11-03T06:46:00Z | vpn | 203.0.113.66 | success
2026-11-03T13:33:00Z | vpn | 198.51.100.104 | success
```

Earlier the same morning, gopal's account was one of the targets of the password spray from `203.0.113.66`, and the spraying address logged in successfully as gopal at 06:46. That is exactly what rule R2 (`vpn_spray_then_success`) in `minisiem.py` detects, and it is supported by a current ISAC indicator whose context ("password spraying against VPNs") fits exactly. gopal's account therefore does need action: a credential reset, a review of what the 06:46 session did, and a check of the 13:33 session as part of that case. The expired indicator plays no part in that decision. **The account is at risk because of the spray, not because of the stale address.** An analyst who disabled the account "because of 198.51.100.104" would have done roughly the right thing for the wrong reason, and would write a case record that misleads everyone who reads it later.

**Decision logic.** From **Events fit the context?**, "yes" leads to **Act proportionately** and "no" leads to **Record collateral match**. No path reaches action without passing that question.

**Technical interpretation.** The first two questions can be partly automated (validity checks, warning lists of shared ranges); the third needs an analyst with access to the surrounding telemetry and asset context.

**Practical example.** The `198.51.100.104` match takes the "no" branch at **Indicator current?**, then the dashed arrow: the event is gopal's routine evening VPN session, so the answer at **Events fit the context?** is "no" and the outcome is **Record collateral match**, with a note to the open-feed producer if a feedback channel exists. The `203.0.113.66` match on 3 November follows "yes", "no", "yes" to **Act proportionately**: eight failures and one success fit the ISAC context exactly.

**Security implications.** Skipping the first two questions produces false accusations against users; skipping the third lets a current, plausible indicator drive action against activity that does not fit.

**Key learning.** Judge the indicator, then the infrastructure, then your own events; only the events can justify action.

## Absence of evidence: "no match" is not "clean"

The last line of the matcher output is as instructive as the expired one. The vendor's SHA-256 indicator for the malicious `invoice.docm` has the highest confidence in the feed (90) and reports `no match`. Read the code and the reason is plain: for hash indicators the matcher sets `rows = []`, with the comment that the dataset has no file hashes. The SOC never collected the field, so the indicator could not have matched whatever happened. Yet the same dataset shows divya downloading `invoice.docm` from the look-alike domain at 06:12, and Word spawning PowerShell on WS-104 two minutes later. The file was almost certainly present; the telemetry simply could not see its hash.

A match can only occur where three things line up: the adversary used the indicator against you, your telemetry recorded the field it lives in, and your matcher compared that field with the feed. A "no match" result therefore has at least three explanations: the activity did not happen, it happened but was not logged in a form you can match, or it happened with different infrastructure that no feed has listed yet. The last is the normal case for a targeted attack: an adversary who builds fresh infrastructure for each victim will never appear in anyone's feed before they reach you. This is why the module keeps returning to behaviour-based detection. Rule R1 (Office spawning a shell) fired on this intrusion without any intelligence at all, and it would still fire if the attacker changed every domain and file.

## Circular reporting and feed overlap

Feeds and reports copy each other. A researcher publishes a blog post with ten indicators; an open feed scrapes them; two commercial vendors include the open feed in their products; an ISAC member forwards the vendor's alert to the community; a national advisory cites the ISAC. Your platform now shows the same address from five sources and the score climbs, although there is still exactly one original observation behind it, and nobody downstream re-checked it. If the original was wrong, or was an address of shared hosting, five sources agree on the error.

Defend against circular reporting in three ways. Record provenance, not only source: where possible note the original report each feed entry derives from. Merge duplicates without summing confidence (Lesson 5). And treat agreement between sources as strong only when they observed the activity independently, for example your own proxy log and a different organisation's incident.

## Attribution: who is behind it, and does it matter?

Attribution means naming the actor responsible for activity. Intelligence reports often lead with it, and it can be useful at the strategic level: knowing that a group with a history of targeting financial services is active in your region helps leaders prioritise investment. For the analyst handling a case, attribution is limited in three ways.

**It is hard and uncertain.** Infrastructure is rented and shared, tools are sold, leaked and reused, and capable actors sometimes plant misleading clues. Reliable attribution usually needs evidence that a SOC does not have, and public attributions are often made with stated or unstated uncertainty.

**Names do not line up.** Each vendor names the groups it tracks in its own scheme, and their boundaries differ. One well-known group is called APT29, Cozy Bear or Midnight Blizzard depending on whose report you read, and the groups behind those names do not necessarily overlap completely. MITRE ATT&CK group pages list associated names reported by others, which helps translate between reports, but a shared name is a claim about overlap, not proof of identity.

**It is rarely needed for defence.** Whether the spray from `203.0.113.66` was run by a named group or by an opportunist, the response is the same: reset the credential, review the session, require multi-factor authentication for VPN access, and block the address while it is current. Act on behaviour and evidence; leave attribution to those whose decisions depend on it, and never state it in a case record more firmly than the evidence allows.

## Bias in producers and analysts

Intelligence passes through people twice: the producer who wrote it and the analyst who reads it. Both are subject to cognitive bias. **Confirmation bias** leads an analyst who already suspects an incident to read every match as support and to explain away the expired flag. **Anchoring** gives undue weight to the first piece of information, such as an alert title naming an actor. **Availability bias** makes a dramatic recent breach in the news seem more likely to be behind today's alert than it is. Producers have their own pressures: vendors compete for attention, and a report that names a sophisticated actor attracts more of it than one describing an ordinary password spray. A feed's selection of what to publish also reflects what that producer can see, which may be very different from your sector and region.

Structured techniques help. Richards Heuer's *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, published by the CIA's Center for the Study of Intelligence, describes **Analysis of Competing Hypotheses**: list every plausible explanation, list the evidence, and look for the evidence that is inconsistent with each hypothesis rather than the evidence that fits your favourite. Applied to the `198.51.100.104` match, the hypotheses "attacker reusing old infrastructure" and "employee working from home" both fit the bare match; only the second is consistent with the session timing, the country, the user's normal pattern and the absence of any other suspicious activity from that address. A simple **key assumptions check** works too: write down what you are assuming (for example "the feed entry is still accurate") and ask what would happen if it were false.

**Purpose.** The figure separates the questions threat intelligence can help answer from those only your own evidence can answer. It matters because most misuse of intelligence comes from asking a feed a question from the right-hand column.

**Components.** Two columns are separated by "vs". The left, green column is titled **Intelligence can tell you** and lists: "what others saw, where and when"; "context: C2, spraying, phishing"; "the producer's confidence and validity"; "which values to hunt for first"; "TTPs worth detecting". The right, red column is titled **It cannot tell you** and lists: "whether you are compromised"; "that no match means clean"; "that an old value is still bad"; "who is really behind it"; "what your own users were doing".

**Relationships.** The "vs" between the columns signals that each item on the right is the limit of the matching item on the left. Intelligence says what others saw; it cannot say whether you are compromised. It supplies confidence and validity; it cannot say that an old value is still bad. It tells you which values to hunt for; it cannot promise that a hunt with no results means you are clean.

**Execution flow.** In practice an analyst moves from left to right: start from what intelligence offers (a list of values to look for, with context), then answer the right-hand questions with your own telemetry, asset knowledge and user context.

**Decision logic.** If a question you are asking appears on the right, intelligence alone cannot decide it, and any case note that answers it must cite evidence from your own environment.

**Technical interpretation.** Left-column items are properties of the feed entry: its pattern, context string, confidence, `valid_until` and associated TTPs. Right-column items are properties of your environment and of the world beyond any one producer's view, which no feed can encode.

**Practical example.** For the lab case, the left column supplies "`203.0.113.66` was used for VPN password spraying, confidence 60, valid until 10 November" and "`198.51.100.104` was seen scanning on shared hosting, confidence 40, expired". The right column is answered from the logs: gopal's 06:46 session came from the spray, the 13:33 session was routine, and the missing hash match reflects a telemetry gap, not a clean host.

**Security implications.** Over-trusting intelligence causes false accusations and outages; under-using it wastes the speed it offers. Keeping the two columns distinct prevents both.

**Key learning.** Intelligence tells you what to look for and how much others trust it; only your own evidence tells you what happened to you.

## Stating confidence in your own conclusions

Because intelligence is uncertain, conclusions built on it must say how uncertain they are. Use a fixed vocabulary, such as low, moderate and high confidence, defined once in your SOC's procedures, and give the reasons in the same sentence. For the lab case a well-formed set of judgements reads:

- High confidence that gopal's VPN credential was compromised by password spraying: eight failed logins across all users from `203.0.113.66` within a minute, followed by a successful login as gopal, matching a current ISAC indicator with the same context.
- High confidence that the `198.51.100.104` match is collateral: expired, low-confidence open-feed entry on shared hosting, matching a routine session consistent with the user's normal behaviour.
- Moderate confidence that the spray and the WS-104 phishing are the same actor: they share timing on the same morning but no infrastructure.
- No assessment of attribution: the evidence does not support one.

Each sentence separates what was observed from what was inferred, and a reader can see exactly what would change the judgement.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"The feed says malicious, so it is."** A feed entry is someone else's past observation; it needs your own evidence before it becomes a finding.
- **Treating expiry and staleness as the same.** Expired indicators can be historically correct; unexpired ones can be stale. Judge each match.
- **Counting sources as confirmations.** Five feeds repeating one blog post are one observation.
- **Reading "no match" as "clean".** Check that your telemetry records the indicator's field before drawing any conclusion.
- **Leading with attribution.** Naming an actor adds little to the response and much risk to the case record if it is wrong.
- **Doing the right thing for the wrong reason.** Record the evidence that actually justifies an action, as with gopal's account and the spray.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Intelligence limitations become operational risks when indicators drive automation. Collateral matches on shared infrastructure lead to outages and to users being locked out; stale indicators produce alert fatigue until analysts stop reading intelligence matches at all; an empty match list creates false assurance. The defences are those of this module combined: expiry evaluated at match time, allowlists of shared infrastructure, human review before blocking, behaviour-based detections alongside indicators, telemetry coverage checks for each indicator type you consume, and explicit confidence in every written judgement. Measure the result: track the share of intelligence matches closed as collateral, by feed and by indicator type, and use it at the next feed review.

> [!CAUTION]
> Even a collateral match involves real people. A case record that says a named employee "matched a malicious IP" can follow them long after the match is shown to be stale. Write the conclusion precisely, and correct the record when the assessment changes.

## Key takeaways

- An indicator match is a lead: it shows that a value matched someone else's observation, not that you are compromised.
- Indicators perish; expiry is a policy marker, staleness a judgement, and they do not always coincide.
- Shared infrastructure produces collateral matches; ask what kind of address it is before asking what it did.
- "No match" often means "no data" or "new infrastructure"; behaviour-based detection covers what feeds cannot.
- Circular reporting, contested attribution and bias distort intelligence; record provenance, act on behaviour, and state confidence with reasons.
- In the lab, the expired open-feed match on gopal's routine session must not drive action; his account needs action because of the spray, and the case record should say so.
