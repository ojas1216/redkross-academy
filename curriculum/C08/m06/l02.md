## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every line that `ioc_match.py` prints carries a number: `conf= 85` for the C2 domain, `conf= 70` for the look-alike domain, `conf= 60` for the VPN-spraying address, `conf= 40` for the old open-feed address and `conf= 90` for the vendor's file hash. An analyst who reads those numbers as "85 per cent likely to be malicious" will make bad decisions, because the number does not say that. It says how sure *the producer* was, on *their* scale, about *their* claim, *when they made it*. It says nothing about whether the producer is usually right, whether anyone else has confirmed the claim, how old the claim is or whether it applies to your network.

Confidence is the property of intelligence that tells a consumer how much weight a piece of information can bear. Lesson 1 showed that an indicator's *type* predicts how long it stays useful and how easily the adversary can change it. This lesson adds the second half of the judgement: for a particular indicator from a particular source, how much do we believe it, and how should that belief change over time? Three ideas carry the lesson:

1. **Grade the source and the information separately.** The long-established Admiralty system (also called the NATO system) gives every report two grades: a letter for the reliability of the source and a number for the credibility of the information itself. A reliable source can pass on a doubtful report; an unknown source can report something you can confirm yourself.
2. **Turn grades into a local score you can act on.** Platforms and standards express confidence as a number (STIX 2.1 uses an integer from 0 to 100). A SOC needs its own score that combines the producer's confidence with its own grading and its own sightings, and thresholds that tie the score to actions: block, alert, or keep as context only.
3. **Let confidence age.** A claim that was well founded a month ago may be wrong today. The lab feed uses a hard `valid_until` cut-off; many platforms also decay scores gradually. Either way, age must lower the weight of a match.

Module 5 lesson 3 covered how enrichment uses an intelligence match during triage. This lesson is about the intelligence itself: where the confidence number comes from, how to grade it, and how to keep it honest.

> [!NOTE]
> The lab feed carries a single producer confidence (0-100) per indicator and no Admiralty grades. Where this lesson assigns Admiralty grades to feed entries, they are the course's worked judgements, made to illustrate the method; they are not part of the kit's data.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Confidence | How much weight an assessment or a piece of information can bear, given the evidence and sources behind it. Not the same as likelihood or severity. |
| Source reliability | A judgement of the *source's* track record and access: how often its past reporting proved correct. Graded A-F in the Admiralty system. |
| Information credibility | A judgement of *this item* of information: whether it is confirmed, consistent and plausible. Graded 1-6 in the Admiralty system. |
| Admiralty (NATO) system | A two-part grading such as "B2": source reliability letter plus information credibility number, judged independently. |
| Producer confidence | The confidence the originator attached to an indicator, for example `conf= 85` in the lab feed or the STIX `confidence` property. |
| Local score | The consumer's own working confidence, combining producer confidence, grading, local sightings and age. |
| Corroboration | Independent confirmation of a claim by a different source or by your own data. Copies of the same report are not corroboration. |
| Circular reporting | One original report reaching you through several channels and looking like several independent confirmations. |
| Ageing (decay) | Reducing an indicator's weight as time passes since it was last confirmed. |
| Hard expiry | Treating an indicator as inactive after a fixed `valid_until` date, as `ioc_match.py` does. |
| Sighting | A recorded observation of an indicator in your own (or a partner's) data, with time and context. |

## Why one number is not enough

Consider two reports about the same domain. One comes from a sector information-sharing group whose reports have been right for years, but this report is second-hand: "a member saw it in an incident". The other comes from an anonymous paste, but it includes the domain's registration date and a URL path, both of which you can verify yourself in minutes. If you collapse each into one confidence number you lose exactly the information you need: the first is a good source with unconfirmed information, the second a poor source with information you can confirm. They call for different actions (the first: look for corroboration; the second: verify and, if confirmed, the source matters much less).

This is why intelligence practice separates the two questions. The separation is old - it comes from military and naval intelligence, which is where the "Admiralty" name comes from - and it remains in use in law-enforcement and cyber-intelligence work because it forces the grader to think about the source and the content separately. Its weakness, discussed later, is that it is subjective and that grades from different organisations are not calibrated against each other.

## The Admiralty grading system

The Admiralty system gives each report a letter and a number. The letter grades the source; the number grades the information. Each axis has a final value meaning "cannot be judged", which is not a bad grade but an honest statement that there is no basis for one.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The table sets out the two independent axes of the Admiralty system side by side, so a grader can pick one value from each column and combine them into a two-character grade such as **B2**. It matters because it stops "how good is the source?" being confused with "how good is this claim?".

**Components.** There are four columns: **Code** and **Source reliability** on the left, **Code** and **Information credibility** on the right. There are six rows. Source reliability runs **A Completely reliable**, **B Usually reliable**, **C Fairly reliable**, **D Not usually reliable**, **E Unreliable**, **F Reliability cannot be judged**. Information credibility runs **1 Confirmed by other sources**, **2 Probably true**, **3 Possibly true**, **4 Doubtful**, **5 Improbable**, **6 Truth cannot be judged**. A coloured strip at the left edge of each row moves from green for the A row, through blue, violet and orange, to red for E and slate for the "cannot be judged" row.

**Relationships.** The two halves of each row share a row only because they share a position in their scales; there is no link between them. A grade is made by choosing a row on the left and a row on the right independently. **A5** (a completely reliable source passing on an improbable claim) and **F1** (an unknown source whose claim is confirmed elsewhere) are both legitimate grades.

**Execution flow.** Grade the source first, from its history and its access: has it been right before, and could it plausibly know this? Then grade the information on its own merits: is it confirmed by an independent source, consistent with what you know, plausible? Write the combined grade next to the indicator, with a line of reasoning.

**Decision logic.** The bottom row is special on both axes. **F** is for a new or unassessed source, not a bad one; **6** is for a claim with no way of checking it, not a false one. Treat both as "unknown" and seek more evidence rather than as "low".

**Technical interpretation.** The grades are ordinal labels, not measurements. "B" is better than "C", but there is no defined distance between them, and averaging grades is meaningless. Converting them to numbers is a local policy choice that must be written down.

**Practical example.** In the Banyan case, the internal team's note on `banyan-payroll-update.example` (registered on 29 October) could be graded **B1**: an internal source with a good record, and a registration date anyone can check. The open-feed address `198.51.100.104` fits **F3** or worse: an open feed with no known track record, carrying a claim ("scanning, old, shared hosting") that was possibly true once and cannot be confirmed now.

**Security implications.** Inflated source grades are a common failure: a well-known vendor or group is graded A by habit. Reserve A for sources with a long, checked record, and record why each grade was given so it can be challenged.

**Key learning.** Always grade the source and the information separately; a reliable source can carry a doubtful claim and an unknown source can carry a confirmed one.

## Grading in practice

Grading is a judgement, and it is only useful if it is made consistently and written down. A few working rules keep it honest.

**Source reliability is earned from history and access.** A source's grade should rest on how often its past reports were confirmed, and on whether it could plausibly have the access it claims. A sector group that passes on members' incident findings has good access to the sector's attacks; an aggregator that re-publishes other feeds has no access of its own and should not inherit its upstream sources' grades. Internal sources (your own SOC, your own incident reports) often deserve a high grade for claims about your own environment and a lower one for claims about the wider world.

**Information credibility is about this claim, now.** A claim is graded **1** only when an *independent* source confirms it. Your own telemetry can be that source: if the feed says a domain is C2 and your own hunt finds a machine-regular beacon to it, the claim is confirmed in your environment. Consistency with what you already know and technical plausibility support grades **2** and **3**. A claim that conflicts with solid evidence moves towards **4** or **5**.

**Beware circular reporting.** The same original report often arrives through a vendor feed, an open feed and a sharing group within a day. Three copies of one report are one report. Before grading anything **1**, trace whether the "other sources" share an origin. Many platforms record the original producer of an indicator; use that field, not the number of feeds that carry it.

**Record the reasoning.** A grade without a sentence of justification cannot be reviewed. "B2: ISAC-A, accurate over the past year; claim consistent with sector phishing reports, not yet seen locally" is reviewable; "B2" alone is not.

Two points stand out. Second, local corroboration upgraded two indicators to **1**. That upgrade is the most valuable thing a SOC adds to intelligence it receives: it turns "someone says" into "we see".

## From grades to a working score

Analysts can reason with letters and numbers; SIEM rules and blocking lists cannot. Most platforms therefore carry a numeric confidence. STIX 2.1, the common exchange format, defines an optional `confidence` property as an integer from 0 to 100. The lab feed follows that idea with its `conf` column.

The producer's number is an input, not the answer. A SOC needs a **local score** with a written policy for how it is built and what each range allows. The policy below is a course illustration of the shape such a policy takes; real teams tune the numbers to their own risk appetite and history:

- Start from the producer confidence.
- Adjust for the source grade: no change for **B**, downwards for **C** or worse, and treat **F** as at most 40 until a track record exists.
- Add for each *independent* local corroboration, such as a matching detection or hunt finding (for example +10 each, capped at 100).
- Apply ageing (next section).
- Map the result to actions, combined with Lesson 1's type rules: **80 and above** may drive automatic blocking for types where blocking is safe; **50-79** raises an alert for analyst review; **below 50** is context only, shown during enrichment but never alerting or blocking on its own.

Applied to the lab feed on 3 November, this policy gives `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` 85 + 10 = 95 (block-eligible; it is not a shared platform), `banyan-payroll-update.example` 70 + 10 = 80 (block-eligible; it is a look-alike of Banyan's own name), `203.0.113.66` 60 + 10 = 70 (alert; an address on its own is not blocked automatically), `198.51.100.104` expired and capped well under 50 (context only) and the hash 90 with no possible sightings (block-eligible at an endpoint control, if Banyan had one that checks hashes).

> [!WARNING]
> Confidence is not severity. A confidence-95 indicator for commodity adware and a confidence-60 indicator for ransomware staging call for different urgency. Keep confidence (how sure) and impact (how bad) as separate fields and combine them only at the point of prioritisation.

## Ageing: how confidence changes with time

Lesson 1 showed that indicator types decay at different rates. Ageing is how a SOC builds that decay into the score. There are two common designs, and the lab kit demonstrates the first.

**Hard expiry.** Each indicator carries a `valid_until` date. Before it, the indicator counts at full weight; after it, it is inactive. `ioc_match.py` implements exactly this: it computes `expired = date.fromisoformat(until) < AS_OF` and labels the line `EXPIRED` instead of `MATCH`. Hard expiry is simple and transparent, which is why it suits a teaching kit and many production feeds. Its weakness is the cliff: `203.0.113.66` (valid until 10 November) counts at full weight on 9 November and not at all on 11 November, although nothing about the address changed overnight.

**Gradual decay.** The score falls continuously with the time since the indicator was last confirmed. One simple form is a half-life model, where $s_0$ is the score at the last confirmation, $t$ the days since then and $h$ the half-life chosen for the indicator's type:

$$s(t) = s_0 \times 0.5^{\,t/h}$$

As an illustration, with a 7-day half-life for addresses, `203.0.113.66`'s local score of 70 falls to 35 after 7 days and to about 17 after 14, sliding from "alert" to "context only" during its second week. With a 30-day half-life for C2 domains, `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` falls from 95 to about 47 after 30 days. A new sighting, or a fresh report from a well-graded source, resets $t$ to zero. The half-lives here are illustrative; the point is that they follow the type. MISP, covered in lesson 4 of this module, provides configurable decay models of this general kind.

Whichever design is used, the rule is the same: **the age of the last confirmation must lower the weight of a match**, and an expired or decayed indicator must stop driving automatic action. The figure below shows the whole path from a received indicator to the action it is allowed to drive.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows how a SOC turns a received indicator into a permitted action, with confidence built up from separate judgements and then reduced by age. It matters because each step catches a different way intelligence goes wrong: a weak source, an unconfirmed claim, a stale value, an over-eager block.

**Components.** Six rounded steps run top to bottom. **Receive indicator** (slate) carries the producer confidence, for example `conf= 60`. **Grade source** (blue) assigns the reliability letter A-F. **Grade information** (blue) assigns the credibility number 1-6. **Corroborate locally** (green) checks your own sightings and detections, tracing origins to avoid circular reporting. **Apply age** (orange) applies `valid_until` or decay. **Action by threshold** (dark) maps the final score to block, alert or context only. A dashed loop labelled **new sighting or report: re-grade and reset age** runs from the last step back to **Grade information**.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each solid arrow passes the indicator, with its accumulated grade and score, to the next step. The two grading steps sit next to each other but are independent judgements, as in Figure 1. The dashed loop is the feedback path: a new sighting changes the credibility grade and restarts the age clock.

**Execution flow.** A new feed entry enters at the top, is graded, checked against local data, aged against the as-of date and finally allowed (or not allowed) to act. Matches that arrive later re-enter at the information-grading step through the loop.

**Decision logic.** The final step holds the thresholds of the local policy: 80 and above may block where the type allows it, 50-79 alerts, below 50 is context only. **Apply age** can override everything above it: an expired indicator goes to context only whatever its grade.

**Technical interpretation.** In the lab, `ioc_match.py` implements the first and fifth steps (it reads the producer confidence and applies hard expiry against `AS_OF`); the grading, corroboration and threshold steps are the analyst's and the platform's work, recorded alongside the indicator.

**Practical example.** `198.51.100.104` enters with `conf= 40` from an open feed, is graded **F3**, has no independent local corroboration, and is expired at **Apply age**, so it reaches **Action by threshold** as context only. Its 13:33 match on `gopal`'s session therefore cannot settle anything; the Module 5 lab recorded that session as undetermined and assigned a task to confirm it with `gopal` by phone, through his manager.

**Security implications.** Skipping the corroboration step invites circular reporting; skipping ageing fills blocklists with recycled addresses; skipping thresholds lets low-grade intelligence block business traffic.

**Key learning.** Confidence is built from separate judgements and must decay with time; only the final, aged score decides what an indicator is allowed to do.

The two IPv4 entries in the lab feed show confidence and ageing working together. Run in a private copy of the kit (after `python minisiem.py load`), filtering the output to the address lines:

```terminal

```

`grep -A1 ipv4` keeps lines containing `ipv4` plus one line after each match, which brings in the script's warning line. On Windows PowerShell, `python ioc_match.py | Select-String ipv4 -Context 0,1` does the same.

Walk each line through the six steps of Figure 2:

1. **`203.0.113.66`.** *Receive*: producer confidence 60 from ISAC-A, valid until 10 November. *Grade source*: B (the sector group's record is good). *Grade information*: 2 - the spraying claim is consistent with what Banyan's own data shows, but an address is a weak identifier of an actor. *Corroborate*: the nine hits are the eight VPN failures from 06:42:00 to 06:42:49 and `gopal`'s success at 06:46, the same events behind the `R2` rule, which was written without knowledge of this indicator; that is independent support (+10). *Apply age*: 7 days of validity remain. *Action*: local score 70, so it alerts and enriches the `R2` case, and the address is a candidate for a short, reviewed block at the VPN gateway rather than an automatic one.
2. **`198.51.100.104`.** *Receive*: producer confidence 40 from an open feed, expired on 30 June. *Grade source*: F (no track record). *Grade information*: 3 at best when it was issued, now unconfirmable. *Corroborate*: nothing local links this address to scanning. *Apply age*: expired 126 days before the as-of date, so it is inactive. *Action*: context only. The match on `gopal`'s 13:33 session is shown to the analyst as background and does not count as evidence either way.

The second case is the important one. `gopal`'s account was compromised by the spray at 06:46, so a later session from an address on *any* list is tempting to read as the attacker returning. The intelligence cannot carry that weight: an F3, expired indicator about shared hosting tells you almost nothing about who used the address on 3 November. The question has to be answered by other evidence - the user, his device, his normal pattern - which is exactly what the Module 5 lab did.

## Expressing confidence in analytic writing

Indicators are not the only intelligence a SOC handles. Assessments ("this activity is probably part of the finance-sector loader campaign") also carry confidence, and the same discipline applies in prose.

- **Separate likelihood from confidence.** "Likely" describes how probable you judge the event to be; "moderate confidence" describes how good your evidence is. "We assess with moderate confidence that the intrusion is likely linked to the ISAC-A campaign" says both.
- **Use a fixed vocabulary.** Agree a small set of words (for example *almost certain, likely, roughly even chance, unlikely, remote*) and what each means in your team. Words such as "possibly" or "may" without a defined meaning are read differently by every reader.
- **Name the evidence.** "Based on the matching C2 domain (B1) and an identical beacon pattern" lets a reader check the confidence; "we believe" does not.
- **Say what would change the judgement.** "A sighting of the same beacon from a second host would raise confidence; evidence that the domain is a shared service would lower it."

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Reading producer confidence as probability of maliciousness.** `conf= 85` is the producer's certainty in their own claim, on their scale. It is not a probability calibrated for your network.
- **Merging source and information grades.** Grading a report "A" because the vendor is famous, without asking whether this particular claim is confirmed.
- **Counting copies as corroboration.** Three feeds carrying one original report are one source. Trace the origin.
- **Treating "F" and "6" as bad grades.** They mean "cannot be judged"; the right response is to seek evidence, not to discard or to trust.
- **Confusing confidence with severity.** A confident indicator of something harmless should not outrank a moderately confident indicator of ransomware staging.
- **Never ageing.** Without expiry or decay, last year's addresses keep matching new, innocent tenants.
- **Letting an expired match tip a decision.** The `198.51.100.104` match on `gopal`'s 13:33 session neither condemns nor clears him.

## Security implications, detection and defence

- **Feed poisoning and false flags.** Adversaries and careless contributors can push benign values (popular domains, cloud ranges, your partners' addresses) into open feeds. If such values reach a blocklist without grading and thresholds, the feed becomes a denial-of-service against your own users. Grade open and anonymous sources conservatively and never let them block alone.
- **Allowlists as a safety net.** Keep a reviewed list of values that must never be blocked automatically (your own domains and addresses, major cloud and CDN ranges, critical partners) and check every block candidate against it.
- **Audit trail.** Record, for every indicator that drove an action, the producer, the grade, the local score, the age and the reasoning at the time. When a block causes an outage or a match leads to an accusation, the audit trail shows whether the process was followed.
- **Feedback to producers.** Reporting false positives and confirmed sightings back to the source (within the sharing rules attached to the indicator, such as its TLP marking) improves everyone's grading over time and is how sources earn or lose their letter.
- **People are in scope.** An indicator matched to a named user, as here, can affect that person's reputation and job. Low-confidence intelligence must never be the basis for disciplinary or legal action; treat such matches with the same care as any personal data under the DPDP Act 2023.

## Best practices

1. Store producer confidence, Admiralty grade, local score, last-confirmed date and reasoning for every indicator.
2. Grade source and information separately and write one sentence of justification for each grade.
3. Before grading any claim **1**, confirm that the corroborating source is independent of the original.
4. Write down the local scoring policy, including thresholds for block, alert and context only, and review it after incidents.
5. Age every indicator, by hard expiry, by decay or both, with type-dependent windows; reset the clock only on genuine new evidence.
6. Keep confidence and impact as separate fields; combine them only for prioritisation.
7. Never let a low-confidence or expired indicator be the sole basis for blocking, escalation about a person, or an incident declaration.

## Key takeaways

- A confidence number tells you how sure the producer was, not how likely the indicator is to be malicious in your network.
- The Admiralty system grades source reliability (A-F) and information credibility (1-6) independently; F and 6 mean "cannot be judged", not "bad".
- Local corroboration is the most valuable thing a SOC adds to received intelligence, but copies of the same report are not corroboration.
- A written local scoring policy turns grades and sightings into a number with thresholds for block, alert and context only.
- Confidence must age: `ioc_match.py` uses hard expiry against `AS_OF`; decay models lower the score gradually, at a rate set by the indicator's type.
- An expired, low-grade match such as `198.51.100.104` on `gopal`'s session is context only; other evidence must decide.
