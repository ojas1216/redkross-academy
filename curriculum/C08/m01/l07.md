## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A security operations centre produces something that is hard to see. When the SOC is working well, nothing dramatic happens: intrusions are caught early, false alarms are closed quickly, and the business carries on. When it is working badly, the failure is often invisible until the day it becomes catastrophic. The 2013 Target breach is the standard example: the US Senate Commerce Committee majority staff analysis reported that Target appears to have failed to respond to multiple automated warnings from its intrusion detection software. The detection technology fired. What was missing was a measured, accountable response to what it found.

**Metrics** are how a SOC makes its own performance visible. They turn the timestamps and dispositions already recorded in every case into numbers that answer management questions: how quickly do we notice an attack, how long does an alert wait before a human looks at it, how long does it take us to contain and close a case, how much of our analysts' time is spent on alerts that turn out to be nothing, and how much of the estate can we actually see? The previous lesson set out service level agreements (SLAs), the promises a SOC makes about response times. Metrics are the measurements that show whether those promises are being kept, and much more besides.

Metrics are also dangerous. A number that is reported upwards and tied to praise or blame changes the behaviour of the people being measured. If analysts are rewarded for closing alerts quickly, some will close real alerts as false positives to protect their average. If a SOC is judged on alert volume, someone will add noisy rules. Economists call this **Goodhart's law**, usually paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure". A competent SOC lead therefore treats metrics as instruments for asking questions, always pairs speed measures with quality measures, and reads the distribution behind every average.

This lesson defines the core SOC metrics precisely, shows how they are calculated from case records, works through the course lab script `LAB_ASSETS/C08/soc_metrics.py` and its real output, and explains why a median and a mean computed from the same five cases can tell completely different stories. It ends with the pitfalls that make metrics mislead and the practices that keep them honest.

> [!NOTE]
> Every number in this lesson comes from synthetic case data in the course lab kit. Nothing here describes a real organisation's performance, and there are no universal "good" values for MTTA or MTTR: targets depend on the organisation's risk, staffing and SLAs.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Metric | A defined, repeatable measurement with a stated formula, data source and unit. |
| KPI | Key performance indicator: a metric that management has chosen to track against a target. |
| MTTD | Mean time to detect: from the start of malicious activity (or the event) to the moment an alert or notice is raised. |
| MTTA | Mean time to acknowledge: from alert or case creation to an analyst picking it up. |
| MTTR | Mean time to respond or resolve: from alert or case creation to containment or closure. The "R" must always be defined. |
| Dwell time | How long an attacker was active in the environment before detection; closely related to MTTD for a single incident. |
| Disposition | The outcome recorded at closure: true positive (TP), benign true positive (BTP), false positive (FP) or inconclusive. |
| False-positive share | Fraction of closed alerts or cases whose disposition is FP. |
| Coverage | Share of critical assets, log sources or ATT&CK techniques for which the SOC has telemetry and working detections. |
| Median | The middle value of a sorted list; half the values are below it and half above. |
| Percentile (p90) | The value below which 90 per cent of observations fall; shows the experience of the slow tail. |
| Outlier | A value far from the rest of the distribution; in SOC data often the most important case. |

## What a SOC measures and why

SOC metrics fall into four families. Each answers a different question, and a SOC that reports only one family has a blind spot.

### Speed metrics

Speed metrics measure intervals between timestamps on the timeline of an alert. They are the most familiar SOC numbers because they are easy to compute and easy to compare against an SLA.

- **MTTD (mean time to detect)** asks how long malicious activity runs before the SOC notices. It is the hardest speed metric to measure honestly, because the start time is usually established only after investigation: you learn when the attacker's first action happened by reconstructing the timeline, sometimes days later. MTTD therefore lags; it can only be computed for cases where the start of activity was established.
- **MTTA (mean time to acknowledge)** asks how long an alert waits in the queue before a human takes ownership. It measures queue health and staffing, not analyst skill. A rising MTTA in the early morning hours points at a staffing gap, not at slow analysts.
- **MTTR (mean time to respond or resolve)** asks how long it takes from the alert to an outcome. Different organisations mean different things by the "R": respond (first containment action), remediate (root cause fixed), recover (service restored) or resolve (case closed). The definition must be written down and kept stable, otherwise this quarter's MTTR cannot be compared with last quarter's.

Some SOCs add **time to contain** as a separate interval, because for a true positive the moment the attacker loses access matters far more than the moment the paperwork is finished.

### Volume and quality metrics

Volume metrics count things: alerts per day, alerts per rule, cases per analyst, escalations from Tier 1 to Tier 2. On their own they mean little, because a high alert count can indicate either a busy attacker or a noisy rule. They become useful when combined with dispositions:

- **False-positive share per rule** shows which detections waste analyst time. A rule that produces hundreds of alerts a week, almost all FP, is a candidate for tuning by detection engineering (Module 7).
- **True-positive and benign-true-positive share** shows whether rules are firing on what they were designed to catch. A rule that fires correctly on approved administrator activity (BTP) is working, but may need a documented suppression.
- **Escalation accuracy** shows how often a Tier 1 escalation is confirmed by Tier 2, and how often Tier 2 finds that a case closed at Tier 1 should have been escalated.
- **Reopen rate** shows how often closed cases come back, which is often a sign that closure was premature.

Quality is also measured directly by **case review**: a senior analyst samples closed cases each week and scores whether the disposition was correct, the evidence was recorded and the reasoning is clear. This is the essential counterweight to speed metrics.

### Coverage metrics

Coverage asks what the SOC can see and detect at all. Typical measures are the share of critical assets sending logs, the share of expected log sources that reported in the last 24 hours, and the share of relevant MITRE ATT&CK techniques for which the SOC has both telemetry and a tested detection. A SOC with excellent MTTA and no logs from its payment servers is fast at handling the alerts it receives and blind to the attacks that matter.

### Capacity and wellbeing metrics

Alerts per analyst per shift, overtime hours and queue backlog at shift change measure whether the team can sustain its workload. They are directly linked to quality: tired analysts make worse decisions, and burnout drives the staff turnover that makes SOCs lose institutional knowledge. A backlog that grows every night is a staffing problem to raise, not a personal failure to hide.

## Anchoring speed metrics to the alert timeline

Every speed metric is the difference between two timestamps, so the first rule of SOC metrics is that the timestamps must be defined, captured automatically where possible and recorded in UTC. ### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the five moments in the life of a security incident that SOC speed metrics are measured between. It matters because a metric is only as meaningful as its start and stop points: two SOCs that both report "MTTR 40 minutes" may be measuring entirely different intervals.

**Components.** The first box, **Malicious activity begins**, is red because it is the attacker's action; its sub-label "earliest evidence, often found later" warns that this timestamp is usually reconstructed during investigation rather than known in real time. The second box, **Alert or case created**, is orange because it is the focus of the whole model: its sub-label reads "MTTD stops; MTTA and MTTR start". The third box, **Analyst acknowledges**, carries "MTTA stops: someone owns it". The fourth, **Threat contained**, carries "time to contain stops" and is violet because containment is a decision point where the SOC and IT operations act. The final dark box, **Case resolved and closed**, carries "MTTR stops (as in soc_metrics.py)": it is the most important outcome because it is the moment the disposition and evidence are fixed in the record.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each arrow points forward in time and means "this moment must happen before the next". The intervals between boxes are the metrics. The interval from the first box to the second is detection time; from the second to the third is acknowledgement time; from the second to the fourth is time to contain; and from the second to the fifth is resolution time.

**Execution flow.** In a real case, the SOC experiences the timeline from the second box onwards: the alert appears, an analyst picks it up, investigates, contains if needed and closes. Only during investigation does the analyst discover the first box, for example by finding that the suspicious process that raised the alert was preceded by a phishing email delivered hours earlier.

**Decision logic.** Not every case passes through every box. A false positive goes straight from acknowledgement to closure, with no containment and no meaningful "malicious activity begins" time. This is why MTTD and time to contain are computed only over true positives, while MTTA and MTTR can be computed over all cases or split by disposition.

**Technical interpretation.** Note that in this model, and in the course lab script, MTTR is measured from **case creation**, not from acknowledgement, so it already contains the acknowledgement wait. A slow queue therefore inflates both MTTA and MTTR. Some organisations measure resolution from acknowledgement instead; either is defensible if it is written down.

**Practical example.** In `soc_metrics.py`, each synthetic case has three timestamps: `created` (the second box), `acknowledged` (the third) and `resolved` (the fifth). The script has no containment or activity-start timestamps, so it can compute MTTA and MTTR but not MTTD or time to contain.

**Security implications.** If timestamps are typed by hand, they drift and are sometimes falsified under pressure. Capture them automatically in the case management system, and never let a metric depend on a field that analysts can freely edit without an audit trail.

**Key learning.** Every speed metric is an interval between two defined points on the alert timeline; state both points, measure in UTC and record them automatically.

## Worked example: computing MTTA and MTTR with soc_metrics.py

The course lab kit contains a deliberately small, execution-tested metrics script, `LAB_ASSETS/C08/soc_metrics.py` (tested on CPython 3.14.4). It holds five synthetic cases from one fictional day at the course's fictional organisation, Banyan Microfinance, and computes the speed and quality numbers a SOC lead would put in a daily report.

```python
from datetime import datetime
from statistics import mean, median

CASES = [  # (created, acknowledged, resolved, disposition) - synthetic
    ("2026-11-03T06:15", "2026-11-03T06:20", "2026-11-03T07:05", "TP"),
    ("2026-11-03T07:00", "2026-11-03T07:02", "2026-11-03T07:10", "FP"),
    ("2026-11-03T08:20", "2026-11-03T08:21", "2026-11-03T08:30", "FP"),
    ("2026-11-03T09:00", "2026-11-03T11:30", "2026-11-03T15:00", "TP"),
    ("2026-11-03T10:00", "2026-11-03T10:04", "2026-11-03T10:20", "BTP"),
]
p = lambda s: datetime.fromisoformat(s)
ack = [(p(a) - p(c)).total_seconds() / 60 for c, a, r, d in CASES]
res = [(p(r) - p(c)).total_seconds() / 60 for c, a, r, d in CASES]
print(f"MTTA mean {mean(ack):.1f} min, median {median(ack):.1f} min")
print(f"MTTR mean {mean(res):.1f} min, median {median(res):.1f} min")
fp = sum(1 for *_, d in CASES if d == "FP") / len(CASES)
print(f"false-positive share {fp:.0%}")
```

How the script works, line by line:

- `CASES` is the case record: each tuple holds the **created**, **acknowledged** and **resolved** timestamps in ISO 8601 format, plus the **disposition** (`TP`, `FP` or `BTP`). In a real SOC these rows would be exported from the case management system.
- `p` parses an ISO timestamp with `datetime.fromisoformat`. The timestamps carry no time-zone suffix; the course convention is that all case times are UTC, and a production export should include the `Z` or `+00:00` offset explicitly so that nobody has to assume.
- `ack` is the list of acknowledgement intervals in minutes: acknowledged minus created, converted from seconds with `total_seconds() / 60`.
- `res` is the list of resolution intervals: resolved minus **created**. As the first figure showed, this definition includes the time the case waited before acknowledgement.
- `mean` and `median` from the standard `statistics` module produce the two summaries. Reporting both is a deliberate design choice, explained below.
- The false-positive share counts the `FP` dispositions and divides by the total number of cases. Note that `BTP` is **not** counted as a false positive: a benign true positive means the rule detected exactly what it was written to detect, even though the activity turned out to be authorised.

Run it from the lab folder:

```terminal
$ python soc_metrics.py
MTTA mean 32.4 min, median 4.0 min
MTTR mean 90.0 min, median 20.0 min
false-positive share 40%
```

### Interpreting the output

Work out the intervals by hand first; it is the best way to learn to trust, or distrust, a metric. The five acknowledgement times are 5, 2, 1, 150 and 4 minutes. Sorted, they are 1, 2, 4, 5, 150, so the **median** is the middle value, 4 minutes. The **mean** is their sum, 162, divided by 5, which is 32.4 minutes. The resolution times are 50, 10, 10, 360 and 20 minutes; sorted, 10, 10, 20, 50, 360, giving a median of 20 minutes and a mean of 450 divided by 5, which is 90 minutes. Two of the five cases are FP, giving 40 per cent.

So which is the "real" MTTA: 4 minutes or 32.4? Both are correct calculations, and they answer different questions.

- The **median** says what a typical case experienced: half the alerts that day were picked up within 4 minutes. It is robust to outliers; changing the 150-minute case to 1,500 minutes would not move it at all.
- The **mean** says what the total waiting time was, spread evenly across cases. It is sensitive to outliers by design; one case acknowledged after two and a half hours drags it to eight times the median.

The most important observation is not either number. It is **which case** produced the outlier. The 150-minute acknowledgement belongs to the case created at 09:00, and its disposition is `TP`: a real security incident waited two and a half hours before anyone owned it, and then took six hours in total to resolve. A report that showed only the median ("MTTA 4 minutes, excellent") would have hidden exactly the case that matters most. This is the Target lesson in miniature: the alert existed, but the response did not happen in time.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Report the median for typical performance and the mean (or a high percentile) for the tail, and always list the individual cases that breached their target. An average is a summary of a distribution; the distribution is the evidence.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The bar chart shows the five acknowledgement times from `soc_metrics.py` next to the two summaries the script prints, so the effect of a single outlier on the mean is visible at a glance. It is drawn from the script's real data and output, not from illustrative numbers.

**Components.** There are seven bars. The first five are the individual cases, labelled with their creation time and disposition: **06:15 TP** (5 min), **07:00 FP** (2 min), **08:20 FP** (1 min), **09:00 TP** (150 min) and **10:00 BTP** (4 min). The 09:00 bar is red because it is both the outlier and a true positive, the combination that represents real risk. The other four case bars are slate, neutral data points. The last two bars are the summaries: **Median MTTA** at 4.0 minutes in green, because it reflects typical queue performance, and **Mean MTTA** at 32.4 minutes in orange, the figure that demands attention.

**Relationships and arrows.** There are no arrows in a bar chart; the relationship is one of length. The mean bar is long because the 150-minute bar is long. If you mentally remove the red bar, the remaining four cases have a mean of 3 minutes, which is close to the median. The gap between the green and orange bars is therefore almost entirely explained by one case.

**Execution flow.** Read the chart as a SOC lead would read a daily report: first the two summary bars, then the individual cases to find out why they disagree, then the disposition of the case responsible.

**Decision logic.** When mean and median are close, the distribution is tight and either summary is a fair description. When the mean is several times the median, there is a long tail, and the next step is always to identify and review the tail cases rather than to argue about which average to publish.

**Technical interpretation.** The median depends only on the order of values, so it ignores how extreme the largest value is. The mean depends on the sum, so every extra minute on any case moves it. For skewed data such as response times, which have a hard floor at zero and no ceiling, the mean is usually larger than the median.

**Practical example.** In a weekly review at Banyan Microfinance, the SOC lead would open the 09:00 case and ask: was it created during a shift change, was it routed to the wrong queue, was its severity set too low, or was the analyst busy on another incident? Each answer leads to a different fix: handover discipline (Lesson 5), routing rules, severity tuning (Module 5) or staffing.

**Security implications.** A metric that hides tail cases hides the incidents that do the most damage. Conversely, a team judged on the mean has an incentive to close long-running cases prematurely, which is why quality review must accompany any speed target.

**Key learning.** One late true positive turned a 4-minute median into a 32.4-minute mean; always look at the cases behind the average, and treat a slow true positive as an incident review item, not a statistic.

### Extending the script: breaches and dispositions

The summary lines are a starting point. A SOC lead usually wants two more views: which cases breached a target, and how resolution time differs by disposition. The extension below imports the case list and parser from `soc_metrics.py` and adds both. The 15-minute acknowledgement target is an illustrative value chosen for this exercise, not a standard; real targets come from your SLAs (Lesson 6).

```python
from collections import Counter
from statistics import median
from soc_metrics import CASES, p

ACK_TARGET_MIN = 15  # illustrative target, not a standard
for c, a, r, d in CASES:
    ack = (p(a) - p(c)).total_seconds() / 60
    res = (p(r) - p(c)).total_seconds() / 60
    flag = "BREACH" if ack > ACK_TARGET_MIN else "ok"
    print(f"{c}  {d:<3}  ack {ack:5.0f}  resolve {res:5.0f}  {flag}")
print("dispositions", dict(Counter(d for *_, d in CASES)))
for disp in ("TP", "FP", "BTP"):
    rs = [(p(r) - p(c)).total_seconds() / 60 for c, a, r, d in CASES if d == disp]
    print(f"median resolve {disp}: {median(rs):.1f} min (n={len(rs)})")
```

Save it as `metrics_ext.py` next to `soc_metrics.py` and run it. Because `soc_metrics.py` has no `if __name__ == "__main__":` guard, importing it also runs its print statements, so its three summary lines appear first:

```terminal
$ python metrics_ext.py
MTTA mean 32.4 min, median 4.0 min
MTTR mean 90.0 min, median 20.0 min
false-positive share 40%
2026-11-03T06:15  TP   ack     5  resolve    50  ok
2026-11-03T07:00  FP   ack     2  resolve    10  ok
2026-11-03T08:20  FP   ack     1  resolve    10  ok
2026-11-03T09:00  TP   ack   150  resolve   360  BREACH
2026-11-03T10:00  BTP  ack     4  resolve    20  ok
dispositions {'TP': 2, 'FP': 2, 'BTP': 1}
median resolve TP: 205.0 min (n=2)
median resolve FP: 10.0 min (n=2)
median resolve BTP: 20.0 min (n=1)
```

Three things stand out. First, four of five cases (80 per cent) met the illustrative acknowledgement target, and the one breach is the true positive already identified. An SLA report that said "80 per cent compliance" would be accurate, but the breach list is what drives action. Second, the disposition split is two TP, two FP and one BTP; false positives are 40 per cent of cases but, at 10 minutes each, consume far less analyst time than the true positives. Third, the median resolution time for true positives is 205 minutes against 10 for false positives. Blending them into one MTTR of 20 minutes (median) or 90 minutes (mean) describes no real case at all. Splitting by disposition and severity is almost always more informative than a single overall figure.

> [!TIP]
> Notice the `(n=1)` and `(n=2)` counts. With samples this small, a median is fragile and a percentile is meaningless. Always print the sample size next to any summary, and be cautious about trends computed from a handful of cases.

## From case records to a metrics report

A production SOC does not run a script over five hand-typed tuples; it exports case records from the case management system on a schedule and builds the same calculations into a dashboard. The steps are nevertheless identical, and doing them by hand once makes the dashboard easier to question later.

1. **Define each metric in writing.** For every metric, record the name, the formula, the start and stop timestamps, the population (all cases, only true positives, only high severity) and the unit. "MTTR = resolved minus created, all cases, minutes, median and mean" is a definition; "MTTR" alone is not.
2. **Capture timestamps automatically.** Creation, acknowledgement (the moment an analyst assigns the case to themselves), containment and closure should be written by the system, in UTC, with an audit trail.
3. **Export and clean.** Remove test cases and duplicates; decide how to treat cases still open at the end of the reporting period (exclude them from resolution metrics, but report how many there are and how old the oldest is).
4. **Split before you summarise.** Compute metrics per severity, per disposition, per rule and per shift. A single blended number hides almost everything useful.
5. **Summarise with median, mean and tail.** Report the median for typical performance and the mean or 90th percentile for the slow tail, with the sample size.
6. **List the exceptions.** Every case that breached its target, every reopened case and every true positive with a long acknowledgement time goes on a named list for review.
7. **Pair with quality.** Attach the results of the week's case review: how many sampled closures were judged correct, and what was learned.
8. **Feed back.** Turn findings into actions with owners: a noisy rule to tune, a queue routing change, a staffing request, a missing log source.

A dashboard that is read but never acted on is decoration.

### Who reads which metrics

Analysts and shift leads need near-real-time numbers: queue depth, oldest unacknowledged alert, cases breaching SLA now. The SOC manager needs weekly trends by severity and rule, with case review scores. Senior management needs a few outcome measures tied to risk, such as time to contain serious incidents and coverage of critical assets. Regulators and auditors need evidence that obligations were met; in India, for example, CERT-In's directions of April 2022 require specified cyber incidents to be reported within 6 hours of noticing them, so a SOC that serves an Indian entity should be able to show, case by case, when an incident was noticed and when it was reported.

> [!WARNING]
> Never publish per-analyst speed rankings. They reward cherry-picking easy alerts and closing hard ones early, and they punish the analyst who spends three hours on the one case that really was an intrusion. Use per-analyst data privately for coaching, alongside case review.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"MTTR means the same thing everywhere."** It does not. Respond, remediate, recover and resolve are different intervals, and some SOCs start the clock at acknowledgement rather than creation. Comparing your MTTR with a figure in a vendor report is meaningless unless the definitions match, and they rarely do.

**"A lower average is always better."** Average resolution time can be lowered by closing cases before investigation is complete, by reclassifying real incidents as false positives, or by splitting one incident into many small cases. A falling MTTR with a rising reopen rate or falling review scores is a warning, not a success.

**"The median is the honest number, so report only that."** The median is robust, which is exactly why it hides the tail. The tail is where serious incidents live. Report both, and the exceptions.

**"A high false-positive share means analysts are doing badly."** False positives are a property of detection rules and telemetry, not of the analysts who close them correctly. A high FP share is a signal for detection engineering to tune rules, and closing FPs quickly and accurately is good work.

**"Benign true positives are just false positives."** They are not. A BTP is a correct detection of authorised activity, such as an administrator legitimately creating an account. Lumping BTPs into FPs makes good rules look noisy and can lead to them being disabled, removing coverage. The right response to a recurring BTP is usually a documented, time-limited suppression.

**"MTTD can be computed in real time."** The start of malicious activity is usually established only by investigation, so MTTD is a lagging, retrospective measure available only for investigated true positives.
## Security implications, detection and defence

Metrics are themselves a security control. They are how a SOC detects its own failures: an alert that nobody acknowledged, a log source that went silent, a rule that stopped firing after a change. The Target case is often summarised as a detection failure, but the warnings were generated; what failed was the response process, and response metrics with an exception list are the tool that exposes that kind of failure before an attacker exploits it.

Several metrics deserve automated alerting of their own rather than waiting for a weekly report:

- **Unacknowledged high-severity alerts** older than their target, routed to the shift lead and on-call staff.
- **Silent log sources**, measured as the time since each expected source last sent an event. A source that stops reporting creates a blind spot that no speed metric will reveal.
- **Rules with zero hits** over a long period, which may be broken by a field-name change rather than genuinely quiet. Module 7 covers testing detections so that this is caught early.
- **Sudden volume changes** per rule or per source, which may indicate an attack, a misconfiguration or a broken parser.

Metric data also needs protecting. Case records contain personal data (user names, hosts, sometimes email content), so exports for reporting should be minimised and access-controlled in line with the DPDP Act 2023. Timestamps used for metrics should be system-generated and auditable: a metric that analysts can edit is a metric that will be edited.

## Best practices

- Write a **metrics definition sheet**: name, formula, start and stop points, population, unit, owner and review date for every metric.
- Record all timestamps in **UTC**, captured automatically by the case system.
- Report **median and mean (or p90)** together, with the **sample size**, split by severity and disposition.
- Maintain an **exception list** of every breach and every slow true positive, and review it weekly.
- Pair every speed metric with a **quality metric**: case review scores, reopen rate, escalation accuracy.
- Track **coverage** (log sources reporting, critical assets monitored, techniques with tested detections) alongside speed.
- Use metrics to improve **systems**, not to rank individuals; discuss individual data privately as coaching.
- Revisit metrics when they stop driving decisions, and retire any that encourage the wrong behaviour.

## Key takeaways

- SOC metrics fall into four families: speed (MTTD, MTTA, MTTR), volume and quality (dispositions, FP share, review scores), coverage, and capacity. Reporting only speed leaves blind spots.
- Every speed metric is the interval between two defined timestamps; write the definition down, especially what the "R" in MTTR means.
- In the lab data, one true positive acknowledged after 150 minutes turned a 4.0-minute median MTTA into a 32.4-minute mean; the outlier was the case that mattered most.
- Report median and mean (or a high percentile) with the sample size, split by severity and disposition, and always list the exceptions.
- False positives are a detection-tuning signal, not an analyst failure, and benign true positives are not false positives.
- Metrics change behaviour. Pair every speed target with a quality check, never rank analysts on speed, and use the numbers to fix systems.
