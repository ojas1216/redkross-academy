## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Lesson 1 described the SOC as a funnel: Tier 1 triages, Tier 2 investigates, Tier 3 provides specialist depth. That picture answers one question, *how deep does the analysis go at each stage?*, but it leaves another unanswered: *who is responsible for each piece of work that keeps the SOC running?* Somebody must write and test the detection rules. Somebody must notice when a log source stops sending data. Somebody must decide whether an incident is reportable to CERT-In and file the report inside six hours. Somebody must actually disable the compromised account, and somebody else must be told that it has been disabled. None of these is a tier; each of them is a **role**.

A role is a defined set of responsibilities, decisions and authorities attached to a position, not to a person. In a large SOC each role may be a team; in a small one, one person may hold three roles and swap between them during a shift. What matters is not the headcount but that every responsibility is held by someone, that everyone knows who holds it, and that the authority to act matches the responsibility to act. Most of the operational failures that SOCs suffer are role failures rather than technical ones: an alert that nobody owned, a containment action that nobody was authorised to take, a reporting deadline that nobody was watching.

This lesson maps the roles in and around a SOC in three rings. The **inner ring** is the analysis and response work that cases pass through. The **engineering ring** builds and maintains what the analysts use: detections, automation, the SIEM platform and threat intelligence. The **outer ring** is the set of teams outside the SOC that it depends on and serves: IT operations, the identity team, legal and compliance, communications, management, and external parties such as CERT-In and service providers. The lesson then shows how to make the split of responsibility explicit with a RACI matrix, and how the roles are divided between an organisation and a managed provider when some of the SOC is outsourced.

> [!NOTE]
> Job titles vary enormously between organisations. "Security analyst", "SOC engineer" and "cyber defence specialist" can each mean almost anything. Learn the responsibilities in this lesson, and map whatever title you meet onto them.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Role | A defined set of responsibilities, decisions and authorities attached to a position rather than to a person. |
| SOC manager | The person accountable for the SOC's service: staffing, quality, metrics, budget and stakeholder communication. |
| Shift lead | The senior analyst in charge of a shift, who assigns work, approves escalations and owns the handover. |
| Incident responder | An analyst who leads investigation and coordination of a declared incident. |
| Detection engineer | A specialist who designs, tests, deploys and tunes detection rules and maps them to attacker techniques. |
| Threat intelligence analyst | A specialist who collects and assesses information about threats and turns it into detections, hunts and briefings. |
| SOAR engineer | A specialist who builds the automation (playbooks in a SOAR platform) that enriches alerts and runs approved actions. |
| Platform engineer | A specialist who keeps the SIEM and log pipeline healthy: ingestion, parsing, storage, retention and access. |
| RACI | A responsibility matrix: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome, one per activity), Consulted, Informed. |
| MSSP | Managed security service provider: an external company that monitors an organisation's security telemetry. |
| MDR | Managed detection and response: a provider service that adds investigation and response actions to monitoring. |

## The inner ring: analysis and response roles

These are the people whose work you saw in Lesson 1's funnel. Here the emphasis is on what each role is responsible for and what it is entitled to decide.

### SOC analyst

The SOC analyst (often labelled Tier 1 or Tier 2) owns cases from the queue. Responsibilities: triage and enrich alerts, link related alerts into cases, record dispositions with evidence, carry out pre-authorised first-response actions, and escalate within the agreed service level. Decisions the analyst is entitled to make: close a case as false positive or benign true positive when the playbook criteria are met; escalate; take a pre-authorised containment action such as isolating an endpoint when a high-confidence detection fires. Decisions the analyst is usually *not* entitled to make alone: declaring a major incident, taking a production server offline, or deciding whether an incident is reportable.

### Shift lead

The shift lead is the most senior analyst on a shift. Responsibilities: assign work and watch the queue for ageing high-severity cases, approve or redirect escalations, act as the first point of contact for other teams during the shift, and own the shift handover (Lesson 5). The shift lead is often the person who calls the on-call incident responder out of hours, which makes this role the practical guardian of the escalation path.

### Incident responder

The incident responder (often Tier 2, or a dedicated incident response team) leads cases once they are declared incidents. Responsibilities: scope the incident, build its timeline, recommend containment, eradication and recovery steps, coordinate the teams that carry them out, and provide accurate facts to whoever makes notification decisions. In many organisations the senior responder on a major incident becomes the **incident commander** for its duration, coordinating everyone else; Module 7 covers incident response in depth.

### Threat hunter, forensic analyst and malware analyst

These are the Tier 3 specialists. The **threat hunter** searches proactively for activity no rule has detected, starting from a hypothesis. The **forensic analyst** acquires and examines evidence from disks, memory and logs in a way that preserves its integrity, which matters if the case may end up in court or before a regulator. The **malware analyst** examines suspicious files in an isolated environment to learn what they do and extract indicators. All three are consulted by incident responders and, crucially, feed what they learn to the engineering ring.

## The engineering ring: the people who build the SOC

A SOC's analysts can only see what the platform collects and can only be alerted by rules that someone has written. The engineering ring is often invisible to people outside the SOC, but a SOC without it decays quickly: log sources silently stop reporting, rules drift out of date and noisy alerts are never tuned.

### Detection engineer

The detection engineer treats detections as software. Responsibilities: design a rule from a known attacker behaviour, test it against data that should and should not trigger it, deploy it, measure its volume and false-positive rate, tune it, and map the coverage to MITRE ATT&CK so gaps are visible (Module 6). The detection engineer is the main recipient of the feedback arrow from Lesson 1: when an analyst closes an alert as a false positive and flags the rule, it is the detection engineer who decides whether to change the logic, add a suppression, or retire the rule.

The course lab shows this division of labour in its own code. In `LAB_ASSETS/C08/triage.py`, the rule severities and confidences are introduced by the comment "Rule base severity 1-4 and confidence 0.0-1.0, reviewed by detection engineering", and the suppression list by "Suppressions: (rule, field, value, reason, expiry) - every entry has an owner ticket". Its single suppression silences the new-local-administrator rule for the account `itadmin` with the reason "approved build account, CHG-1042" and an expiry of 2026-12-31. The analyst benefits from that suppression; the detection engineer owns it, justifies it with a change ticket, and must review it before it expires.

### Threat intelligence analyst

The threat intelligence analyst collects information about adversaries, from commercial feeds, sector sharing groups and national advisories such as those from CERT-In, and assesses which of it is relevant to the organisation. Responsibilities: turn relevant intelligence into indicators for matching, hypotheses for hunting, and requirements for detection engineering; brief management on the threats the organisation faces (Module 5). The role's value lies in filtering: loading every indicator from every feed produces floods of alerts, while curated intelligence produces a few that matter.

### SOAR engineer

The SOAR engineer builds the automation that removes repetitive work from analysts: enrichment look-ups, de-duplication, ticket creation and approved response actions such as isolating a host or disabling an account. Because a SOAR platform holds credentials powerful enough to change production systems, the SOAR engineer is also responsible for the safety of those automations: which actions run without human approval, which require a click from an analyst, and how every action is logged.

### Platform engineer

The platform engineer keeps the SIEM and its log pipeline healthy: onboarding new log sources, maintaining parsers so fields are normalised correctly, monitoring ingestion so that a silent source is noticed, managing storage and retention, and controlling who can search what. For CERT-In covered entities, the platform engineer is the person who makes the 180-day log retention requirement real. A gap in ingestion is a gap in detection that no analyst can see from the queue, which is why "are all log sources reporting?" is one of the first checks at the start of every shift.

### SOC manager

The SOC manager is accountable for the SOC as a service. Responsibilities: staffing and shift rotas, hiring and training, the quality of analysis, metrics and service levels (Lessons 6 and 7), budget and tooling, and communication with the CISO and other stakeholders. The SOC manager also owns the SOC's interfaces with the outer ring: agreeing with IT operations which containment actions the SOC may take on its own, agreeing with legal and compliance how notification decisions are reached, and holding any managed provider to its contract.

**Execution flow.** In daily operation, work starts at the left (the shift team handles the queue), moves right when a case becomes an incident or needs specialists, and the engineering groups on the right turn what was learned into better detections and automation that reach the shift team the following day.

**Decision logic.** The tree implies a division of decision rights. Analysts decide dispositions; incident responders recommend containment; detection engineers decide rule changes and suppressions; the manager decides staffing, priorities and what the SOC commits to in its service levels.

**Technical interpretation.** The tree is a responsibility map, not a headcount chart. In a five-person SOC, one person may sit in three boxes. The boxes still exist, and the question "who is doing the platform engineering this month?" must still have an answer.

**Practical example.** In the course lab, the `RULE_META` and `SUPPRESS` structures in `triage.py` belong to the detection engineers' box, the queue that `triage.py` prints belongs to the shift team's box, and keeping `minisiem.py` loading all 340 events from its four sources is the platform engineers' job.

**Security implications.** The engineering boxes hold the most privileged access: SIEM administrators can read everything, and SOAR automations can change production systems. Those roles need strong authentication, least privilege and logging of their own actions.

**Key learning.** A SOC is not just analysts. Every box in this tree must have an owner, even if one person owns several.

## The outer ring: interfaces outside the SOC

The SOC rarely owns the systems it protects. It cannot reimage a server, reset a director's password, approve a press statement or file a regulatory report on its own authority. It works through other teams, and its effectiveness depends on agreements made with them before an incident, not during one.

- **IT operations** owns servers, endpoints and networks. It carries out most containment and recovery work: isolating network segments, rebuilding hosts, restoring from backup. The SOC recommends; IT operations executes, except for actions pre-authorised for the SOC.
- **Identity team** owns the directory and identity provider. It resets credentials, revokes sessions and disables accounts, which are among the most frequent containment actions.
- **Legal and compliance** decide whether an incident triggers a legal obligation and what must be said. In India this includes the CERT-In Directions (reporting specified incidents within six hours of noticing them) and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, which requires notification of personal data breaches to the Data Protection Board of India and to affected data principals. The SOC's job is to give them accurate, timely facts; theirs is to make the decision.
- **Communications** handles internal and external messaging, so that staff, customers and the press receive consistent, accurate information.
- **Management** (the CISO and above) sets priorities, accepts risk, and authorises disruptive actions such as taking a business service offline.
- **External parties** include CERT-In, law enforcement, sector bodies, and the providers whose services are involved: cloud platforms, managed security providers and software vendors.

Notice the pattern: in almost every case, the SOC holds the *knowledge* and another team holds the *authority*. The interface must transmit knowledge quickly and authority must be agreed in advance, or decisions wait while the attacker does not.

## Making responsibility explicit: the RACI matrix

The standard tool for writing down who does what is a **RACI matrix**. Each row is an activity; each column is a role; each cell holds one or more of four letters. **R**, Responsible, marks who does the work. **A**, Accountable, marks the single role that owns the outcome and signs it off; there must be exactly one A per row. **C**, Consulted, marks roles whose input is sought before the action. **I**, Informed, marks roles that are told afterwards. A role can be both A and R, written A/R.

The rule of exactly one A is what makes the matrix useful. When two teams both believe they are accountable for containment, they argue during the incident; when neither believes it, nothing happens. **Relationships.** Read each row across. In "Triage and disposition", the SOC analyst is A/R and the incident responder is only I. In "Declare an incident", the incident responder is A/R, the analyst C (their evidence is the basis), and legal I. In "Isolate endpoint (EDR, pre-agreed)", the analyst is R because the action is pre-authorised, the incident responder is A, and IT ops / identity is I. In "Reset account / revoke sessions", IT ops / identity is A/R and the responder C. In "Rebuild or restore a server", IT ops / identity is A/R, the responder C, the analyst I. In "CERT-In report decision (6 h)", legal / compliance is A/R and both SOC roles are C.

**Execution flow.** Read top to bottom, the rows follow a typical incident: triage, declaration, quick containment, identity containment, recovery, and the reporting decision that runs in parallel from the moment the incident is noticed.

**Decision logic.** The single A in each row is the decision-maker. If a cell is disputed, for example whether the SOC may isolate a server rather than a workstation, the matrix must be changed and agreed before the next incident, not argued during it.

**Technical interpretation.** Accountability shifts away from the SOC as actions move from analysis to changing production systems and making legal statements. The SOC is accountable for knowing; other teams are accountable for acting on what they own.

**Practical example.** For the WS-104 case from Lesson 1, the analyst escalates (row 1), the responder declares an incident (row 2), the analyst isolates WS-104 under the pre-agreed EDR action (row 3), the identity team revokes divya's sessions (row 4), and legal starts the CERT-In decision with facts from the responder (row 6).

**Security implications.** Pre-agreeing row 3 is what lets containment happen in minutes. Leaving row 6 without a clear A risks missing the six-hour window.

**Key learning.** Exactly one Accountable per activity, agreed before the incident; the SOC supplies the facts, owners make the changes.

## Who provides the SOC: in-house, managed or hybrid

Roles can be held by an organisation's own staff or by a provider. There are three broad delivery models.

| Model | What it means | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| In-house | The organisation employs its own SOC staff | Full control and business context | Costly; hard to staff around the clock |
| Outsourced (MSSP / MDR) | A provider monitors, and with MDR also investigates and responds | Fast to start; round-the-clock coverage and expertise | Less business context; depends on the contract |
| Hybrid | Internal team owns context and response; provider covers hours or specialist work | Balances context and coverage | Hand-offs between two organisations must be designed |

Outsourcing moves roles, not accountability. An organisation that uses a managed provider is still accountable for its own regulatory reporting, for the containment decisions on its systems, and for knowing what the provider is and is not doing. The contract is where the roles are written down, and a contract that says only "monitoring of logs" leaves almost every question unanswered. A useful contract specifies the log sources in scope; hours and coverage; who writes and tunes detections; severity definitions and response times; escalation contacts and methods that work around the clock; what containment the provider may perform; evidence handling and retention, including the 180-day CERT-In requirement; support for regulatory reporting; data protection and access controls; metrics and review meetings; and how data is returned at exit.

## Worked example: roles at 03:00

Consider a fictional scenario. At a managed provider serving a fictional retailer, the night analyst sees signs of ransomware staging: an Office document launching a script, followed by new local administrator accounts on two file servers. The analyst messages a colleague by chat and goes back to the queue. The colleague has already gone home; no case is opened. The morning shift finds the file servers encrypted.

Map the failure onto roles. The analyst correctly recognised the pattern but escalated outside the case system, so the shift lead never saw it and could not call the on-call responder. Nobody was accountable for isolation of the servers, because the contract did not say whether the provider could do it. The customer's legal team learned of the incident hours after it was noticed, with the CERT-In window already running.

The fixes are role fixes. Escalation only through the case system, with acknowledgement. An automatic alert to the shift lead on any unacknowledged critical case. An on-call rota with named responders. A RACI agreed with the customer stating that the provider may isolate hosts on a high-confidence ransomware detection. A handover checklist that lists every open critical case. None of these needs new technology.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Two accountables, or none.** If the SOC and IT operations both think they own containment, they argue; if neither does, nothing happens. One A per activity.

**Unowned platform health.** When nobody holds the platform engineer role, a log source can stop reporting for weeks. The SOC looks calm because it is blind.

**SOC acting beyond its authority.** An analyst who takes a production server offline without pre-authorisation may cause more damage than the attacker. Pre-agree the actions, then use them confidently.

**Assuming the provider does everything.** An MSSP that monitors logs is not responsible for the organisation's CERT-In report unless the contract says so, and even then the organisation remains accountable.

**Titles instead of responsibilities.** Hiring a "SOC engineer" without writing down which boxes of the role tree they own leaves gaps that only appear during an incident.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Roles carry privileges, and privileged roles are attractive targets. SIEM administrators can read every log, including sensitive personal data; SOAR automations hold credentials that can disable accounts and isolate hosts. Apply least privilege to SOC roles themselves, require phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication for SOC tooling, log every administrative and automated action, and review those logs, ideally by someone outside the role being reviewed. Separate duties where it matters: the person who adds a suppression should not be the only person who reviews it. Case records contain personal data about employees and customers, so access to them must follow need-to-know, consistent with the organisation's obligations under the DPDP Act 2023.

## Best practices

- Write a role tree and a RACI for the SOC; agree the RACI with every team named in it.
- Ensure every role has an owner and a deputy, including the engineering roles.
- Pre-authorise a small set of reversible containment actions for the SOC.
- Name the accountable role for each regulatory decision and give it a direct line to the SOC.
- For managed services, write roles into the contract and review them at least yearly.
- Review the RACI after every major incident.

## Key takeaways

- A role is a set of responsibilities and authorities, not a person or a title; every role must have an owner.
- Inner-ring roles work cases; engineering-ring roles (detection, intelligence, SOAR, platform) keep the SOC able to see and alert; the SOC manager is accountable for the whole service.
- The SOC usually holds the knowledge while other teams hold the authority; containment and reporting authority must be agreed in advance.
- A RACI matrix with exactly one Accountable per activity makes responsibility explicit.
- Outsourcing to an MSSP or MDR provider moves roles but not accountability; the contract defines what happens at 03:00.
