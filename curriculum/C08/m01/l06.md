## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every security operations centre makes promises about time. An in-house SOC promises the business that a critical alert will be looked at within minutes, not hours. A managed security service provider (MSSP) or managed detection and response (MDR) provider writes those promises into a contract and is paid, and sometimes penalised, according to whether it keeps them. Regulators impose their own clocks: in India, the CERT-In Directions of April 2022 require covered entities to report specified cyber incidents within six hours of noticing them. A **service level agreement (SLA)** is the instrument that turns these expectations into explicit, measurable commitments.

Without SLAs, "we monitor your environment" can mean anything. The course's scenario of a fictional hospital whose MSSP contract said only "monitoring of logs" makes the point: such a contract does not say which logs, during which hours, how quickly an alert must be examined, who is called at 3 a.m., or what happens when a deadline is missed. Its answer key ends with a sentence worth remembering: contracts define what actually happens at 3 a.m. An SLA is the part of the contract, or of the internal service definition, that fixes those answers before they are needed.

SLAs matter inside the SOC as well as between the SOC and its customers. They tell an analyst which item in the queue to pick next, they tell the shift lead when to intervene, they give the handover (Lesson 5) explicit deadlines to pass on, and they make failure visible early enough to fix. This lesson explains what a SOC SLA contains, how its timers work internally, how severity drives the targets, how regulatory deadlines fit in, and how SLAs go wrong when people optimise the number rather than the outcome. Lesson 7 then deals with the aggregate metrics, such as mean and median time to acknowledge, that summarise SLA performance over weeks and months.

> [!NOTE]
> The specific target times used as examples in this lesson (for instance "acknowledge a critical alert within 15 minutes") are illustrative values for the fictional Banyan Microfinance SOC. There is no universal standard for SOC response times; each organisation sets targets according to its risk, staffing and contracts.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Service level agreement (SLA) | A documented agreement between a service provider and its customer that defines the service and the measurable levels it must meet. |
| Service level objective (SLO) | A specific measurable target within an SLA, for example "95% of critical alerts acknowledged within 15 minutes". |
| Operational level agreement (OLA) | An internal agreement between teams of the same organisation that supports an SLA, for example the identity team's commitment to reset accounts within an hour of a SOC request. |
| Underpinning contract | An agreement with an external supplier that the service depends on, such as a log-collection platform vendor. |
| Time to acknowledge | Time from alert or case creation until a named analyst takes ownership. |
| Time to triage | Time from creation until an initial disposition: benign, malicious, or needs investigation. |
| Time to notify | Time from confirming an incident until the customer or stakeholder is informed. |
| Time to resolve | Time from creation until the case is closed or the incident contained, as defined in the SLA. |
| Clock pause | A defined condition, such as waiting for information from the customer, during which an SLA timer stops counting. |
| Breach | A case for which an SLA target was missed. |
| Service credit | A contractual remedy, usually a fee reduction, when a provider misses agreed service levels. |

The ITIL service-management framework popularised the SLA, OLA and underpinning-contract vocabulary, and most SOC tooling and contracts use it. The key relationship is that an SLA can only be met if the agreements beneath it are also met: a SOC cannot promise to contain a compromised account within an hour if the identity team has no commitment to act on its request.

## What a SOC SLA defines

A useful SOC SLA answers six groups of questions. Leaving any of them vague leads to disputes at exactly the moment the SOC is under pressure.

### Scope of the service

Which log sources, sites and asset groups are monitored, which are not, and what the provider is responsible for doing (monitoring and triage only, or also investigation, containment and forensic support). Scope also covers detection content: who writes and tunes the rules, and how quickly a new log source must be onboarded.

### Hours of coverage

Whether monitoring is 24x7 or limited to business hours, and in which time zone. An SLA that says "acknowledge within 30 minutes" means something very different if the clock runs only between 09:00 and 18:00 IST. Calendar-hour timers run continuously; business-hour timers stop outside the defined working day. For security monitoring, calendar hours are the norm for high-severity work, because attackers keep no office hours.

### Severity definitions

SLA targets attach to severity levels, so the severity scale must be defined in the agreement, with examples. If the customer and provider disagree on whether an event is critical or high, they also disagree on which deadline applies. Module 5 of this course treats severity and prioritisation in depth; here the point is that the SLA must state who assigns the severity and how a severity change affects running timers.

### Time targets per severity

For each severity: time to acknowledge, time to triage, time to notify the customer, and, where the provider has that responsibility, time to contain or resolve. Each target needs a defined start event and stop event, discussed in the next section.

### Escalation and contacts

Who is contacted, by which method, in what order, and within what time, for each severity, both on the provider side and on the customer side. A critical incident at 03:00 needs a named on-call person on both sides and a method that wakes them, such as a phone call, not only a ticket update.

### Reporting, evidence and review

How performance is reported (monthly SLA reports, breach explanations), how evidence is handled and retained (including the 180-day log retention required by the CERT-In Directions for covered entities), what support is given for regulatory reporting, how the SLA itself is reviewed, and what happens at contract exit, including return of data.

## How SLA timers work

A target such as "acknowledge within 15 minutes" is only measurable if everyone agrees on exactly when the clock starts, what stops it, and whether anything can pause it. Case management and SOAR platforms implement SLAs as **timers** attached to each case, driven by events in the case's history. Understanding that mechanism prevents most SLA disputes.

### Start events

The obvious start event is the moment the alert is created in the SIEM or case system. But there are alternatives, and the choice matters. If the timer starts when a case is created from an alert, any delay between the alert and the case is invisible. If it starts at the time of the underlying event, delays in log collection and rule scheduling count against the SOC. Most SOC SLAs start the clock when the alert reaches the SOC's queue, because that is the first moment the SOC could act, and measure the earlier delay separately as part of time to detect (Lesson 7). Whatever is chosen, it is written down and every timestamp is recorded in UTC from synchronised clocks. The CERT-In Directions require covered entities to synchronise their ICT system clocks with the NTP servers of the National Informatics Centre (NIC) or the National Physical Laboratory (NPL), or with servers traceable to them, which also makes cross-system timing reliable.

### Stop events

Each target has its own stop event. Time to acknowledge stops when a named analyst takes ownership, not when someone opens the alert to look at it. Time to triage stops when an initial disposition is recorded. Time to notify stops when the customer has been informed through the agreed channel, which the record must show. Time to resolve stops at closure or containment as the SLA defines it.

### Pauses

Some SLAs allow the clock to pause while the provider is waiting for the customer, for example for approval to isolate a server or for confirmation that an administrator's activity was authorised. Pauses are legitimate but dangerous. A pause condition must be defined in the agreement, set by a recorded event (a request sent at a stated time), and cleared automatically when the customer replies. Pauses should never apply to time to acknowledge or time to notify for critical severities, because those are the moments when delay causes harm.

### Warnings and breaches

A well-configured timer does not simply record a breach after the fact; it warns before the breach. A common pattern is to notify the case owner when a set share of the target has elapsed (for example 75%) and to alert the shift lead at the breach itself. Breached cases are then reviewed and given a reason code (staffing, tooling failure, misclassified severity, customer delay) so that the monthly report explains failures rather than just counting them.

### Severity changes

Cases are often reclassified. A medium alert may become critical when the analyst finds that the host is a domain controller. The SLA must say how timers respond. A sound rule is that an upgrade applies the stricter target measured from the original creation time, so that the SOC cannot gain time by starting with a low severity, while a downgrade keeps any breach already recorded.

**Execution flow.** The clock starts when the alert reaches the queue. Either an analyst acknowledges it within the target, or the warning fires and, if nothing happens, the breach is recorded and escalated. After acknowledgement the analyst triages; the case closes, escalates, or waits for the customer and then returns to triage.

**Decision logic.** The decision has three outcomes, and each changes the timers differently: closure stops the triage and resolution clocks; escalation stops triage and starts the notification clock; waiting pauses only the timers the SLA allows to pause.

**Technical interpretation.** In a case platform each arrow corresponds to a recorded event with a timestamp: created, assigned, disposition set, status changed to awaiting customer, customer reply. The SLA engine computes elapsed time from those events, subtracting paused intervals, and raises the warning and breach notifications as scheduled jobs.

**Practical example.** In the lab data used later in this lesson, one true-positive case was created at 09:00 and not acknowledged until 11:30. On this diagram it took the dashed path: the warning should have fired at about 09:11 against a 15-minute target, and the breach at 09:15, long before anyone picked it up.

**Security implications.** The breach path exists because the worst failures are silent. The Target 2013 case analysis in this course describes automated warnings that were not acted on effectively; an unacknowledged-alert breach that pages the shift lead is precisely the control that makes such inaction visible while there is still time.

**Key learning.** Define the start, stop and pause events for every target, and configure timers to warn before they breach.

The notification column is measured from confirmation of an incident, not from alert creation, which is why it has a different start event from the other two.

**Execution flow.** An analyst who acknowledges a case reads its severity, finds the row, and knows the remaining deadlines. The case system shows the same values as live timers.

**Decision logic.** The only decision is the severity itself. Everything else follows from it, which is why the severity definitions and the rule for reclassification are part of the SLA.

**Technical interpretation.** Notice the change of units. Critical and High use calendar minutes and hours; Medium and Low use business days. That is a deliberate design: high-severity work is 24x7, while lower-severity work can wait for daytime staff. The case system needs both calendar and business-hour clocks to implement this.

**Practical example.** CASE-1042 from Lesson 5, a High-severity suspected credential-stuffing case, had to be acknowledged within 30 minutes and triaged within two hours. If it is confirmed as an account takeover, the customer must be told within four hours of that confirmation, and the CERT-In six-hour clock must be checked separately.

**Security implications.** If targets are too loose, real attacks progress while the SOC stays within its SLA; if too tight for the staffing level, analysts start acknowledging without looking, which meets the number and defeats the purpose. Targets must be set together with staffing and alert-volume planning.

**Key learning.** Severity selects the deadline, so a SOC that gets severity wrong gets every timer wrong; define both together.

## Regulatory clocks and internal targets

An SLA is a commitment the SOC chooses; a regulatory deadline is one imposed on the organisation. The two must be designed together, and the internal target must always be shorter than the external one.

The CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022, issued under section 70B of the Information Technology Act 2000, require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations to report specified types of cyber incident to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them or being notified about them. The six hours run from noticing, not from completing the investigation. A SOC serving an Indian organisation should therefore have an internal target that hands the facts to whoever files the report (often legal or compliance) well inside that window, for instance within two hours of confirmation, so that the report can be prepared and approved in time. The handover note must state when such a clock started.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 adds a separate obligation: in the event of a personal data breach, the data fiduciary must inform the Data Protection Board of India and each affected data principal, in the form and manner prescribed under the Act. The SOC does not usually make that decision, but its notification SLA to the customer's privacy or legal team determines how early the decision can be made.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> An MSSP's SLA does not move the customer's regulatory obligation. If the provider takes five hours to notify, the customer has one hour left to report to CERT-In. Contracts should state notification targets that leave the customer enough time to meet its own legal deadlines.

## Worked example: checking SLA compliance from case data

The course lab kit includes `LAB_ASSETS/C08/soc_metrics.py`, which holds five synthetic case records, each with a created time, an acknowledged time, a resolved time and a disposition (TP for true positive, FP for false positive, BTP for benign true positive). Run unchanged, it prints the summary metrics that Lesson 7 examines in detail:

```terminal
$ python soc_metrics.py
MTTA mean 32.4 min, median 4.0 min
MTTR mean 90.0 min, median 20.0 min
false-positive share 40%
```

MTTA is mean time to acknowledge and MTTR mean time to resolve. These are aggregates; an SLA, by contrast, is checked case by case against a target. To turn the same data into an SLA check, copy the script to `sla_check.py` and append these lines, which reuse its `CASES` list and its computed `ack` list of acknowledgement times in minutes:

```python
ACK_TARGET = 15  # minutes - illustrative SLA target
met = [m <= ACK_TARGET for m in ack]
print(f"acknowledged within {ACK_TARGET} min: {sum(met)}/{len(met)} ({sum(met)/len(met):.0%})")
for (c, a, r, d), m in zip(CASES, ack):
    if m > ACK_TARGET:
        print(f"BREACH created {c} acknowledged {a} after {m:.0f} min, disposition {d}")
```

`ACK_TARGET` is the Critical acknowledgement target from the Banyan matrix. `met` is a list of booleans, one per case, and `sum(met)` counts the `True` values. The `:.0%` format prints the share as a whole-number percentage. The loop pairs each case with its acknowledgement time and prints any case that missed the target. Running it:

```terminal
$ python sla_check.py
MTTA mean 32.4 min, median 4.0 min
MTTR mean 90.0 min, median 20.0 min
false-positive share 40%
acknowledged within 15 min: 4/5 (80%)
BREACH created 2026-11-03T09:00 acknowledged 2026-11-03T11:30 after 150 min, disposition TP
```

The interpretation is the important part. The median acknowledgement time of 4.0 minutes looks excellent, and four of five cases met the target. But the single breach is the case that mattered most: a confirmed true positive that waited two and a half hours. An SLA report that said only "80% compliance" would hide this; a report that lists breaches with their dispositions exposes it immediately. This is why SLA reporting should always include the breached cases themselves, with reason codes, and not only the percentage.

> [!TIP]
> Treat every breach on a true positive as a review item, even when overall compliance is high. A SOC can meet its SLA on 99% of alerts and still miss the one intrusion.

## How SLAs go wrong

SLAs change behaviour, and not always in the intended direction. Goodhart's law, often paraphrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure", describes the risk well.

**Acknowledging without acting.** If only time to acknowledge is measured, analysts learn to click "assign to me" on every new alert and look at it later. The timer is met; the alert still waits. Measuring time to triage as well, and reviewing samples, counters this.

**Closing to stop the clock.** If time to resolve carries penalties, there is pressure to close cases early, for example as false positives, and reopen them later or never. This is the most dangerous distortion, because it turns real alerts into "noise". Quality reviews of closed cases, and treating reopened cases as a quality failure, are the controls.

**Severity games.** Starting a case at a lower severity to get a longer deadline, or downgrading it near a breach, defeats the matrix. The reclassification rule described earlier, stricter target measured from original creation, removes the incentive.

**Abuse of pauses.** Setting cases to "awaiting customer" without a real request stops clocks artificially. Pauses should require a recorded outbound request and end automatically on reply.

**Green dashboards, unhappy customers.** An SLA report can show every target met while the customer feels poorly served, because the SLA measures the wrong things, for instance speed of acknowledgement rather than quality of investigation. Practitioners sometimes call this a "watermelon" SLA: green outside, red inside. Regular service reviews that discuss real cases, not just numbers, keep the agreement honest.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"The SLA is the same as the metrics."** The SLA is the commitment and its per-case targets; metrics are aggregates that describe performance over time. Both are needed, and Lesson 7 covers the second.

**Undefined start and stop events.** "Respond within 30 minutes" is not measurable until "respond" is defined. Write the events down.

**Ignoring the chain beneath the SLA.** If the SOC depends on another team or vendor to act, that dependency needs an OLA or underpinning contract, or the SLA promises something the SOC cannot deliver.

**Business-hour timers for critical work.** A critical alert at 23:00 that is not due until the next morning is an attacker's gift.

**Reporting only percentages.** Always list breached cases with dispositions and reasons.

## Security implications, detection and defence

SLAs are a detection control for the SOC itself. Timers that warn and breach make inaction visible, and breach alerts routed to the shift lead and on-call manager ensure that someone senior knows when a high-severity case is drifting. The 2 a.m. handover gap of Lesson 5 would have been caught by a breach alert on an unacknowledged critical case.

SLA records are evidence. In a post-incident review or a contractual dispute, the timestamps of creation, acknowledgement, escalation and notification show what the SOC did and when. They should be generated by the case system, not typed by hand, kept with the case records, and protected from editing. Because those records contain personal data about users and affected individuals, access to them follows the same controls as the cases themselves.

Finally, SLAs must be realistic. Targets that the staffing level cannot meet produce constant breaches, which teach everyone to ignore breach alerts. Set targets from measured alert volumes and staffing, revisit them after major changes, and fix noisy detections that consume analyst time.

## Best practices

- Define scope, hours, severities, targets, escalation and reporting in writing.
- For every target, state the start event, stop event and any permitted pause.
- Use calendar-hour timers for Critical and High work in a 24x7 SOC.
- Apply the stricter target from original creation time when severity is raised.
- Warn before breach and alert the shift lead at breach.
- Set internal notification targets well inside regulatory deadlines such as CERT-In's six hours.
- Support every SLA with OLAs and underpinning contracts for the teams and vendors it depends on.
- Report breached cases individually with reason codes, and pair speed targets with quality reviews.

## Key takeaways

- An SLA turns "we monitor" into specific commitments: scope, hours, severity, targets, escalation and reporting.
- Every timer needs a defined start event, stop event and pause rule, recorded in UTC from synchronised clocks.
- Severity selects the deadline, so severity definitions and reclassification rules are part of the SLA.
- Internal targets must sit well inside regulatory clocks such as CERT-In's six hours from noticing.
- Percent compliance can hide the breach that matters; the lab data's single breach was a true positive that waited 150 minutes.
- SLAs shape behaviour; pair every speed target with quality review to stop gaming.
