## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Lesson 3 followed an alert through its lifecycle. This lesson follows the person who moves it along: the analyst on shift. The **analyst workflow** is the repeatable pattern of work that an analyst follows from the moment they sit down to the moment they hand over, and the habits that make each decision fast, consistent and reviewable. It is less glamorous than detection engineering or threat hunting, but it is where most SOC outcomes are actually decided. A brilliant detection rule is worth nothing if the analyst who receives its alert at 03:00 is working from stale information, skips the related alerts or writes a note nobody else can follow.

A workflow exists for the same reason a pilot uses a checklist. Analysts are skilled, but they work long shifts, under interruption, against a queue that never empties, often at night. Under those conditions people skip steps, anchor on first impressions and forget what they checked an hour ago. A defined workflow does not replace judgement; it protects judgement by taking routine decisions off the analyst's mind so that attention is free for the hard ones. The course text summarises the principle as "follow playbooks but think".

The workflow has three phases. **Start of shift** establishes situational awareness: what is already open, what changed, and whether the SOC can currently see what it is supposed to see. **During the shift** the analyst works the queue by priority, handles each alert with a consistent loop, documents as they go and escalates early when impact may be high. **End of shift** packages open work for the next person. This lesson covers the first two phases in depth and introduces the third; Lesson 5 is devoted to shift handover, Lesson 6 to the service levels that set time limits, and Module 4 to the detailed technique of triage.

The worked examples use the course lab kit in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/`: `minisiem.py` for health checks and searches, `triage.py` for the work queue, and `evidence_export.py` for preserving case evidence with a hash and a custody record.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Shift | A defined working period with named analysts responsible for the queue; 24x7 SOCs run rotating shifts. |
| Situational awareness | A current understanding of open cases, recent changes, threats and the health of the monitoring itself. |
| Log-source health | Whether each expected source is still sending events at its normal rate and with correct parsing and timestamps. |
| Change calendar | The record of approved changes (deployments, maintenance, tests) that may explain otherwise suspicious activity. |
| Acknowledge | Taking ownership of an alert in the case system, which starts the clock on handling it. |
| Time box | A planned maximum time for a first decision before escalating or asking for help. |
| Case note | The running, timestamped record of what an analyst checked, found and decided on a case. |
| Chain of custody | A record of who collected, handled and stored an item of evidence, when, and how its integrity was protected. |
| Escalation note | A concise hand-off message, attached to the case, that lets the receiver act without re-investigating. |

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram represents one analyst's shift as a procedure. It matters because it separates the work an analyst does once per shift (orientation and handover) from the loop they repeat dozens of times (pick, handle, document, decide), and shows that documentation is a step inside the loop rather than an afterthought at the end.

**Components.** There are seven steps, top to bottom. **Read the handover** (slate) is the inherited context from the previous shift. **Check SOC health** (violet) covers log-source health, open cases, threat intelligence and the change calendar. **Pick and acknowledge** (blue) is taking the highest-priority, oldest alert and becoming its owner. **Handle the alert** (orange) is the per-alert analysis loop: read the rule, scope the entities, enrich, validate against raw events and check related alerts. **Document as you go** (green) is writing the case note while working. **Decide** (red diamond) is the moment of disposition or escalation, with its three outcomes in its sub-label. **Write the handover** (dark) ends the shift. A dashed arrow labelled **next alert** runs from **Decide** back to **Pick and acknowledge**, and an arrow labelled **shift ends** leads to the handover.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each downward arrow means "this step's output is the next step's input". The handover and health checks feed the alert-handling steps with context: an approved change on the calendar can turn an alarming alert into a benign true positive, and a silent log source tells the analyst that "no evidence found" may mean "no evidence collected". The dashed **next alert** arrow shows that deciding on one alert immediately leads to picking the next; only **shift ends** leaves the loop.

**Execution flow.** At the start of the shift the analyst reads the handover, then checks health. From then on the loop repeats: pick and acknowledge, handle, document, decide, return to the queue. When the shift ends the analyst leaves the loop and writes the handover, which becomes the first step of the next analyst's shift.

**Decision logic.** The **Decide** step has three outcomes, listed in its sub-label: close with a disposition, keep investigating within the time box, or escalate now. Escalation can also happen in the middle of handling, the moment the analyst sees that impact may be high; waiting until the analysis is complete is a common and costly error.

**Technical interpretation.** In a case management system each step leaves a trace: acknowledging writes an owner and timestamp, documenting adds notes and evidence links, deciding changes the state. A supervisor can reconstruct an analyst's shift from those traces, which is what makes the work reviewable.

**Practical example.** In the Banyan lab, an analyst who starts the shift with a source-health query notices that the last Sysmon event is at 06:14. They pick R5 (`log_cleared`, priority 10.8), see that it shares WS-104 with R1, R3, R4 and R6, record each query in the case note, and escalate CASE-0001 rather than closing alerts one by one.

**Security implications.** Skipping the health check lets blind spots go unnoticed; skipping documentation makes decisions unreviewable; skipping the loop's related-alert check lets slow intrusions through.

**Key learning.** Orient once, then repeat the same loop for every alert, writing the case note inside the loop, and leave a handover that starts the next shift's loop correctly.

## Start of shift: orientation before the queue

It is tempting to start a shift by opening the queue and taking the top alert. Experienced analysts resist that for ten or fifteen minutes, because the queue does not tell you what you cannot see. Orientation has four parts.

### Read the handover and the open cases

The previous shift's handover lists open cases, decisions pending, ongoing incidents, platform issues and notable intelligence. Read it fully, then open the cases it mentions and confirm their state in the case system rather than trusting the summary. Note anything that is now yours: a pending user confirmation, a containment action awaiting approval, a host the previous analyst asked to keep watching. Lesson 5 covers how to write and receive a handover well.

### Check that the SOC can see

Monitoring is only as good as its data. A source that has stopped sending events produces no alerts, and an empty queue for that source looks exactly like a quiet night. A start-of-shift health check asks, for every important source: is it still sending, at about its usual rate, with current timestamps and correct parsing? In minisiem, a single query gives a first view:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, COUNT(*) AS n, MIN(ts) AS first_seen, MAX(ts) AS last_seen FROM events GROUP BY source ORDER BY n DESC"
source | n | first_seen | last_seen
proxy | 284 | 2026-11-03T03:19:00Z | 2026-11-03T12:32:00Z
sysmon | 33 | 2026-11-03T03:13:00Z | 2026-11-03T06:14:00Z
windows | 13 | 2026-11-03T03:07:00Z | 2026-11-03T08:17:00Z
vpn | 10 | 2026-11-03T06:42:00Z | 2026-11-03T13:33:00Z
```

The query groups the loaded events by `source` and reports the count and the earliest and latest timestamp for each. Read the `last_seen` column first. In this dataset the most recent Sysmon event is at 06:14, the time of the R1 alert, while proxy and VPN events continue past midday. In a real SOC that gap would be a question to answer straight away: was endpoint logging stopped, did the host go offline, is the collector broken, or is this simply how the dataset was generated? The analyst does not know yet, and should not guess; the point is that a health check surfaces the question, and the answer changes how much weight "no Sysmon evidence after 06:14" can carry in any investigation. Production SIEMs have dedicated health dashboards for this (Lesson 8 and Module 3), but the question is always the same.

### Check intelligence and the change calendar

Two other sources change how alerts should be read. **Threat intelligence** updates (Module 5) may add indicators to watch or warn of a campaign against the sector, which raises the priority of matching alerts. The **change calendar** lists approved work: patching windows, software deployments, administrator account creations, scheduled penetration tests. An alert for a new local administrator at 07:02 means something very different if a change ticket says IT was building that account at 07:00. Recording the change reference is what makes a benign true positive defensible.

### Set the shift's priorities

Finally, decide what this shift must achieve beyond the queue: an incident call to join, a pending escalation to chase, a case due for closure. Writing these down at the start makes it less likely that they are lost under a busy queue.

## During the shift: working the queue

### Picking the next alert

The default rule is simple: take the highest-priority alert, and among equal priorities the oldest. In the Banyan lab, `LAB_ASSETS/C08/triage.py` produces exactly such a queue:

```terminal
$ python triage.py
10.8  R5 log_cleared             host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 7.2  R2 vpn_spray_then_success  host=WS-107 (sales laptop) user=gopal (standard) ip=external (documentation range in this lab)
 7.2  R1 office_spawns_script    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 4.5  R4 large_upload            host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 3.6  R6 beacon_regular_small    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=None (?) ip=n/a
```

The analyst takes R5 and **acknowledges** it, which makes them the owner and records the time. Two refinements apply. First, before starting, glance down the queue for alerts that share entities with the one you picked. Here five other lines name WS-104, so the analyst opens a case for the host and works them together rather than leaving them for colleagues to pick up separately. Second, the score is a starting point, not an order to obey: an analyst who sees a low-priority alert that fits a pattern they are already investigating takes it, and says why in the note.

### The per-alert loop

Every alert, simple or complex, gets the same short loop. Module 4 teaches each step in depth; at the level of workflow the loop is:

1. **Read the alert and the rule.** What does this detection look for, and what exactly matched? If you are unsure what a rule means, read its logic. R1 in `minisiem.py`, for example, matches a Sysmon process-creation event whose parent image contains `winword.exe` and whose image is PowerShell or `cmd.exe`.
2. **Scope the entities.** List the host, user, IP addresses, process, file and URL involved.
3. **Enrich.** Who is this user, what is this host, is this address internal or external? `triage.py` adds this from its asset and identity tables: WS-104 is a finance workstation, `divya` is a payments approver.
4. **Validate.** Look at the raw events around the alert and ask what else would be true if the alert were real. Is it there?
5. **Check related alerts** on the same entities over recent days.
6. **Decide**: close with a disposition, continue investigating, or escalate.
7. **Document** what you checked, what you found and why you decided, while you do it.

The loop is short on purpose. It can take two minutes for an obvious false positive or an hour for a complex case, but its steps do not change, which makes analysts' work comparable and reviewable.

### Time boxes and early escalation

An unclear alert can absorb an analyst for hours while the rest of the queue waits. A **time box** is a planned limit for reaching a first decision; the course text notes that many SOCs aim for a first decision within roughly 15 to 30 minutes, with figures varying by organisation. When the time box expires without clarity, the analyst escalates or asks for help rather than continuing alone.

Escalation timing deserves its own rule: **escalate early when impact may be high**. An analyst who sees signs of ransomware precursors, a privileged account compromise or data leaving a critical system should not finish the analysis before raising the alarm. Escalating an alert that later turns out benign costs a colleague some minutes; escalating a real intrusion an hour late can cost the organisation its file servers. Good SOC culture rewards early escalation even when it proves unnecessary.

### Following playbooks, and thinking

A **playbook** documents how to handle a type of alert: what to check, what evidence to collect, when and to whom to escalate. Playbooks make Tier 1 work consistent and let new analysts work safely. They are also written in advance by people who could not foresee every situation. The course's fictional teaching scenario makes the point: a Tier 1 analyst suspects ransomware precursors at 03:00, but the playbook says "escalate during business hours". The right action is to escalate immediately through the emergency on-call path, documenting the evidence, and afterwards raise a playbook change so that escalation timing depends on severity. Playbooks support judgement; they must never block an urgent response.

## Documentation as you go

The case note is the analyst's most important output. It is what the next analyst reads at handover, what an incident responder relies on after escalation, what a reviewer samples for quality, and what may be produced in a disciplinary hearing or a legal process months later. Notes written at the end of the shift from memory are less accurate and less complete than notes written while working, so the workflow puts documentation inside the loop.

Four habits make a note defensible:

- **UTC timestamps** for every observation and action. Local times create confusion across sites and daylight-saving changes; the lab data and tools use UTC throughout.
- **Exact queries**, copied as run, so that anyone can reproduce the result. "Checked the proxy logs" cannot be reproduced; the SQL or SIEM query text can.
- **Evidence references**: event identifiers, exported files and their hashes, ticket numbers, the names of people consulted.
- **Facts separated from hypotheses.** "WS-104 made 18 requests of 220 bytes to cdn-telemetry-sync.example" is a fact. "This is command-and-control beaconing" is an assessment, and should be labelled as such with the reasoning.

**Relationships.** Each line on the left has a counterpart on the right. "10:30 checked proxy" becomes a UTC timestamp plus the query text. "Looks like beaconing" is split into the fact (18 requests, 220 bytes each, to one URL) and a labelled assessment. "Escalated to T2" becomes an escalation through the case system with an acknowledgement time.

**Execution flow.** The defensible note is written line by line as the analyst works: the query is pasted when it is run, the result when it returns, the assessment when it is formed, the export reference when the export completes, and the escalation when it is acknowledged. Nothing depends on memory.

**Decision logic.** The note makes the analyst's reasoning visible: the facts on the right are what justify the escalation decision. A reviewer can disagree with the assessment and still rely on the facts.

**Technical interpretation.** The query text, the evidence file name and the hash link the note to reproducible artefacts. Anyone with the same data can rerun the search and verify the exported file against its `SHA256SUMS` entry.

**Practical example.** A Tier 2 responder receiving the weak note would need to repeat the whole investigation. Receiving the defensible note, they can start containment within minutes, because they know what was found, where the evidence is and what is still only a hypothesis.

**Security implications.** Weak notes lead to repeated work, lost evidence and wrong conclusions inherited across shifts. They also cannot show that an alert was closed for a good reason, which hides both honest mistakes and deliberate misuse.

**Key learning.** Write the note while you work: UTC times, exact queries, facts separated from assessments, evidence references and acknowledged escalations.

## Preserving evidence during the shift

SIEM data does not last forever. Retention periods, storage limits and re-indexing can remove or change the raw events an investigation depends on. When a case may lead to an incident report, a disciplinary process or a legal or regulatory action, the analyst preserves the relevant events at the time, with an integrity check and a custody record. The lab kit's `LAB_ASSETS/C08/evidence_export.py` shows the pattern in miniature:

```terminal
$ python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-104
exported 32 events -> evidence\CASE-0001\WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
sha256 8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf
```

The two arguments are the case identifier and the host. The script selects the raw JSON of every event for that host between its default window of 06:00 and 09:00 UTC on 3 November 2026, writes them to a file under `evidence/CASE-0001/`, computes the file's SHA-256 digest, writes a `SHA256SUMS` manifest and appends a line to `custody.jsonl`. The output path uses Windows separators because the run shown was on Windows. The custody entry records the case, item, digest, number of events, the exact query parameters, the action, the actor and the UTC time of the export:

```json
{"case": "CASE-0001", "item": "WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl", "sha256": "8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf", "events": 32, "query": {"host": "WS-104", "start": "2026-11-03T06:00:00Z", "end": "2026-11-03T09:00:00Z"}, "action": "exported from minisiem", "by": "analyst.t2", "utc": "2026-11-14T22:18:15Z"}
```

The `utc` field is the time the export was run, so yours will differ; the digest will match only if your dataset is identical. Anyone can later confirm that the file is unchanged by running `sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS` in that folder, which prints `WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl: OK` when the digest matches. Module 7 covers evidence handling in depth; at workflow level the habit is simply to export early and reference the export in the case note.

## Escalating well

An escalation is a hand-off of ownership, and its quality decides how quickly the receiver can act. A good escalation note answers, in a few lines: what was detected and when (UTC), which entities are affected and how critical they are, what evidence supports the assessment (with references), what has already been done, what is recommended, and what is still unknown. For the Banyan main case, a note might read: "CASE-0001: WS-104 (finance workstation, user divya, payments approver). Word started hidden encoded PowerShell at 06:14 after invoice.docm was downloaded from banyan-payroll-update.example at 06:12; fixed 220-byte requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example from 06:17; local account svc_update created and added to Administrators 07:02 to 07:03; 48.2 MB upload to files-share-drop.example 08:12; Security log cleared 08:17. Evidence exported with hash and custody record. Assessment: likely compromise with possible data exfiltration. Recommend host isolation and account review. Unknown: link to VPN spray on gopal (R2)."

The escalation is complete when the receiver acknowledges it in the case system. Until then the original analyst still owns the alert, and if acknowledgement does not arrive within the agreed time they follow the escalation path to the next contact or the on-call lead.

## Attention, fatigue and bias

Analysts are the SOC's most important instrument, and like any instrument they drift. Three effects are worth naming because a workflow can counter them.

**Anchoring** is over-weighting the first piece of information. An analyst who reads "low priority" or a reassuring rule title may stop looking too early. The related-alert check in the loop, and the habit of asking "what else would be true if this were real?", counter it.

**Confirmation bias** is seeking evidence that supports a hypothesis already formed. Separating facts from assessments in the case note, and writing down what evidence would refute the assessment, keeps alternative explanations alive.

**Alert fatigue** is the dulling that comes from hundreds of similar, mostly benign alerts. It leads to fast closures of exactly the alert that mattered. The defences are organisational (tuning noisy rules, rotating duties, reasonable shift lengths, breaks) as much as individual, and analysts should report rules that are wearing them down so that the feedback stage of the lifecycle can act.

Interruptions compound all three. When an analyst is pulled away mid-alert, a note that records "where I am and what I was about to check" lets them, or someone else, resume without starting again.

## End of shift

The shift ends with a handover: open cases and their state, decisions pending, ongoing incidents, platform issues such as the silent log source found during orientation, and notable intelligence. The handover is written into the case system or the SOC's handover log, not delivered only in conversation, and the incoming analyst confirms receipt. Lesson 5 is devoted to doing this well; the workflow point is that the handover closes the loop of the shift diagram above by becoming the next shift's first step.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Starting with the queue instead of orientation.** Without the handover and health check, the analyst cannot tell a quiet source from a broken one.
- **Working alerts one at a time** without checking related alerts on the same entities.
- **Finishing the analysis before escalating** a potentially high-impact alert.
- **Treating the playbook as a ceiling.** Playbooks are minimum steps, not a limit on judgement.
- **Writing notes at the end of the shift** from memory, without query text or evidence references.
- **Mixing facts and assessments**, so that the next reader inherits a guess as if it were an observation.
- **Escalating by chat or phone only**, with no change of owner in the case system.

## Security implications, detection and defence

The analyst workflow is a control, and its failures are security failures. Unacknowledged critical alerts, silent log sources and escalations without acknowledgement are all detectable: the case system can alert when a high-severity alert stays unowned beyond its service level (Lesson 6), the SIEM can alert when a source's last event is older than its expected interval, and the escalation process can require acknowledgement within a fixed time before automatically paging the next contact. Quality reviews that sample closed alerts against the four documentation habits catch both careless closures and deliberate misuse.

Analysts also handle sensitive data: user identities, browsing records, email content. Searches should be limited to what the case needs, exported evidence must be stored with access controls, and personal data must be handled consistently with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and the organisation's own policies. Everything in this lesson's lab uses synthetic data, but the same discipline applies to real cases from the first day on the job.

> [!CAUTION]
> Analysts sometimes have powerful tools: SIEM-wide search, EDR live response, account controls. Use them only for authorised work on assigned cases, and record what you did. Searching a colleague's activity out of curiosity, or taking a response action outside your authority, is misuse even if the tool allows it.

## Best practices

- Spend the first minutes of every shift on orientation: handover, open cases, log-source health, intelligence, change calendar.
- Acknowledge before working, and check the queue for alerts sharing entities with the one you picked.
- Apply the same short loop to every alert, inside a time box, and escalate early when impact may be high.
- Follow playbooks but think, and raise a playbook change when one gets in the way.
- Document as you go: UTC times, exact queries, evidence references, facts separate from assessments.
- Preserve evidence early with a hash and custody record when a case may go further.
- Write escalation notes that let the receiver act without re-investigating, and confirm acknowledgement.

## Practical application (authorised lab only)

This exercise uses only the synthetic Banyan dataset on your own computer.

1. In your copy of `LAB_ASSETS/C08/`, run `python minisiem.py load` (expect `loaded 340 events`).
2. **Orientation:** run the source-health query from this lesson. Write two sentences on what the `last_seen` column tells you and what you would ask about the Sysmon source.
3. **Queue:** run `python triage.py`. Acknowledge (on paper) the top alert and list every other alert that shares an entity with it.
4. **Loop:** for R1, write down each of the seven loop steps and what you found, including the exact query you ran with `python minisiem.py search "..."`.
5. **Evidence:** run `python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-104`, then verify the export with `sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS` inside `evidence/CASE-0001/` (on Windows without that tool, compute the digest with `certutil -hashfile <file> SHA256` or PowerShell's `Get-FileHash` and compare it by eye).
6. **Note:** write a defensible case note for CASE-0001 using the four habits, then an escalation note of no more than eight lines.
7. **Reflection:** which step would you be most tempted to skip at 03:00, and what would make it easier not to?

Troubleshooting: `no such table` means you have not run `load` in the same folder. Re-running `evidence_export.py` appends a second custody line and overwrites the export, which is correct behaviour for this teaching tool; a production system would refuse to overwrite. Cleanup: delete `minisiem.db` and the `evidence/` folder if you wish.

## Key takeaways

- The analyst workflow has three phases: orientation at the start of the shift, a repeated per-alert loop, and a handover at the end.
- Orientation includes checking that the SOC can see: a silent log source looks like a quiet night unless you check `last_seen`.
- Every alert gets the same loop (read, scope, enrich, validate, check related, decide, document) inside a time box, with early escalation when impact may be high.
- Playbooks support judgement and must not block urgent response.
- Document as you go with UTC times, exact queries, evidence references and facts separated from assessments; preserve evidence with a hash and custody record.
- Escalation is complete only when the receiver acknowledges it in the case system.
