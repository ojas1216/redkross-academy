## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A security operations centre that promises round-the-clock monitoring is making a promise that no single person can keep. Analysts work shifts, and every shift ends. At each boundary the SOC's entire working memory, everything the outgoing team knows about what is happening right now, has to move from one group of people to another. That transfer is the **shift handover**, and it is one of the most fragile moments in security operations. Detection rules do not stop firing at 11:00 UTC, attackers do not pause because the night team is logging off, and a half-finished investigation does not remember itself.

Lesson 4 described the analyst's day: reading the handover at the start of a shift, working the queue by priority, and writing a handover at the end. This lesson looks at the handover itself as an operational control. It asks what exactly must be transferred, how the transfer should be carried out so that nothing is lost, how responsibility for a case moves from one person to another, and how a SOC can tell whether its handovers are working. The detailed end-of-shift report that a Tier 2 analyst writes for leadership is practised in the capstone (Module 12); here the focus is the mechanism that every shift, quiet or busy, depends on.

The core idea is simple to state. A handover succeeds when the incoming shift can act on everything that matters **without having to ask the outgoing shift anything**, because by the time a question occurs to them, the person who knows the answer may be asleep. A handover fails when knowledge stays in someone's head, in a private chat message or in a browser tab that is about to be closed.

The course's fictional teaching scenario "the 2 a.m. handover gap" shows the failure in its purest form. At a managed security service provider (MSSP) serving a fictional retailer, the night analyst spotted what looked like a ransomware precursor and escalated it by chat to a colleague who had already gone home. No case was opened. Nobody on the morning shift knew the alert existed, and they arrived to find encrypted file servers. Nothing about the detection failed: the rule fired and a capable analyst recognised its meaning. What failed was the transfer of knowledge and responsibility. The fixes the scenario lists are all handover controls: escalation only through the case system with acknowledgement, on-call rotas, handover checklists, and alerts on unacknowledged critical cases. This lesson explains each of them.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A handover is not a courtesy between colleagues. It is the point at which accountability for live security cases changes hands. Treat it with the same discipline as an escalation: written, timestamped, acknowledged.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Shift handover | The structured transfer of situational awareness, open work and responsibility from an outgoing shift to an incoming shift. |
| Outgoing / incoming shift | The team finishing its shift and the team taking over. |
| Handover note | The written record of the handover, published in the case or ticketing system and timestamped in UTC. |
| Overlap | A period during which both shifts are on duty, used for the verbal briefing and questions. |
| Read-back | The incoming analyst restating the key points in their own words so that the outgoing analyst can correct misunderstandings. |
| Case ownership | The named person accountable for the next action on a case at a given moment. |
| Situational awareness | Understanding of what is happening now, what it means, and what is likely to happen next. |
| Follow-the-sun | A coverage model in which SOC teams in different time zones each work their local daytime and hand over to the next region. |
| On-call | A named person, outside the staffed shift, who can be reached for urgent escalations according to a rota. |
| Platform health | Whether the SOC's own tools are working: log sources reporting, ingestion delays, parsing errors, connector failures. |

## Why handovers fail

It is worth understanding the failure modes before designing a process, because each element of a good handover exists to block one of them.

### Knowledge that lives only in heads

During a shift an analyst builds up a great deal of context that never reaches the case record: "I already checked that IP and it is the backup provider", "the user rang to say she was travelling", "I suspect these three alerts are the same thing". If the analyst writes only conclusions, or writes nothing until the case is closed, that context leaves the building at the end of the shift. The incoming analyst then either repeats the work, wasting time, or skips it and reaches a wrong conclusion. The defence is the documentation habit from Lesson 4, document as you go, so that the handover is a summary of records that already exist rather than an act of memory.

### Fatigue and time pressure at the end of a shift

The end of a shift is exactly when analysts are most tired and most eager to leave. Handovers written in the last five minutes are short, vague and missing the awkward items, such as the case the analyst could not resolve. A shift structure that reserves time for the handover, and a template that prompts for every section, counteracts this. Many SOCs make the outgoing shift's last 20 to 30 minutes handover time in which new low-priority alerts are left for the incoming shift, while high-severity alerts are still worked by whoever is best placed.

### Ambiguous ownership

The most dangerous gap is the one in which each shift believes the other is handling a case. The outgoing analyst thinks "I mentioned it, so the morning team has it"; the incoming analyst thinks "it was not assigned to me, so someone else must own it". The fix is mechanical: ownership changes only when the case system shows a new named owner and that owner has acknowledged it. Mentioning a case in a meeting or a chat does not transfer it.

### Channels that do not persist

Chat, phone calls and hallway conversations are excellent for speed and terrible for records. A message in a direct chat cannot be seen by the rest of the shift, does not trigger any alerting if it is ignored, and is hard to find during a later review. Chat can be used to say "please look at CASE-1042 now", but the case itself, with its evidence and its status, must live in the case management system, which is the SOC's single source of truth.

### Platform problems that nobody mentions

A SOC can miss an attack not because the analysts failed but because a log source silently stopped sending data three hours ago. If the outgoing shift noticed that the VPN logs looked thin and did not say so, the incoming shift will interpret the quiet as safety. Platform health is therefore a standing section of every handover, even when the answer is "all sources ingesting normally".

## Shift patterns and where handovers occur

How many handovers a SOC performs, and how risky each one is, depends on its coverage model.

**Fixed shifts in one location.** A common pattern is three eight-hour shifts (for example 03:00-11:00, 11:00-19:00 and 19:00-03:00 UTC) or two twelve-hour shifts. Three shifts give three handovers a day; two shifts give two handovers but longer, more tiring shifts. In India a SOC aligned to IST might run 08:30-16:30 IST as its day shift, which is 03:00-11:00 UTC; recording both times avoids confusion when the SOC serves clients in other regions.

**Follow-the-sun.** Large organisations and managed providers sometimes place teams in several regions, for example Asia, Europe and the Americas, so that every team works local daytime hours. This reduces night work, but every handover now crosses a language, culture and time-zone boundary, and the teams may never meet. Written handover quality becomes even more important, and the overlap period is often a scheduled video call.

**Business hours plus on-call.** Smaller in-house teams often staff the SOC only during business hours and rely on an on-call rota (or an MSSP) for the rest. Here the handover happens twice: from the day team to the night coverage, and back again. The night coverage must know which cases are live and what authority it has; the morning team must know what happened overnight.

Whatever the pattern, the boundaries should be scheduled so that they do not coincide with predictable busy periods. A handover at the moment most staff log in, when authentication alerts spike, is harder than one an hour later. An **overlap** of 15 to 30 minutes, during which both shifts are on duty, is the single most valuable design choice: it allows the verbal briefing, read-back and questions to happen while the outgoing analyst is still available.

> [!NOTE]
> The time values in this lesson (overlap length, reserved handover time) are typical practice, not a standard. Each SOC sets its own and records them in its operating procedures.

## The handover procedure

A reliable handover has a fixed shape. It separates the **written** transfer, which is the durable record, from the **verbal** transfer, which catches misunderstandings, and it ends with an explicit **transfer of ownership** that is visible in the case system. The steps below describe that shape; Figure 1 shows the same exchange as a sequence between the people and the system involved.

### Step 1: bring every open case up to date

Before writing anything new, the outgoing analyst makes sure that each case they own states, in its own record, the current status, what has been checked, what has not, and the next action. This is the most important step, because a handover note that says "see CASE-1042" is only useful if CASE-1042 itself is complete. An analyst who has documented as they worked spends a few minutes here; one who has not spends much longer, which is why the habit matters.

### Step 2: publish the handover note

The outgoing analyst (or the shift lead, for the whole shift) writes the handover note in the case or ticketing system, stamped in UTC. It summarises the shift using a fixed template, covered in detail later in this lesson: headline first, then active incidents, open cases and their deadlines, pending decisions, platform health, relevant intelligence and changes, and actions with owners. The note links to cases rather than repeating them.

### Step 3: the incoming shift reads before it talks

The incoming analysts read the note and look at the queue **before** the verbal briefing starts. This turns the briefing from a monologue into a conversation: the incoming analyst arrives with questions, and the outgoing analyst spends the overlap on what is unclear rather than reading the note aloud.

### Step 4: verbal briefing, highest risk first

During the overlap the outgoing analyst briefs the incoming one, starting with the most serious item and with anything that is time-critical. Structured communication formats from other safety-critical fields are useful models. Healthcare uses **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to keep a briefing focused, and the **I-PASS** handoff method ends with "synthesis by receiver", in which the person taking over summarises what they have heard. A SOC equivalent for each live case is: what is happening, how we got here, what we think it means, and what should be done next.

### Step 5: read-back

The incoming analyst restates the key points in their own words: "So CASE-1042 is a suspected credential-stuffing run against the customer portal; you have blocked the two source ranges, the account owners have not yet been contacted, and the identity team owes us a reply by 06:00 UTC." The outgoing analyst corrects anything wrong. Read-back is cheap and catches the misunderstandings that a written note cannot, because the writer cannot see how the reader interprets it.

### Step 6: reassign and acknowledge ownership

Each open case is reassigned in the case system to a named incoming analyst, who acknowledges it. Until that acknowledgement exists, the outgoing analyst still owns the case. This step is what would have prevented the 2 a.m. handover gap: a chat message cannot be acknowledged in the case system, so it cannot transfer ownership.

### Step 7: shift lead confirms coverage

The shift lead checks that every open case has an acknowledged owner, that the on-call rota for the coming shift is correct, and that any platform issue has been raised with the team that fixes it. The lead then records the handover as complete.

**Purpose.** The figure shows one shift handover as a sequence of messages between four parties, read from top to bottom in time order. It matters because it makes visible the point at which responsibility actually moves, which in a badly run SOC is never defined at all.

**Components.** There are four lanes. **Outgoing analyst** (sub-label "night shift, owns open cases") is the person whose shift is ending. **Case system** (sub-label "single source of truth") is dark because it is the trust anchor: every durable fact in the handover is written there. **Incoming analyst** (sub-label "day shift") is the person taking over. **Shift lead** (sub-label "confirms coverage") is the supervisor who closes the handover.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows are actions or briefings; dashed arrows are replies or confirmations. The first two arrows, **Update each open case: status, next step** and **Publish handover note (UTC)**, both point from the outgoing analyst into the case system, showing that the written record is created before anyone talks. The arrow **Read note and queue** points from the incoming analyst to the case system: the incoming shift pulls the information itself. **Verbal briefing: highest risk first** goes from outgoing to incoming analyst, and the dashed **Read-back and questions** returns in the opposite direction, which is the error-correction loop. **Reassign case owners** goes from the outgoing analyst to the case system, and the dashed **Acknowledge ownership** goes from the incoming analyst to the case system. Finally **Confirm every case owned; sign off** goes from the shift lead to the case system.

**Execution flow.** Records first, reading second, talking third, ownership fourth, sign-off last. Reversing the first and third steps, talking before writing, is the most common way handovers degrade, because the verbal briefing then becomes the only record.

**Decision logic.** The diagram has one implicit gate. Ownership has moved only when the **Acknowledge ownership** reply exists. If it does not, the case still belongs to the outgoing analyst, and the shift lead's sign-off should not happen. Many case systems can enforce this by refusing to close a handover while any case is unassigned or unacknowledged.

**Technical interpretation.** In a case management platform, reassignment and acknowledgement are separate events with separate timestamps. That gives the SOC an audit trail: for any case, it can show who owned it at any moment and how long it sat between reassignment and acknowledgement. The same timestamps feed the SLA timers discussed in Lesson 6.

**Practical example.** At the fictional Banyan Microfinance, the night shift ends at 03:00 UTC (08:30 IST). The night analyst updates CASE-1042, publishes the note at 02:40 UTC, and the day analyst reads it at 02:45. They talk from 02:50 to 03:00; the day analyst reads back the credential-stuffing summary, the case is reassigned at 02:58, acknowledged at 02:59, and the shift lead signs off at 03:02.

**Security implications.** Without the acknowledgement step, an attacker active across a shift boundary gains free time: every minute in which nobody owns a live case is a minute of undetected progress. Alerts on critical cases left unacknowledged after a handover close that gap.

**Key learning.** Write first, then talk, and treat responsibility as transferred only when the new owner has acknowledged it in the case system.

## What a handover note contains

The procedure tells you how to transfer; the template tells you what. A fixed template matters for two reasons. It prompts tired analysts for the items they would otherwise forget, and it lets readers find information in the same place every time, which is why a good note can be read in about two minutes. The sections below are ordered by importance, so that a reader interrupted after the first lines still has the most critical facts.

**Headline.** One or two sentences: the single most important thing the incoming shift must know. If there is an active incident, it is the headline. If the shift was quiet, the headline says so explicitly ("No active incidents; queue normal"), because silence is ambiguous.

**Active incidents.** For each declared incident: its severity, the case reference, current status, what has been done (containment, notifications), the next step and who owns it, and any external deadline. Regulatory clocks belong here. Under India's CERT-In Directions of April 2022, covered entities must report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them, so if that clock started during the outgoing shift, the note must state when it started and whether the report has been made.

**Open cases and deadlines.** Cases that are not incidents but are still being worked, each with status, next action and the time by which that action is due. Lesson 6 explains how service-level timers make these deadlines explicit.

**Pending decisions and approvals.** Things the SOC is waiting for from someone else: approval to isolate a server, a reply from the identity team, a decision from legal. These are easy to lose because no analyst action is due; the SOC is simply waiting. Listing them makes someone responsible for chasing.

**Platform health.** Whether every expected log source is reporting, any ingestion delay, parsing errors, failed connectors or tools that are down, and the ticket raised with the platform team. A quiet SIEM is good news only if the data is arriving.

**Intelligence and changes.** New threat advisories relevant to the organisation, indicators added to watchlists, and planned changes or maintenance windows that may cause unusual alerts (a firewall migration, a patch weekend, a penetration test with its own authorisation and contact list).

**Handover actions.** A short numbered list of what the incoming shift must do, each with a named owner and a time.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure is the handover note template drawn as a mapping from each section to what it must contain, in reading order from top to bottom. It matters because the order is deliberate: the most important information comes first so that a reader with two minutes, or one interrupted by an urgent alert, still receives what matters most.

**Components.** The left column holds the seven section names; the right column holds the content each must carry. **Headline** maps to "most important fact; say 'quiet' if quiet" and is dark because it is the one line everyone reads. **Active incidents** maps to "severity, status, owner, next step, deadline" and is red because it is where harm is happening now. **Open cases** maps to "status, next action, time due" and is orange as the main body of ongoing work. **Pending decisions** maps to "what we wait for, from whom, since when" and is violet because it concerns approvals and policy. **Platform health** maps to "sources reporting, lag, parse errors, tickets" and is slate as infrastructure. **Intel and changes** maps to "advisories, watchlists, maintenance windows" and is blue. **Handover actions** maps to "numbered, named owner, time" and is green because it is the defender's to-do list.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each arrow reads "this section must contain". The arrows go from section to content, not the other way, because the template is a set of obligations: every section heading is a question the writer must answer, even if the answer is "none".

**Execution flow.** The writer fills the sections top to bottom. The reader reads them top to bottom and stops when they have what they need; most readers finish the first three sections and skim the rest.

**Decision logic.** For every section the writer answers one question: is there anything to report? If not, they write "none" rather than deleting the heading, so that the reader can distinguish "nothing happened" from "the writer forgot".

**Technical interpretation.** In practice the template lives in the case or ticketing system as a structured form or a pinned page, and many sections can be partly generated: the list of open cases and their owners, the time remaining on each timer, and per-source event counts for platform health. Automation fills facts; the analyst supplies interpretation and priority.

**Practical example.** The Banyan worked example below follows this structure exactly, from a headline about a credential-stuffing case to two handover actions with owners and times.

**Security implications.** The sections most often skipped, pending decisions and platform health, are the ones that create blind spots: an approval nobody chases delays containment, and a dead log source turns into a false sense of safety. A template removes the option to forget them.

**Key learning.** A good handover note answers the same seven questions in the same order every time, putting the most dangerous facts first and writing "none" rather than leaving a gap.

## Worked example: a night-shift handover at Banyan Microfinance

The example below is a **fictional teaching scenario** for Banyan Microfinance, the course's fictional organisation. The cases, names and addresses are invented; the platform-health figures come from the course lab kit.

Before writing the platform-health section, the outgoing analyst checks that every log source is reporting by counting events per source and noting the first and last event times. Using the course mini-SIEM (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py`) against the course dataset `banyan_events.jsonl`:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, COUNT(*) AS n, MIN(ts) AS first_ts, MAX(ts) AS last_ts FROM events GROUP BY source ORDER BY n DESC"
source | n | first_ts | last_ts
proxy | 284 | 2026-11-03T03:19:00Z | 2026-11-03T12:32:00Z
sysmon | 33 | 2026-11-03T03:13:00Z | 2026-11-03T06:14:00Z
windows | 13 | 2026-11-03T03:07:00Z | 2026-11-03T08:17:00Z
vpn | 10 | 2026-11-03T06:42:00Z | 2026-11-03T13:33:00Z
```

`load` reads the JSON-lines dataset into a local SQLite database. `search` runs the quoted SQL: `COUNT(*)` gives the number of events per `source`, and `MIN(ts)` and `MAX(ts)` give the earliest and latest event timestamps (UTC, marked by the trailing `Z`) for each source. The interpretation is the analyst's job. All four expected sources appear, but their last events differ: the endpoint telemetry (`sysmon`) has nothing after 06:14 UTC while the proxy continues until 12:32 UTC. That may be normal for this dataset, since the number of events depends on what users were doing, but it is exactly the kind of difference a handover should mention as a question ("sysmon quiet after 06:14Z: confirm the agent is reporting") rather than leave unstated. In a live SOC the same check would compare current counts against a normal baseline for that hour.

A complete handover note for a different, fictional night might read:

```text
HANDOVER - Banyan Microfinance SOC (FICTIONAL)
Shift: 2026-11-10 19:00-03:00Z   Outgoing: T1 analyst A, T2 analyst B
Written: 02:40Z

1. Headline: no declared incidents. One open High case (CASE-1042,
   suspected credential stuffing on customer portal) needs follow-up
   at start of shift.
2. Active incidents: none.
3. Open cases:
   CASE-1042 High - 412 failed portal logins from 203.0.113.0/24
     and 198.51.100.0/24, 01:05-01:50Z; 3 accounts with a success after
     failures. Source ranges blocked at WAF 01:58Z (change ref CHG-88).
     Next: contact 3 account owners via identity team. Due 04:00Z.
   CASE-1047 Medium - new local admin on WS-221 at 23:12Z; IT says
     planned rebuild, awaiting ticket number to confirm. Due 06:00Z.
4. Pending decisions: identity team to approve forced password reset
   for the 3 CASE-1042 accounts (requested 02:05Z, no reply yet).
5. Platform health: all sources ingesting. Endpoint telemetry volume
   low since 01:30Z; ticket PLAT-301 raised with platform team.
6. Intel and changes: firewall rule review scheduled 04:00-05:00Z
   (expect connection-reset alerts from DMZ hosts).
7. Handover actions:
   (a) Day T1 analyst C - chase identity team for CASE-1042; 04:00Z.
   (b) Day T2 analyst D - confirm PLAT-301 resolved; 05:00Z.
```

Notice what the note does. The headline says there is no incident, which prevents the reader assuming the worst, and names the one case that needs attention. Each open case states facts, the action taken, the next step and a deadline. The pending decision is listed separately so that someone owns the chasing. The platform issue has a ticket reference. The maintenance window warns the day shift about alerts it should expect. Every action has a person and a time.

> [!TIP]
> Separate observed facts from interpretation in the note, exactly as in case records. "412 failed logins from two ranges" is a fact; "suspected credential stuffing" is an assessment and is labelled as one.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"It was quiet, so there is nothing to hand over."** A quiet shift still has platform health, intelligence and changes to report, and "quiet" itself is information. Write the note.

**"I told them in chat."** Chat does not transfer ownership, is invisible to the rest of the shift and leaves no reviewable record. Use it to point at the case, never instead of the case.

**Pasting raw alert lists.** A list of 60 alert titles hides the two that matter. The note summarises and prioritises; the queue holds the detail.

**Handing over unfinished work without a next step.** "Still investigating CASE-1047" forces the incoming analyst to rediscover where the investigation stands. State what was checked, what was not and what to do next.

**Assuming the incoming shift has the same context.** In a follow-the-sun model the next team may not know local systems, people or abbreviations. Write for a competent reader who was not there.

**Mixing time zones.** "Due at 9" is ambiguous across regions. Use UTC throughout and add local time only where a deadline is local.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Shift boundaries are a known weakness, and the defence is to make the boundary observable. The case system should alert when a critical or high-severity case has no acknowledged owner a set time after a handover; this is the control the 2 a.m. scenario lacked. The on-call rota must be current and tested, so that an urgent escalation outside the staffed shift reaches a real person, and escalation procedures should name the case system plus a phone call, never chat alone, for anything critical.

Handover notes are also sensitive documents. They summarise live incidents, affected accounts and sometimes personal data, so they belong in the access-controlled case system, not in email threads or shared documents with broad access. Under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, personal data in SOC records must be protected like any other personal data; include only what the next shift needs.

Finally, handovers are evidence. In a post-incident review the sequence of handover notes, reassignments and acknowledgements shows what the SOC knew and when. Retain them with the case records under the SOC's retention policy.

## Best practices

- Reserve the end of each shift for handover work and schedule an overlap of both shifts.
- Use one template in one system for every handover, quiet or busy.
- Update cases first, then write the note, then brief verbally with read-back.
- Transfer ownership only by reassignment plus acknowledgement in the case system.
- Report platform health every time, with ticket references for problems.
- List pending decisions separately, with who is being waited on and since when.
- Alert on unacknowledged critical and high cases after each handover.
- Review a sample of handover notes each week as part of quality assurance.

## Key takeaways

- The handover is where accountability for live cases changes hands; treat it as a control, not a courtesy.
- Write first, read second, brief third, transfer ownership fourth, sign off last.
- Ownership moves only when the new owner acknowledges it in the case system.
- A fixed template puts the most dangerous facts first and forces "none" rather than silence.
- Platform health and pending decisions are the sections most often forgotten and most likely to hide a blind spot.
- Chat can point at a case; it can never replace one.
