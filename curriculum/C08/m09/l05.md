## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Phishing has two common goals. One is to put malware on a workstation, which is what happened to divya on WS-104 in the Banyan dataset. The other is to steal a credential, a session or a consent, so that the attacker can sign in as the victim and do whatever that person can do. The second kind of compromise is quiet. No process tree turns strange, no antivirus engine fires, and every action the attacker takes is, from the system's point of view, an authorised action by a legitimate user. ATT&CK files it under **Valid Accounts (T1078)**, and it is one of the most common ways intrusions start and persist.

Because an account compromise does not look like an attack, the analyst has to find it in the *pattern* of authentication and account activity. Somebody signs in from a place the user has never been, or from two places at once. A new authenticator app appears on the account the morning after a phishing campaign. A mailbox rule starts sending every message containing the word "invoice" to an external address and deleting the original. A user who only ever logs on interactively to one laptop suddenly authenticates over the network to a file server at three in the morning. None of these alone proves a compromise; each is a sign that raises a question the analyst must answer from other evidence.

This lesson teaches those signs, the telemetry each one lives in, the benign explanations that produce false positives, and how to combine weak signs into a defensible conclusion. The worked example uses the Banyan Microfinance dataset from the course lab kit, where gopal's VPN account (sales) authenticated from 203.0.113.66 after a burst of failures across eight accounts. You will rebuild that evidence with `minisiem.py` and see what the dataset can and cannot tell you about the session the attacker obtained. Lesson 6 turns the conclusion into containment; Lessons 1 to 4 covered the email itself, the links and attachments it carried, sandboxing and user reports.

> [!NOTE]
> All work in this lesson is defensive analysis of your organisation's own logs or the course's synthetic dataset. Investigating a user's sign-ins and mailbox is monitoring of personal data: do it under your organisation's acceptable-use and monitoring policy, record why you looked, and keep the findings on a need-to-know basis.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Account compromise | An unauthorised party holds the ability to act as an account: its password, a valid session or token, a registered authenticator, or a consent granted to an application. |
| Valid Accounts (T1078) | ATT&CK technique for using legitimate credentials to gain access, persist or move, rather than exploiting a flaw. |
| Password spraying (T1110.003) | Trying one or a few common passwords against many accounts, to stay under per-account lockout thresholds. |
| Impossible travel | Two sign-ins by one account whose locations are too far apart for the time between them to be covered by real travel. |
| Concurrent session anomaly | One account active from two incompatible places at the same time, for example an office workstation and an external VPN address. |
| Authenticator registration | Adding an MFA method (authenticator app, phone number, security key) to an account; attackers do it to keep access after a password reset (ATT&CK T1098.005, Device Registration). |
| MFA fatigue | Repeated push prompts sent in the hope that the user approves one (ATT&CK T1621, Multi-Factor Authentication Request Generation). |
| Inbox rule | A mailbox rule that moves, deletes, marks or forwards messages automatically; attackers use them to hide replies (T1564.008, Email Hiding Rules) or to collect mail (T1114.003, Email Forwarding Rule). |
| Session token theft | Capturing a session cookie or token, for example through an adversary-in-the-middle phishing page, so that the attacker can replay a session that already passed MFA. |
| Baseline | The recorded normal behaviour of an account: usual devices, locations, hours, logon types and applications. Every sign in this lesson is a deviation from it. |

## Why compromised accounts are hard to see

An intrusion through malware leaves artefacts that are wrong in themselves: an Office application spawning PowerShell, a binary nobody installed, a beacon to a rare domain. An intrusion through a stolen credential leaves artefacts that are *right in themselves and wrong only in context*. A successful VPN logon is normal. A successful VPN logon for gopal, from an address that has just failed eight different accounts, while gopal's own laptop is browsing the intranet from inside the office, is not.

That shift has three consequences for the analyst.

**First, you need a baseline.** You cannot call a location unusual without knowing the usual ones, or a logon type rare without knowing which types the account normally uses. Identity platforms learn baselines automatically; in a smaller SOC the baseline may be a saved search of the last thirty days for that user.

**Second, you need several sources.** The identity provider records sign-ins and authenticator changes, the VPN gateway records remote sessions, the mail platform records rules and mailbox access, Windows records logons on each host, and the proxy shows where the user's device actually was. A sign that is ambiguous in one source is often resolved by another.

**Third, you reason in combinations.** Each sign has benign explanations. People travel, buy new phones, set up rules to file newsletters, and log on to servers for legitimate reasons. What turns a sign into a finding is coincidence with other signs and with a known trigger, such as a phishing campaign the user received or a spray that preceded the success.

## The four families of account compromise signs

### Impossible travel and location anomalies

The classic location sign compares two consecutive sign-ins. If the distance between their geolocated addresses, divided by the time between them, is faster than any realistic journey (commercial flight speeds are a common ceiling), at least one of the two sign-ins probably was not made by the user. Identity platforms ship this logic as detections named "impossible travel" or "atypical travel", usually combined with the account's learned locations so that a regular commute between two cities stops alerting.

The calculation is simple; the inputs are not. IP geolocation is an estimate, and it is often wrong for mobile carriers, satellite links and corporate egress points. A user on a corporate VPN or a cloud-hosted desktop appears to be wherever the provider's exit node is. Some sources do not record a usable location at all: in the Banyan VPN logs the attacker's session has the country field `??`. For that reason, the related **concurrent session anomaly** is often stronger evidence than distance. If one source shows the user's own device active on the internal network and another shows the same account authenticating from an external address in the same window, the two cannot both be the same person in the same place, whatever the geolocation database says.

Further location signs are a first sign-in from a country the organisation has no staff in, sign-ins from hosting providers or anonymising services instead of residential or corporate ranges, and sign-ins at hours the user never works.

### New MFA devices and authentication method changes

Multi-factor authentication raises the cost of a stolen password, so an attacker who gets past it once tries to make sure they never have to again. The most reliable way is to **register an authenticator of their own**: a new app instance, a phone number or a security key. After that, a password reset alone does not lock them out, because they can satisfy the second factor on their own device.

Authentication method changes therefore deserve attention far beyond their apparent severity. Course Module 4 cited the public account of how FireEye's 2020 investigation began: an alert that an employee's account had registered a second phone for MFA was followed up, the employee confirmed the device was not theirs, and the investigation uncovered a major intrusion. The lesson is that a low-priority identity alert, checked with the user through a known channel, can be the first thread of a large case.

Related signs include MFA methods removed or changed without a helpdesk ticket, a burst of push prompts that the user denies followed by one that is approved (MFA fatigue), a sign-in that satisfied MFA from a new device and a new location at once, and a self-service password reset followed within minutes by a method registration.

### Mailbox rules, forwarding and application consents

Once inside a mailbox, attackers commonly want two things: to read the victim's mail and to keep the victim from noticing. Mailbox rules deliver both. A **forwarding rule** sends copies of incoming mail, or mail matching keywords such as "invoice", "payment" or "bank", to an external address. A **hiding rule** moves replies from a particular correspondent, or anything containing words like "phishing", "hacked" or "suspicious", to a rarely viewed folder or deletes it, and may mark it as read. In business email compromise the pattern is classic: the attacker sends a fraudulent payment instruction from the real mailbox and a hiding rule makes sure the real user never sees the supplier's confused reply.

Rules are not the only mechanism. Many mail platforms also offer mailbox-level forwarding set by the user or an administrator, delegate permissions that give another account access to the mailbox, and organisation-wide transport rules. Cloud suites add **application consent**: an attacker who tricks a user into approving a malicious application gains a token that reads mail or files through the platform's API, which keeps working after the password changes (ATT&CK T1528, Steal Application Access Token). An analyst checking a suspect mailbox looks at all of these, not only at the rules list.

Signs in this family are a newly created rule that forwards externally or deletes, a rule with an odd name (single characters or punctuation are common), new forwarding settings, new delegates, and new application consents with mail or file permissions, especially when any of them appear soon after a suspicious sign-in.

### Unusual logons on hosts and services

On-premises and hybrid environments add Windows authentication to the picture. The Security log records successful logons (event 4624), failed logons (4625) and logons with explicitly supplied credentials (4648), and the logon type field says how the logon happened: type 2 is interactive at the console, 3 is network (for example a file share), 10 is RemoteInteractive (Remote Desktop), 11 is a cached interactive logon. Domain controllers add Kerberos events such as ticket requests (4768) and pre-authentication failures (4771).

The signs are deviations from the account's pattern: a user who only logs on interactively to one laptop appearing as a network or Remote Desktop logon on servers, logons to many hosts in a short time, logons outside working hours, failures with a status meaning "unknown user" (which suggests guessing names) rather than "wrong password", and an account used from a workstation that is not its owner's. For VPNs, the equivalents are a first VPN session for a user who never works remotely, sessions from unusual addresses, and a success from an address that has just produced failures for other users.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The table is a field guide for the four sign families of this lesson. For each one it tells the analyst where to look, what most often causes a false alarm, how far the sign can be trusted alone, and whether the course dataset contains the telemetry to test it. It matters because analysts tend to reason from the signs their tools surface rather than from the signs the attacker leaves.

**Components.** The table has five columns: **Sign**, **Telemetry**, **Common benign cause**, **Weight alone** and **Banyan dataset**. Each of the four rows is one family. **Impossible travel / concurrent** draws on IdP and VPN sign-ins plus the proxy, is most often falsely triggered by VPN egress and geo-IP errors, has medium weight alone, and is testable in part through the available VPN and proxy sources. **New MFA device** draws on the IdP audit log, is most often benign because of a new phone, carries high weight, and is not testable in Banyan because the dataset has no IdP log. **Inbox / forwarding rule** draws on the mail audit log, is commonly benign for newsletter filing rules, carries high weight when the rule forwards externally or deletes, and is likewise absent from Banyan. **Unusual logon** draws on Windows 4624/4625 and VPN logs, is commonly benign for admin work and typing errors, carries low to medium weight, and is testable in Banyan.

**Relationships.** Rows are tinted by their tone: the two rows Banyan can test are blue, and the two it cannot are orange, to flag a telemetry gap rather than a weaker sign. The columns read left to right as a reasoning chain: from the sign, to the source to query, to the innocent explanation that must be ruled out, to how much the result is worth.

**Execution flow.** When an identity alert arrives, an analyst can use the table in order: identify the family, query the named telemetry, test the common benign cause first (call the user about the new phone, check the VPN egress list, read the rule), then weigh the result against the others.

**Decision logic.** The **Weight alone** column encodes the main judgement. A high-weight sign, such as an authenticator nobody in the helpdesk registered, justifies prompt escalation on its own once the benign cause is excluded. Low and medium signs are combined: an unusual logon plus a spray plus a concurrent session is far stronger than any of the three.

**Technical interpretation.** The benign causes are not random; they are the normal processes that use the same mechanism. Users legitimately register phones, file mail with rules and travel. That is why the detection must be followed by verification with the user or the owning team through a known channel.

**Practical example.** In Banyan, rule R2 fires for gopal. The blue rows say to look at the VPN and proxy sources, which the worked example does. The orange rows tell the analyst to record that MFA changes and mailbox rules for gopal could not be checked from the SIEM and must be requested from the identity and mail administrators.

**Security implications.** A SOC that ingests only the blue sources is blind to the two strongest signs. Collecting identity audit and mail audit logs is a detection investment, not a nicety.

**Key learning.** Each sign has a home in a specific log and a common innocent cause; weigh signs together, and record the families your telemetry cannot see as gaps, not as "nothing found".

## Worked example: reading the VPN evidence

In the synthetic Banyan records, the same source address appears on failed VPN authentications for eight different accounts from 06:42:00Z through 06:42:49Z. It then appears on a successful authentication for gopal at 06:46:00Z. The grouping across accounts matters more than the count on any one account. A bad password typed repeatedly by the owner of a single account would leave a different distribution. A shared gateway or a managed health-check process could also produce a cluster, so the source address, device context and normal use of that address remain important to the interpretation.

Start with the authentication rows in the training data. Record the precise observations: eight unique accounts, a 49-second observation span, and one later success for an account in that group from the same source. Record the inference separately: the failure pattern is consistent with password spraying, and the later success raises concern about unauthorised access. Ask the VPN owner whether the address belongs to known egress or a sanctioned testing system. If logs only identify the outcome, the SOC cannot say whether the successful connection established a usable session or what it did afterwards. Request session and network records when the exercise scenario assumes they exist. In the course kit, write down that these records are unavailable.

The queue associates the account with WS-107 from an inventory lookup. That lookup is useful for identifying the device owner and planning a check on the assigned laptop. The VPN record does not identify WS-107 as the device used for the successful login. An analyst who uses the lookup as observed client attribution may isolate the employee's legitimate device and still leave the actual session active. Ask the identity and VPN teams to verify and contain the observed session through their own approved controls. If endpoint checks show only routine traffic on WS-107, document the result with its time range. It limits a claim about the assigned workstation but cannot settle the origin of the independent VPN session.

## Writing the finding and the handoff

A practical escalation can be short. Identify the case and system, name the observed pattern, give the timestamp and reason for urgency, and specify what the receiving team should verify. For Banyan, the open questions are whether the VPN session was authorised, which client and session identifiers were attached to it, whether there were unrecognised authentication-method changes, and whether there are associated mailbox or workload changes. The SIEM has no mailbox audit trail in the exercise. Clearly assign that check to the mail administrator as a data request rather than presenting a clean result.

Consider a variation. An identity audit trail shows a new authenticator enrolled three minutes after the anomalous connection, but the employee says they recently replaced their phone. A helpdesk ticket matching the enrolment could explain the method change. Its existence does not explain the VPN pattern. Keep both hypotheses and look for a link in timestamps, device or event identifiers. Swap the variation: the employee denies both actions and the helpdesk has no enrolment record. That raises confidence and warrants prompt escalation even before you have confirmed later access. In either branch, record what the employee said and the channel used. A person responding on a mailbox or device under investigation may not be a reliable identity check.

## Practical application

Work from the saved R2 result and authenticated records printed in Module 8. Have a reviewer act as the identity owner and ask you for three observations and two next actions. Keep the observation format to timestamp, source, identity and result. Put the analyst's judgement in a separate sentence. Then repeat the drill after adding a fictional note that the source address is an approved corporate VPN exit. Decide what changes in your judgement and what evidence is still relevant. If the address is normal, the widespread sequence of failures followed by one success still deserves explanation. If the address is unknown, do not jump from suspicion to device attribution.

Finally, write a one-line data limitation for each unnecessary assertion you removed. An example is ?The course dataset provides VPN authentication outcomes but does not show command execution or application use inside the 06:46Z session.? This sentence lets the next reviewer decide what they need to request. The ability to describe where evidence ends is as important as the ability to recognise an account anomaly.

## Key takeaways

- Treat sign-in context, method changes, mailbox changes and endpoint behaviour as different sources of evidence.
- Check each deviation against known business activity and approved administrative changes.
- Use inventory enrichment as context and preserve the distinction between an assigned device and an observed client.
- Escalate actionable identity risk with evidence, confidence and precise questions for the owning team.
