## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A phishing case is not finished when the malicious message has been purged from mailboxes and the affected workstation has been contained. Until the work is written down and sent to the people who need it, the organisation has learned nothing it can reuse, the people with legal duties cannot meet them, and the infrastructure that sent the message is still online, ready to target the next victim. **Reporting** is the step that turns one analyst's investigation into organisational knowledge, regulatory compliance and, through abuse reports, a small reduction in the attacker's capability.

Earlier lessons in this module took a phishing case through header analysis, URL and attachment checks, sandboxing, user reports, signs of account compromise and containment. Each of those steps produced facts: a sending IP address, a look-alike domain, a verdict from a sandbox, a list of recipients, the users who clicked, the hosts that ran something, the actions taken and their times. This lesson is about what happens to those facts next. It covers four reporting streams that leave the SOC after a phishing case:

1. **The phishing case report**, the internal record of what happened, what was done and what remains to do, written for the SOC, incident response, IT and management.
2. **Regulatory reporting**, which in India means assessing whether the incident must be reported to the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) and supplying the facts for that report quickly.
3. **Abuse reports** to the providers whose services the attacker used: the registrar of a look-alike domain, the host serving a malicious file, the provider of a sending mail account.
4. **Metrics**, the numbers that show over time whether phishing defences, user reporting and SOC response are improving.

The streams share a source, the case record, but they differ in audience, content, timing and sensitivity. A case report can contain user names and internal host names because its readers are inside the organisation and need them. An abuse report to a hosting provider must contain enough evidence to justify a takedown and nothing more: no employee names, no internal addresses, no customer data. A regulatory report has its own content requirements and a clock. A metric strips away almost everything except times and counts. Learning to cut the same facts differently for each audience is the main skill this lesson teaches.

> [!NOTE]
> This lesson continues the Banyan Microfinance storyline used throughout the course: a finance user, divya, downloaded `invoice.docm` from the look-alike domain `banyan-payroll-update.example` on workstation WS-104 at 06:12Z, and Word started PowerShell two minutes later. All names, domains and addresses are fictional or reserved for documentation. Lesson 8 works through this case hands-on.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Case report | The internal written record of a case: summary, timeline, indicators, scope, actions, impact and follow-up, kept in or attached to the case system. |
| BLUF | "Bottom line up front": the convention of putting the conclusion and the decision needed in the first lines, before the supporting detail. |
| CERT-In | The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team, the national agency for incident response under section 70B of the Information Technology Act 2000. |
| CERT-In Directions | The directions issued by CERT-In on 28 April 2022 under section 70B(6) of the IT Act, which include reporting specified cyber incidents within six hours of noticing them. |
| Point of Contact (PoC) | The person an organisation designates to interface with CERT-In; usually in security or compliance, not the analyst on shift. |
| Abuse report | A notification to a service provider (registrar, host, mail provider) that its service is being used for malicious activity, with evidence, asking it to act under its own terms. |
| Defanging | Rewriting an indicator so it cannot be clicked or resolved by accident, for example `hxxps://banyan-payroll-update[.]example/invoice.docm`. |
| TLP | Traffic Light Protocol (version 2.0 published by FIRST in August 2022): labels CLEAR, GREEN, AMBER, AMBER+STRICT and RED that tell recipients how far they may share information. |
| MTTA / MTTR | Mean time to acknowledge and mean (or median) time to resolve: the interval from alert creation to an analyst taking ownership, and to closure. |
| Report-to-purge time | For phishing, the interval from the first user report (or detection) to removal of the message from every mailbox. |

## Why reporting is part of the response, not paperwork after it

New analysts often treat the report as the boring end of a case. Three observations show why mature SOCs treat it as part of the response itself.

**Decisions depend on it.** The shift lead decides whether a phishing case becomes a declared incident, legal decides whether a regulatory notification is required, and IT decides whether to rebuild a workstation, all on the basis of what the analyst writes. A report that buries "PowerShell ran on a payments approver's workstation" in paragraph six can delay each of those decisions by hours.

**Clocks run while it is written.** The CERT-In Directions give six hours from noticing a reportable incident. That window covers detection, triage, escalation, a legal assessment and the submission itself. If the analyst's notes are scattered across chat messages and a half-filled case form, the people preparing the submission spend the window reconstructing facts instead of checking them.

**The attacker's infrastructure is still live.** A look-alike domain that delivered one macro document to Banyan Microfinance is probably being used against other organisations too. An abuse report to its registrar or host, sent the same day, can take it offline for everyone. Sent a week later, it often arrives after the campaign has moved on.

Reporting also feeds the module's earlier lessons in reverse. Indicators in the case report become blocklist entries and hunt queries; the timeline shows which detection fired first and which should have fired earlier; the metrics show whether user reporting is working. A SOC that does not report well cannot tune well.

## The phishing case report

The case report is the parent document: every other stream is derived from it. It is written progressively, from the first triage note onwards, and finalised when the case closes. A phishing case report has a recognisable structure because readers come to it with predictable questions.

### Structure

| Section | What it contains | Why it is there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Header | Case ID, title, severity, status, analyst, dates, TLP marking | Lets a reader find, file and handle the report correctly |
| Bottom line | Two to four sentences: what happened, current status, decision needed | Most readers stop here; it must stand alone |
| Message details | Sender, envelope sender, subject, delivery time, authentication results, number of recipients | Shows how the message arrived and why filters did or did not stop it |
| Indicators | Domains, URLs, IP addresses, file names and hashes, each defanged | Feeds blocking, hunting and sharing |
| Scope | Recipients, users who reported, users who clicked or opened, hosts with execution, accounts with suspicious sign-ins | Separates "delivered" from "engaged" from "compromised" |
| Timeline | UTC timestamps, one event per line, each with its source | Lets anyone re-check the sequence against the logs |
| Actions taken | Purge, blocks, isolation, resets, with time and who did it | The record of response, and the basis for later review |
| Impact | What data, systems or money were affected, or "none identified", with confidence | Drives the legal and business decisions |
| Notifications | CERT-In assessment, DPDP assessment, internal notifications, abuse reports sent | Shows obligations were considered, by whom and when |
| Root cause and gaps | Which control failed or was missing; which detection fired late or not at all | Turns the case into improvements |
| Follow-up | Open actions, each with an owner and due date | Prevents closed cases with open risks |
| Evidence appendix | Exported logs, the original message file, sandbox report, with SHA-256 hashes and custody entries | Makes the report verifiable |

Two distinctions in this table cause most of the arguments in real case reviews.

The first is the **scope ladder**: delivered, opened, clicked, executed, compromised. A phishing campaign that reached 40 mailboxes is a very different event from one where a single user ran a macro. Each rung needs its own evidence. "Delivered" comes from the mail gateway's message trace. "Clicked" requires interaction evidence, such as user confirmation or a qualified session event, with automated previews and normal resolver activity considered. "Executed" comes from endpoint telemetry such as Sysmon event 1. "Compromised" requires evidence of attacker activity after execution, such as a new account, persistence or command-and-control traffic. Writing "40 users compromised" because 40 received the message is the most common and most damaging overstatement in phishing reports.

The second is **fact versus assessment**. "WS-104 requested `invoice.docm` from `banyan-payroll-update.example` at 06:12:00Z (proxy log)" is a fact. "The document contained a malicious macro" is an assessment, supported by the fact that Word started a hidden, encoded PowerShell command two minutes later. Good reports label assessments and give their confidence, so that a reader can tell what would change if new evidence appeared.

### The bottom line, worked through

For the Banyan case at the point of escalation, a weak bottom line reads: "A phishing email was received and the user may have clicked a link. Investigation ongoing." It contains no host, no user role, no evidence of execution and no request.

A strong one reads:

```text
BOTTOM LINE (TLP:AMBER)
A payments approver (divya, WS-104) downloaded invoice.docm from the
look-alike domain banyan-payroll-update[.]example at 06:12Z; Word started
a hidden encoded PowerShell command at 06:14Z, and WS-104 then contacted
cdn-telemetry-sync[.]example every 300 s. Assessed: malicious document
executed, workstation compromised (high confidence). Available proxy records for the scoped period show no second host
contacting those domains. Requested: approval to isolate WS-104 and revoke
divya's sessions; legal to assess CERT-In reporting.
```

It names the asset and why it matters (a payments approver), gives the evidence for execution, states the assessment with its confidence, bounds the scope to the available proxy records and asks for specific decisions. Every claim in it can be checked against the dataset, which Lesson 8 does.

### Timelines in UTC

Write every timestamp in UTC with the `Z` suffix, because the logs, CERT-In and external providers all need a shared reference. Where management prefers Indian Standard Time, add IST (UTC+05:30) in brackets rather than replacing UTC; 06:12Z is 11:42 IST. Give the source of each line so that a reviewer can re-run the query. A timeline entry without a source is an anecdote.

### Handling marks and personal data

A phishing case report contains personal data: the names of recipients and clickers, their mailboxes and sometimes the content of their mail. Apply your organisation's privacy and retention procedures to this data and ask the privacy owner to determine applicable requirements. In practice: mark the report (TLP:AMBER is common for internal case reports), restrict access through the case system, and when you derive other reports from it, remove names unless the recipient genuinely needs them. A metric does not need to know who clicked; an abuse report never does.

> [!WARNING]
> Do not paste live malicious URLs into case notes, chat channels or email. Many clients render links automatically, preview services fetch them, and a colleague can click one by mistake. Defang every indicator in prose (`hxxps://`, `[.]`) and keep the original form only in the evidence file, where it is needed for blocking and searching.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

Begin with delivery. A recipient appears in this stage when a trusted mail transport or store records a completed delivery, or another defensible record establishes that the message reached their mailbox. A rejected message or one held at the gateway belongs in a different disposition count. Several logs can describe the same message travelling through different internal systems. A combination of message identifiers, recipient and disposition can prevent double counting. This stage answers how widely the message was distributed, with the search interval and gateway coverage stated in the report.

The interaction stage uses a recipient's statement or a reliable event tied to the recipient's session. Some mail systems fetch previews automatically and security gateways can visit destinations during pre-delivery analysis. A proxy or DNS request by itself does not settle who acted or whether a browser followed the URL. Record automated checks separately where they can be identified. If the user reports opening an attachment and the device's audit trail is unavailable, write those two facts together. The report can proceed while the team tries to recover endpoint visibility.

The execution and compromise rows illustrate an evidentiary distinction. Device telemetry may show that an application ran or opened a file. To support a conclusion about compromise, append evidence of suspicious behaviour and tie it to the asset or identity under investigation. An identity compromise may also be supported by authentication and service audit evidence even when no payload ran on a workstation. The rows describe claims that can be tested independently. They do not require every incident to pass through the same progression. An analyst finishing the Banyan report can therefore say that the available proxy and endpoint records support one important execution finding while the total number of delivered messages cannot be established from this dataset. That leaves the mail team a clear question and avoids publishing a guessed denominator.

## Report variants and approval

The case record holds the most detailed account. Include the evidence references, the reviewer, the known gaps and the reasons for both actions taken and actions deferred. Attach approved exports or refer to their restricted location. Use incident and evidence identifiers instead of reproducing complete sensitive message bodies in an update. If a conclusion changes, preserve the prior version and explain the new evidence. Use explicit draft and approval dates so a reader can tell which conclusions were available at the time of each operational decision. A statement sent during first response usually has less context than the final assessment and should carry its own timestamp.

An executive summary needs the business impact, current response status, open decisions and the next time an update will be available. An analyst handoff needs time ranges, specific sources, identifiers, ownership and verification requirements. A report for an external recipient should contain only what the responsible organisational owner has approved for that audience. FIRST's Traffic Light Protocol helps express distribution expectations and does not itself authorise release. Native access controls, contracts and any other applicable requirements still govern what can be shared. The incident lead should resolve incompatible distribution constraints before the report leaves its current audience.

Record a notification decision separately from the technical severity. Capture who owns it, when the relevant facts and timestamps were passed to them, what was decided and whether follow-up is due. A SOC analyst can document the case timeline and supply evidence without treating a severity label as a legal opinion. Use the organisation's approved response plan for applicable duties and routes.

## Worked update

Consider the synthetic source records in Module 8. A proxy record shows that WS-104 sent 48.2 MB to an unfamiliar destination at 08:12Z. A Windows record subsequently indicates the Security log was cleared. A useful update states both events, their sources and the reason the lead should review the case promptly. It says the transferred content is unknown, and it asks which team can corroborate what the destination received. It also records which earlier claims about the endpoint were based on the process and network sequence.

A report should also state where the VPN thread stands. A suspicious authentication for a different identity overlaps the main event window. Timing supports further investigation, but no shared identifier in the course evidence establishes a common actor. A later lead might obtain a common session, a device fingerprint or another corroborating record, at which point the report can be revised. Until then, the response can assign a distinct owner to verify and contain that identity risk. Separate issue tracking can coexist with a single incident lead.

## Practical application

Write a four-sentence update for an incident manager. Sentence one identifies the affected business process and current risk. Sentence two gives two timestamped observations and their sources. Sentence three states one assessment and its confidence or one important uncertainty. Sentence four names an action owner, the requested decision and the next update time. Then write one paragraph for the technical analyst. Include the exact search interval and collection gaps that qualify your scope claim. Ask a colleague to highlight each unsupported assertion and each action missing an owner.

When comparing versions, pay special attention to negative results. Replace a broad claim about what did not happen with a precise description of the search, the result and its boundary. An example is ?The available proxy records for the six-hour review window show no second host requesting the observed destination.? That lets the reader compare a later search and understand whether a newly found host is a change in evidence or simply an expansion in search scope. Clear reporting supports collaboration, and it also protects the people whose data the incident team must review.

## Key takeaways

- Ground every impact and scope claim in a source and a defined window.
- Give the next reviewer the decision, owner and evidence needed to verify it.
- Keep evidence in the approved record and adapt each report to its audience.
- Update uncertainty as new records arrive, including what changed and when.

See the [FIRST Traffic Light Protocol](https://www.first.org/tlp/) for distribution markings and [NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/61/r3/final) for the incident response context.
