## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A report from a colleague is a security observation. It can reveal suspicious messages the gateway delivered, an interaction the proxy cannot attribute, or an urgent payment conversation outside the normal alert feed. The report may arrive with very little context. An employee may say that an invoice looked strange, that their phone showed several authentication prompts, or that they entered information before noticing the address. The SOC needs an intake process that turns these different descriptions into useful, timely evidence. The quality of that process also determines whether people report the next suspicious message.

The first analyst should make it easy to do the right thing. Publish a known reporting route, give the reporter a clear acknowledgment, and ask only for facts that can change the response. Explain what will happen next in plain language. The employee should be able to continue the conversation without touching the suspicious content. A good exchange is brief enough for an anxious person to complete and specific enough that the next shift can understand why the case was escalated.

## Design the reporting route

Employees need a trusted way to reach the SOC from the applications where suspicious activity occurs. A reporting button can pair the user's intent with the original email and the organisation's normal case routing. A designated contact can help when the button is absent, such as on a phone or for a message received outside corporate mail. Publish these routes in onboarding and in a page employees can reach independently of any message. The route should explain how to report urgent credential, payment or endpoint events and whom to call if work communication is unavailable. A usability check should include mobile users, keyboard navigation and staff who need assisted reporting.

A supported reporting mechanism may preserve the original email and relevant metadata. Ordinary message forwards can change which content appears first, replace the original subject, or hide original routing information. If a reporter forwards a message to an unfamiliar person, the artifact can also reach an unintended mailbox. Accept the report you receive and record its acquisition method. Use policy to obtain the original, such as through a mail administrator's authorised export. A screenshot, pasted text or phone description can be enough to begin response. Each is better represented as a specific evidence type than treated as the same thing as preserved message bytes.

Treat work chat, messaging apps, voice and SMS reports similarly. Preserve the evidence that can be safely provided through the organisation's route. For example, document a reported phone call's time, apparent caller and claimed purpose, and note whether the employee disclosed information or acted. A phone number shown by caller ID is an observation of what was displayed. It does not establish who actually placed the call. Exposure affecting an employee's personal device requires the authorised incident and privacy process. Avoid taking custody of unrelated messages or devices when a more limited description can answer the response question.

## Ask questions that change the response

Capture the reported time, channel, account and affected device if relevant. Ask the reporter what they did after seeing the message and in what order. Useful questions include whether they replied, followed a link, entered information, approved a prompt, opened a file, or initiated a payment. Ask about time and the work service involved, without requesting sensitive values. A user can say that they entered a work password without telling anyone the password. A report that names an authentication prompt should say whether it was approved and what application it appeared to come from, as described by the user.

Requests should be concrete. ?Where did you see it and what did you do next?? is often better than ?Were you compromised?? The employee can report an action; the analyst will assess its security significance. Use neutral wording and allow for incomplete memory. People sometimes use ?opened? to mean they read the email, and sometimes mean they opened its file. Clarify that difference. If the employee estimates a time, record it as approximate. Later logs can corroborate or refine the timing, but they should not erase the source of the original statement.

A user may also be uncertain what information they entered. Ask about categories relevant to response, such as work credentials, financial information, or customer files, and route the answer to authorised owners. Do not ask them to re-create the action to see what happened. A second attempt can increase exposure, alter evidence and confuse the timeline. If continued contact in the original channel is risky, establish an independent recognised channel and record the handoff. The service desk, incident lead, identity team and finance team may each have a different safe channel, and predictable routing removes delay.

This action can be urgent even when malware and sign-in signals are absent. Several rows can apply to a single report. If an employee clicked and approved a prompt, an identity escalation can occur before the mail action finishes. A support lead should keep the priorities and case links visible to each owner. What ties the rows together is the difference between the report and its later verification. Each report can justify careful response while the investigation determines which consequences the evidence supports.

## Link duplicates without losing details

An incident can produce many reports. Collapse repeat submissions by the same reporter into a single thread once you can show they refer to the same event. Group related recipient reports under a shared case when the evidence supports the relationship. Preserve the individual observations. The fact that one employee took no action is compatible with another submitting information. Retain the reported channel, time and affected identity for each record. Otherwise campaign grouping can hide exactly the exposure that requires a separate identity or endpoint handoff.

Avoid grouping only by subject text. Two campaigns may share a common subject, and automated prefixes or forwards can change it. Use available mail identifiers and trusted trace records to establish linkage. If only some attributes match, mark the relationship as a hypothesis. Revisit it as the mail team confirms message delivery and other sources show which systems or users were involved. Keep the original report accessible only within the approved case permissions. Campaign summaries can offer aggregate counts without unnecessary exposure of personal details.

The reported timestamp and the notice timestamp can matter for later review. Preserve both and record the time zone the user supplied, as well as your normalised timestamp where appropriate. If a precise event time is unknown, preserve an approximate window. This is often more honest than copying the ticket creation time into a field meant to record a click. Identify which part of the timeline comes from the source system and which comes from the reporter. A future review can then calculate intervals with meaningful endpoints rather than treating every field as equally reliable.

## Respond to the reporter

A useful acknowledgment confirms receipt and tells the employee what to do now. Thank them for reporting, give them a case or reference number if appropriate, and ask them to leave the suspicious message alone while the SOC works. Route urgent follow-up through the organisation's known channel. Keep the wording factual. A good reply can say ?We received your report and are reviewing the message. Please do not reopen it. If you entered information or acted on the payment request, contact the incident desk at the number in the company directory.? This keeps the employee oriented while protecting both the investigation and the user.

For a case with confirmed interaction, communicate the practical next step and owner. The responder may need the employee to stop using a device, wait for a call, or verify a business action through a separate process. Coordinate that message with the teams authorised to act. Fear and blame make people delay future reports or omit details. Focus on what the employee observed and what can be done to help. Avoid an expectation that the user will remember technical details or establish whether a security control worked.

Closing feedback can be simple. If policy permits, tell the reporter what category the SOC found, whether they need to do anything, and how they should report similar cases. Check that any message a person receives from the SOC is identifiable through a known route. If the evidence is incomplete, say the team reviewed the available records and state any remaining action; avoid reassuring them beyond what the records establish. The closure message can be generic in a broader incident and specific in a restricted case when the employee needs information about their own actions.

## Worked case: two reports and different priorities

Use a fictional finance campaign. At 08:05Z, a recipient in procurement presses Report suspicious on an apparent supplier notice. They viewed the subject but took no other reported action. The supported workflow saves the original and associates it with a case. The analyst records the employee's statement and asks the mail team to check the delivery trace. The account linked to the report is the mailbox recipient, not necessarily the device user or the person whose name appears in the message. The analyst acknowledges the report and gives a known route for updates.

At 08:09Z, an accounts payable employee calls the known service desk line about a notice with the same apparent supplier. They state that they used a work password on a linked page before seeing an unexpected prompt, which they did not approve. Payment records are unchanged as far as they know. The incident desk creates an identity handoff promptly with the interaction time and account. A mail analyst attempts to link the two reports using the trusted message trace and original artifacts. No password is copied into the ticket. The preliminary incident record treats them as possibly related, and the response to the second report does not wait for proof of that relationship.

At 08:14Z, the mail owner sees a preview request immediately after each delivery. That finding explains two automated observations; it cannot erase the employee's direct report that they took an action. Compare the device evidence and the reported window if such evidence exists. If telemetry misses the employee's mobile device, record this as a coverage gap. The identity owner can still act on the reported exposure and other authentication evidence. The correct resolution can include a tentative message classification, a definite protective response, and a statement of what additional data would be needed.

## Exercise and review criteria

Give one student the two fictional reports and another the role of incident lead. Have the intake analyst create one case entry per report and a short linking note. The first entry should make it easy to request the delivery check. The second should describe the reported credential interaction and a handoff to the identity owner. Give each entry a time and source. Then write an acknowledgment that can be shared without exposing the details of the other reporter. Swap roles and point out unclear or missing ownership.

Repeat the exercise with two changes. First assume the employee does not know whether the prompt was approved. Ask one question that reduces uncertainty without requesting a secret. Then assume the reporter used a private phone and offers to send an image of a page containing personal notifications. Decide which fields are necessary for the case and route collection through the organisation's approved process. The exercise tests whether you can retain useful evidence and reduce unnecessary disclosure at the same time. Finish with a shift note that distinguishes observed actions, possible impact and the pending verification request.

## Review the route with operational metrics

Track the time from report to acknowledgment and from reported exposure to an authorised handoff. Define each timestamp so repeat reports do not appear to speed up or slow down response inaccurately. Review cases that arrived through fallback routes to see whether the main route was available and understandable. Compare a sample of intake records with preserved artifacts and follow-up decisions. A reported increase can reflect improved trust in the reporting route rather than a change in incident frequency. Keep recipient-level details in the case and use aggregate counts in the process review.

## Key takeaways

- Offer a known, usable reporting route and preserve the best evidence available.
- Ask what the user did, when and in which channel, without collecting secrets.
- Group related cases while preserving each recipient's exposure and follow-up.
- Acknowledge useful reports promptly and route urgent risks to their authorised owners.
