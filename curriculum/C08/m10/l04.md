## What a baseline is and why hunts need one

Most hunt predictions contain a hidden comparison. "A destination not seen on peer workstations," "more outbound data than usual," "a child process that Office does not normally start": each of these depends on knowing what is normal. A baseline is the explicit, recorded description of that normal, built from a stated population over a stated period, so the analyst can judge whether an observation departs from it.

Without a baseline, analysts fall back on intuition. Intuition is useful for generating hypotheses but unreliable for testing them, because what looks strange to one analyst is routine to the team that runs the application. A file sync agent that sends tens of megabytes every hour looks alarming until someone checks how many workstations do the same thing. Equally, something that looks routine, such as a domain with "telemetry" in its name, may be unique to one host.

A baseline answers three questions: normal for whom, normal over what period, and normal by what measure. This lesson covers each, then explains how to compare an observation against a baseline, and why a baseline must never be treated as a list of safe behavior.

## Normal for whom: choosing a peer group

A peer group is the set of entities whose behavior the observation is compared against. Choosing it well is the most important baseline decision.

**Too broad.** Comparing a finance workstation to every device in the organization, including servers, build machines and kiosks, mixes populations with very different behavior. Servers send large volumes routinely, so a finance workstation sending a large volume looks ordinary by comparison.

**Too narrow.** Comparing a workstation only to its own history works for detecting change on that host, but it cannot tell the analyst whether a behavior is rare across the organization. If the host has always contacted a strange destination, its own history will call that normal.

**Well chosen.** Peers should share role, software build and usage pattern. For a finance workstation, a good peer group is other finance workstations on the same image. Many hunts use two baselines together: the entity's own history (has this host done this before?) and its peer group (do similar hosts do this?). An observation that is new for the host and rare among peers is far more interesting than one that fails only one comparison.

Peer groups depend on enrichment. The analyst usually decides which workstations are "finance" from an asset inventory, not from the logs. That is legitimate, but the documentation should label the peer group membership as enrichment with a date, because inventories go stale and a misfiled host can distort the baseline.

## Normal over what period: the baseline window

The baseline window is the period used to describe normal. It is separate from the hunt window, which is the period being tested, and the two should not overlap; otherwise the behavior under test helps define its own normal.

Window length involves trade-offs:

- **Short windows** of a few days reflect current behavior but miss weekly and monthly cycles. A month-end reporting job will look anomalous if the baseline covers only mid-month days.
- **Long windows** of several months capture cycles but include outdated behavior from before software changes or reorganizations.
- **Retention limits the choice.** A baseline cannot be longer than the data the source keeps. If proxy retention is 14 days, a 30-day baseline is impossible, and any "first seen in 30 days" result would be wrong.

Seasonality deserves explicit thought. Finance teams often have month-end, quarter-end and payroll cycles. A baseline that excludes those dates will flag them; one that includes them may hide an anomaly that happens to fall on a busy day. One practical approach is to compare like with like: compare a month-end day with previous month-end days, not with an average day.

The saved Banyan dataset contains 340 rows focused on 2026-11-03. If the saved records cover only that day, no multi-day baseline is available, and the only defensible comparison is a same-day peer comparison across the workstations present in the proxy data. The hunt documentation should say this plainly: "Baseline limited to same-day peer comparison; no historical baseline available in the saved records."

## Normal by what measure: choosing the metric

The metric is what is counted or measured. Different metrics answer different questions.

**Prevalence:** how many distinct entities show the value. This is the main metric for rarity: a destination contacted by one of forty workstations is rare by prevalence.

**Frequency:** how many times the value occurs. High frequency on one host with low prevalence across hosts is a common pattern for both beaconing malware and legitimate per-user software, so frequency needs context.

**Volume:** bytes, counts or durations. Volume is the natural metric for transfer hunts, but volume distributions are heavily skewed. A few large legitimate uploads dominate the upper end.

**Timing:** time of day, interval regularity, duration. A workstation active at 03:00 UTC when peers are idle may be interesting, depending on staff working patterns.

**Novelty:** whether the value appeared before in the baseline window. First-seen analysis is powerful but sensitive to the window length, as noted above.

For skewed metrics like volume, the mean and standard deviation are often misleading, because a single large legitimate value inflates both. The median and upper percentiles describe typical and high-but-normal values more robustly. A simple, explainable comparison is the ratio of the observation to the peer median, alongside whether it exceeds the highest value the peer group has shown.

## Stacking and the long tail

Stacking, introduced in Lesson 3, is the workhorse of baselining. Group by a field, count distinct entities, and sort. Real-world distributions almost always have the same shape: a short head of very common values, a long tail of values seen on only one or two entities.

The long tail is where hunts look, but most of it is benign. Personal browsing, one-off vendor portals and newly released software all live in the tail. The analyst's job is not to investigate every tail value but to use the hypothesis to decide which tail values matter. A rare destination that also received a large transfer, or that was followed by an unusual process, is worth attention. A rare destination that received a single small request with no other signal usually is not.

The saved records show that WS-104 made 18 requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example between 06:17:00Z and 07:42:02Z on 2026-11-03. That span is 85 minutes and 2 seconds, which would give an average gap of about five minutes across 17 intervals. Two baseline questions follow. First, prevalence: do other workstations contact this domain? If many do, it is likely a legitimate service, whatever its name. If only WS-104 does, its name says nothing about its legitimacy. Second, timing: are the intervals regular? An average says nothing about regularity; the analyst must read each timestamp in the saved rows before calling the pattern periodic.

## Comparing an observation to a baseline

The following tables are synthetic, authored for this lesson. They are not Banyan source data.

**Synthetic peer baseline: daily outbound bytes per finance workstation, 14 days from 2026-10-18 to 2026-10-31 UTC (includes the October month-end)**

| Host | Median daily MB | Max daily MB | Note from enrichment |
|---|---|---|---|
| WS-301 | 6.1 | 14.0 | Finance image |
| WS-302 | 5.4 | 11.2 | Finance image |
| WS-303 | 7.0 | 18.5 | Finance image |
| WS-304 | 4.8 | 9.9 | Finance image |
| WS-305 | 6.6 | 61.0 | Finance image; month-end report upload |
| WS-306 | 5.9 | 12.3 | Finance image |

**Synthetic observation, 2026-11-01 UTC:** WS-303 sends 52.0 MB in one request to a destination no peer contacted in the baseline window.

Step through the comparison.

**Against its own history:** WS-303's median is 7.0 MB and its maximum is 18.5 MB. 52.0 MB is about 7.4 times its median and nearly three times its previous maximum. It is new behavior for the host.

**Against peers by volume:** the peer group maximum is 61.0 MB, from WS-305. A naive threshold of "above the highest peer value" would not flag WS-303. Reading the note shows why that threshold is weak: WS-305's spike was a month-end upload, a known cyclic event. Including it in a single volume threshold lets any upload up to 61 MB pass.

**Against peers by destination:** no peer contacted the destination. That makes the destination rare by prevalence.

**Combined:** new for the host by volume, rare among peers by destination. Neither comparison alone is conclusive, and the volume comparison against the peer maximum is actively misleading. Together they support escalating the observation for validation. They do not establish what was sent or why.

This case illustrates the main skill of the lesson: an observation should be compared on more than one dimension, and each comparison should be read with its context.

## Baselines are not allowlists

A baseline describes what happened in the past. It does not certify that what happened was safe. This distinction matters in three ways.

**Pre-existing compromise.** If an adversary has been present for weeks, their activity is part of the baseline. A hunt that only looks for departures from baseline will never find them. Periodic hunts that examine the head of the distribution, such as common processes with unusual parents, help offset this.

**Common is not safe.** A behavior seen on every host could be a legitimate agent or a widespread problem. Prevalence lowers suspicion for a single host's anomaly; it does not clear the behavior itself.

**Allowlists go stale.** Teams sometimes convert baselines into suppressions: "these destinations are normal, exclude them from hunts." That can be reasonable for well-understood services, but each suppression should have an owner, a reason and a review date. A destination added because it was common in March may have changed hands by November.

The language in hunt documentation should reflect this. Write "consistent with the peer baseline" rather than "normal" or "safe."

## Bounded case reasoning

Apply the lesson to the saved-record Banyan transfer: one proxy request from WS-104 to files-share-drop.example at 08:12:00Z on 2026-11-03, with 48,200,000 outbound bytes, payload unknown.

What a defensible baseline statement could say, after checking the saved rows:

- Whether any other workstation in the saved proxy data contacted files-share-drop.example that day (same-day prevalence).
- How WS-104's 48,200,000 bytes compares with the outbound bytes of other workstations' requests that day (same-day volume).
- That no historical baseline exists in the saved records, if that is confirmed.

What it could not say: that WS-104 had never contacted the destination before, because the saved records may not include earlier dates; that the transfer was abnormal for the user, because there is no user history; or that the content was sensitive, because there is no payload evidence.

The outcome is likely a positive-for-validation result if the destination is unique to WS-104 and the volume is high among same-day requests, with an explicit limitation that the baseline is a single day.

## Common errors

- **Overlapping hunt and baseline windows**, so the behavior under test helps define normal.
- **Peer groups that mix roles**, such as workstations with servers.
- **Using the mean on skewed volume data**, inflated by a few legitimate large transfers.
- **Single-dimension thresholds** that pass anomalies hidden behind cyclic spikes.
- **Reading names as evidence**, such as trusting a domain because it contains "cdn" or "telemetry."
- **Calling an average interval regular** without checking the individual intervals.
- **Treating the baseline as an allowlist**, which hides pre-existing or widespread activity.
- **Baselines longer than retention**, which make every value look first-seen.

## Practical exercise

Use the synthetic peer baseline above and the synthetic observations below. None of these records is Banyan data.

Synthetic observations:

| Obs | Date (UTC) | Host | Destination prevalence among peers (baseline window) | Bytes out that day |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 2026-11-01 | WS-301 | 6 of 6 | 13.5 MB |
| B | 2026-11-01 | WS-304 | 0 of 6 | 0.4 MB |
| C | 2026-11-01 | WS-306 | 0 of 6 | 31.0 MB |
| D | 2026-11-30 (month-end) | WS-305 | 6 of 6 | 58.0 MB |

Tasks:

1. For each observation, compare it with the host's own median and maximum and with peer prevalence.
2. Classify each as "consistent with baseline," "deviates on one dimension" or "deviates on multiple dimensions."
3. State what additional evidence you would need before calling C a positive hunt result.
4. Write one sentence explaining why D should be compared with previous month-end dates rather than the overall median.

**Model answer notes.** A is just under WS-301's own maximum and goes to a destination every peer uses: consistent with baseline. B goes to a rare destination but with a very small volume: it deviates on one dimension and is a low priority unless another signal exists. C is over five times WS-306's median and about two and a half times its maximum, to a destination no peer uses: it deviates on multiple dimensions and should be validated, for example by checking for related endpoint activity and for a change record. D is high against the overall median but in line with WS-305's previous month-end peak and goes to a common destination; comparing it with prior month-end days is the fair test, and on that test it is consistent with the baseline.

## Measure the interval and same-day comparison

The C08 kit's saved proxy records contain 18 matching requests for cdn-telemetry-sync.example, all attributed to WS-104. Sorting the matching timestamps produces 17 successive gaps. In this fixture the shortest gap is 298 seconds, the median is 300 seconds and the longest is 302 seconds. State these as measurements from the 2026-11-03 fixture. A mean near five minutes would be less informative by itself because a series of short and long gaps can have a similar mean. The gap range confirms regularity of the recorded requests while leaving the purpose of the activity for investigation.

The largest outbound byte count in the proxy fixture is 48,200,000 for WS-104. The next largest request in the same fixture is 3,999 bytes, on WS-107. This is a comparison across the saved single-day rows and cannot establish the normal long-term volume for either host. Legitimate business tasks and sparse source data can both affect what the analyst sees. Give the evidence source and baseline period when reporting an unusual measurement. Ask which additional source or period would support a stronger comparison. This exercise gives the analyst a reason to investigate a distinct outlier without treating size as proof of what was transferred.

## Key takeaways

- A baseline states normal for whom, over what period and by what measure.
- Use both the entity's own history and a well-chosen peer group.
- Keep baseline and hunt windows separate, and keep the baseline within retention.
- Prefer median and percentiles for skewed metrics, and compare on more than one dimension.
- A baseline is a description of the past, not an allowlist; write "consistent with baseline," not "safe."
