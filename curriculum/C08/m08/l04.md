## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every decision in an incident rests on evidence: what happened, when, on which systems, through which accounts, and what left the organisation. That evidence is fragile. Memory disappears when a machine is switched off. Network connections close. Logs roll over when local storage fills, SIEM retention expires, and an attacker who has administrative rights can clear the Security log, as the Banyan attacker did on WS-104 at 08:17Z. Evidence that is not preserved deliberately and early is often not available later, and evidence that was preserved carelessly may not be trusted when it matters.

**Evidence preservation** is the discipline of capturing information about an incident in a way that keeps it available, unchanged and explainable. Available means it still exists when the investigation, the regulator, the insurer or a court needs it. Unchanged means you can show that what you present today is bit-for-bit what you collected. Explainable means you can say who collected it, from where, how, when, and who has handled it since. The three map onto three practices this lesson teaches: collecting in **order of volatility**, protecting **integrity with cryptographic hashes**, and recording a **chain of custody**. Around them sit two organisational practices: **log preservation** (making sure the records the investigation depends on are not deleted by routine retention) and **legal hold** (a formal instruction suspending deletion when litigation or regulatory action is possible).

A SOC analyst is not usually a forensic examiner. Disk and memory imaging, and the analysis of those images, are specialist work covered in Course 09. But the SOC is present at the start of almost every incident, often hours before anyone else, and it controls the logs. What the SOC does in the first hour decides what forensics can do on the first day. The analyst's responsibilities are therefore to avoid destroying evidence, to request volatile capture before it is too late, to preserve log evidence with integrity and custody records, and to document every action taken on live systems. Lesson 3 showed that containment should be chosen with evidence in mind; this lesson shows what that evidence is and how to keep it.

You will work through these practices with the course kit's `LAB_ASSETS/C08/evidence_export.py`, run in a temporary working copy, exporting the Banyan WS-104 events, verifying them, detecting a deliberate change, re-running the export to show it is reproducible, and discovering one real limitation of the tool that matters for practice.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Evidence preservation | Capturing incident information so that it remains available, unchanged and explainable. |
| Volatile data | Data that is lost when a system is powered off or changes quickly while it runs: memory, processes, network connections. |
| Order of volatility | Collecting the most short-lived evidence first, as described in RFC 3227. |
| Cryptographic hash | A fixed-length digest computed from data such that any change to the data produces a different digest; SHA-256 produces 256 bits (64 hexadecimal characters). |
| Manifest | A file listing evidence items with their hashes, for example a `SHA256SUMS` file. |
| Chain of custody | The documented history of an evidence item: who collected it, when, how, where it has been stored and every transfer since. |
| Forensic copy | A copy made with methods that do not alter the source and whose integrity is verified by hash. |
| Log preservation | Actions that stop routine retention or rotation from deleting logs an investigation needs. |
| Legal hold | A formal instruction, usually from legal counsel, to preserve information relevant to actual or anticipated litigation or regulatory action, suspending normal deletion. |
| Write-once storage (WORM) | Storage that prevents modification or deletion for a set period, such as object lock in cloud storage. |

## Why the SOC must care about evidence

Evidence serves more audiences than the courtroom, and most incidents never reach one. The SOC preserves evidence because several later decisions depend on it.

**Scoping and eradication.** The investigation must establish every system and account the attacker touched. If the logs showing the attacker's other footholds have expired, eradication is incomplete and the attacker returns.

**Regulatory reporting.** A report to CERT-In or a data protection notification must state facts: what happened, when it was noticed, what data was affected. Statements that cannot be backed by preserved records are risky to make and hard to defend.

**Impact assessment.** Whether the 48.2 MB upload from WS-104 contained personal or financial data decides much of the legal and business response. Answering it needs the proxy record, the files accessed on WS-104 before 08:12Z and, ideally, forensic analysis of the host.

**Insurance, disputes, discipline and prosecution.** Insurers and counterparties may ask what happened and when the organisation knew; disciplinary or criminal proceedings test evidence against people who want to show it was altered or mishandled.

Because the SOC cannot know at the start which of these will matter, the professional default is to preserve as though any of them might.

## Order of volatility

Not all evidence disappears at the same rate. RFC 3227, *Guidelines for Evidence Collection and Archiving* (2002), sets out an order of volatility for a typical system, from most to least volatile: registers and cache; the routing table, ARP cache, process table, kernel statistics and memory; temporary file systems; disk; remote logging and monitoring data relevant to the system; physical configuration and network topology; and archival media. The principle is simple: collect what will vanish first before collecting what will last.

The order matters to a SOC analyst in two ways. First, it decides what you ask for, and how urgently. If WS-104 is still running, its memory may hold the decoded PowerShell that the process log shows only as `-EncodedCommand <redacted-for-course>`, the beacon's configuration, and network connections at the moment of capture. None of that survives a reboot. The analyst's request to the forensic team, made at the same time as the isolation recommendation, is "capture memory before anything else happens to this host". Second, it decides what you must stop other people doing. A helpful technician who reboots the machine, runs a clean-up tool or reimages it destroys the most volatile evidence first. A clear "do not touch, do not reboot" instruction to the help desk and the user is an evidence-preservation action.

The order also explains why EDR isolation, rather than power-off, is the default containment for a workstation: isolation preserves every layer of the order, while power-off destroys the top layers.

Red marks layers that are gone the moment a host is switched off; orange marks layers that survive power-off but not routine clean-up or reimaging; blue and slate mark the more durable layers held away from the host.

**Relationships and arrows.** The stack has no arrows; position is the relationship. Each layer is more durable than the one above it, so the vertical position is both a durability scale and the collection order.

**Execution flow.** Collection proceeds from the top down. On WS-104 that means memory first (by the forensic team through the EDR channel after isolation), then disk imaging, while the SOC preserves the remote logs in parallel, because they are held on different systems and can be preserved without touching the host.

**Decision logic.** Before any action on a live host, ask which layers it would destroy. Power-off destroys the two red layers; a clean-up tool damages temporary files and disk; reimaging destroys everything on the host. Only the blue and slate layers survive all of these.

**Technical interpretation.** Volatility is about how long data persists without deliberate capture. Memory changes continuously while a system runs and is lost at power-off. Disk content persists but is altered by normal use, which is why imaging tools are designed not to write to the source. Remote logs are durable but bounded by retention periods and storage limits.

**Practical example.** WS-104's Security log was cleared at 08:17Z (event 1102). The local disk layer for that log is therefore damaged, but the remote layer survived: the SIEM had already collected the events, which is why the Banyan timeline still exists. That is the value of the blue layer.

**Security implications.** Attackers know the order too. Clearing local logs, running tools only in memory and deleting files are attacks on the lower and upper layers respectively. Central log collection and quick memory capture are the defences.

**Key learning.** Capture from the top of the stack down, protect the remote logs in parallel, and never let a well-meant reboot or reimage destroy the layers you have not yet collected.

## Integrity: what a hash proves

Once evidence is collected, you must be able to show that it has not changed. The standard tool is a **cryptographic hash**. SHA-256 takes any input and produces a 256-bit digest, written as 64 hexadecimal characters. Two properties make it useful as evidence: any change to the input, even one character, produces a completely different digest; and it is computationally infeasible to construct a different input with the same digest. Recording the digest at collection time and recomputing it later therefore shows whether the item is identical to what was collected.

Be precise about what a matching hash proves. It proves that the file is unchanged **since the hash was recorded**. It does not prove that the file was complete or accurate when it was collected, that the source system's logs were intact, or that the collection query selected the right events. A hash recorded after someone had already edited a file proves only that the edited file is unchanged. This is why the hash must be computed at the moment of collection and recorded somewhere the evidence handler cannot silently alter, and why the custody record also stores how the item was produced.

Older algorithms such as MD5 and SHA-1 have known collision weaknesses and should not be relied on alone for new evidence work; SHA-256 is the common default today.

## Chain of custody

A hash tells you whether an item changed. A **chain of custody** tells you who could have changed it. It is the continuous record of an item's life: who collected it, from which source, by what method, at what time (in UTC), with what hash; where it was stored; and every time it was accessed, copied or transferred, by whom and why. A gap in the chain, such as a period when nobody can say who held the file, invites the argument that the evidence could have been altered in that gap, even if the hash still matches.

In practice a chain of custody record holds, for each item: a unique item identifier and the case number; a description; the source (host, system, query); the collection method and tool; the collector's name; the date and time of collection; the hash and algorithm; the storage location; and a transfer log with dates, names, purpose and the hash verified at each hand-over. In mature SOCs this lives in a case-management system with access control and an audit trail, and the evidence itself is stored on write-once or tightly controlled storage.

## Worked example: preserving the WS-104 log evidence

The course kit's `LAB_ASSETS/C08/evidence_export.py` implements hashing and custody for log evidence in about forty lines. It exports the raw events for one host and time window from the teaching SIEM, writes a SHA-256 manifest and appends a custody entry. The run below was made in a temporary working copy of the kit with CPython 3.14.4 on Windows, using Git for Windows bash for `sha256sum`. Never run lab scripts inside the kit folder itself.

### Step 1: export and verify

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-104
exported 32 events -> evidence\CASE-0001\WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
sha256 8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf
$ cd evidence/CASE-0001
$ cat SHA256SUMS
8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf  WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
$ sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS
WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl: OK
$ cat custody.jsonl
{"case": "CASE-0001", "item": "WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl", "sha256": "8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf", "events": 32, "query": {"host": "WS-104", "start": "2026-11-03T06:00:00Z", "end": "2026-11-03T09:00:00Z"}, "action": "exported from minisiem", "by": "analyst.t2", "utc": "2026-11-20T15:47:43Z"}
```

The script takes two arguments, the case identifier and the host; the time window is fixed by its defaults at 06:00Z to 09:00Z on 3 November 2026. It selects the `raw` column (the original JSON of each event, not the parsed fields) with a parameterised query ordered by timestamp, writes the rows to a JSONL file with Unix line endings, hashes the file in 65,536-byte blocks, writes `SHA256SUMS` in the two-space format that `sha256sum -c` expects, and appends one JSON line to `custody.jsonl`. The `-c` option of `sha256sum` reads the manifest, recomputes each listed file's digest and prints `OK` or `FAILED`. The export path prints with backslashes because the run was on Windows. Your digest must match the one above exactly, because the dataset and export are deterministic; only the `utc` field will differ.

Read the custody entry as a colleague would. It names the case and item, gives the digest and event count, records the **exact query parameters** (host and window) so that anyone can re-run the export and compare, says what was done and by whom, and gives the UTC time of the action. The `by` value is hard-coded as `analyst.t2` in this teaching script; a real system takes the identity from the authenticated user.

On Windows without `sha256sum`, PowerShell gives the same digest in upper case:

```terminal
PS> Get-FileHash -Algorithm SHA256 .\WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl | Format-List Algorithm,Hash
Algorithm : SHA256
Hash      : 8521C97CAB10C667D87B01DB92FA9C45A5F130DFC314FB49C622EAF1F2919ACF
```

`-Algorithm SHA256` selects the hash function; `Format-List` prints only the two named properties. Hexadecimal digests are compared case-insensitively.

### Step 2: show that a change is detected

To see what the hash protects against, copy the export and its manifest to a separate folder, change one value in the copy, and verify it:

```terminal
$ cp evidence/CASE-0001/WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl evidence/CASE-0001/SHA256SUMS ../tamper/
$ cd ../tamper
$ sed -i 's/48200000/482000/' WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
$ sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS
WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl: FAILED
sha256sum: WARNING: 1 computed checksum did NOT match
$ sha256sum WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
3401cb180d5cda71ca8e6a154994b99f1e8f02302295f3a4837f6092a01b4b4f *WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
```

`sed -i` edits the file in place, replacing the upload size of 48,200,000 bytes with 482,000: the kind of change that would turn a 48.2 MB exfiltration into a harmless-looking 0.5 MB transfer. Verification fails, `sha256sum` exits with a non-zero status, and the recomputed digest shares nothing visible with the original. (The `*` before the file name marks binary mode in this build of `sha256sum`.) Always tamper with a copy in a separate folder; never alter the original evidence, even in a lab.

### Step 3: show that the export is reproducible

```terminal
$ python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-104
exported 32 events -> evidence\CASE-0001\WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
sha256 8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf
$ wc -l evidence/CASE-0001/custody.jsonl
2 evidence/CASE-0001/custody.jsonl
```

The second run produces the identical digest, because the query, the ordering and the line endings are fixed, and it appends a second custody line rather than replacing the first. Reproducibility is a strong form of trust: anyone with access to the same SIEM data can re-run the recorded query and obtain the same bytes. Note that the second run also overwrote the export file; a production evidence system would refuse to overwrite an existing item and would instead create a new, separately identified one.

### Step 4: a second item, and a limitation

The Lesson 3 recommendation also covered gopal. Exporting his laptop's events into the same case shows a real limitation of the teaching tool:

```terminal
$ python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-107
exported 10 events -> evidence\CASE-0001\WS-107_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
sha256 1ab3194ca5e6e692dfb9ea7331a969ec47240968555aad3711427edfec4f1868
$ cat evidence/CASE-0001/SHA256SUMS
1ab3194ca5e6e692dfb9ea7331a969ec47240968555aad3711427edfec4f1868  WS-107_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
```

Two lessons follow. First, the manifest now lists **only the WS-107 file**: the script rewrites `SHA256SUMS` on every run instead of appending to it, so the WS-104 digest has disappeared from the manifest. It still exists in `custody.jsonl`, which now has three lines, and that is why the custody log, not the manifest, is the authoritative record here. In practice, keep one manifest entry per item and never let a tool silently replace a previous record. Second, the WS-107 export contains gopal's ordinary proxy and process events, but **not** the suspicious VPN events: VPN records in this dataset carry no host field, so a host-based export cannot select them. Preserving them needs a separate, documented query (for example on `source='vpn'` and `src_ip='203.0.113.66'`), hashed and recorded in the same way. Always check that the export method actually covers the evidence you mean to preserve.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows how a piece of log evidence becomes trustworthy: from the query that selects it, through hashing and recording, to the verification that anyone can repeat later. It matters because each step answers a different challenge that could be raised against the evidence.

**Components.** There are six steps, top to bottom. **Documented query** (violet) has the sub-label "host WS-104, 06:00Z-09:00Z, raw events": the control step that defines what is collected. **Raw export** (blue) has "WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl, 32 events". **SHA-256 digest** (orange) has "8521c97c...19acf", the key step that fixes the content. **Manifest + custody entry** (green) has "SHA256SUMS; custody.jsonl: who, when, query". **Store and transfer** (slate) has "restricted or write-once storage; log every hand-over". **Re-hash and compare** (dark) has "OK = unchanged; FAILED = altered copy", the outcome on which trust rests.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each downward arrow means "the output of this step is the input to the next". The query determines the export; the export's bytes determine the digest; the digest is written into both the manifest and the custody record; the stored item is later re-hashed and compared with the recorded digest. The dashed loop arrow labelled "every hand-over and every use" returns from the last step to **Store and transfer**, because verification is repeated each time the item moves or is used.

**Execution flow.** Steps one to four happen once, at collection time, in seconds. Steps five and six repeat for the life of the evidence.

**Decision logic.** The final step has two outcomes. **OK** means the item is identical to what was collected and can be used. **FAILED** means it is not; the copy is set aside, the discrepancy is recorded, and a verified copy is obtained from storage or the export is re-run from the documented query.

**Technical interpretation.** The query step gives reproducibility, the digest gives integrity, the custody record gives accountability, and controlled storage limits who could have changed the item. Remove any one and a challenge succeeds: without the query no one can reproduce the export; without the digest no one can detect alteration; without custody no one can say who held it.

**Practical example.** In the lab, the original verified **OK**, the copy with 48200000 changed to 482000 returned **FAILED**, and a re-run of the documented query gave the identical digest. The WS-107 run showed why the custody entry, not the overwritten manifest, carries the record.

**Security implications.** The chain begins at the SIEM, so it inherits the SIEM's integrity: if events were lost or altered before ingestion, a perfect hash of the export will not reveal it. Protecting the collection pipeline and the SIEM's own storage is part of evidence preservation.

**Key learning.** Query, export, hash, record, store and re-verify: each link answers a different challenge, and the custody record ties them together.

## Log preservation

For a SOC, logs are the evidence most directly under its control, and the most often lost to routine operation rather than to attackers. Retention periods expire, indexes are rolled over, endpoint logs overwrite themselves when they reach their size limit, and cloud platforms keep some activity logs for limited periods by default. An investigation that starts days after the intrusion can find that the first and most revealing events have already gone.

Log preservation therefore starts at the first sign of an incident and runs in parallel with containment. Its steps are:

1. **Identify the sources** that cover the incident: for Banyan, Sysmon and Windows Security events from WS-104, proxy logs for the three domains, VPN logs for 203.0.113.66 and gopal, and identity platform sign-in logs for divya and gopal.
2. **Export the relevant records** with integrity and custody, as in the worked example, so that they no longer depend on retention.
3. **Extend or suspend retention** on the source systems for the affected period, through the SIEM's index management or the platform's retention settings, so that the wider data set remains available for scoping.
4. **Protect the copies** on restricted or write-once storage.
5. **Record clock information** for each source (time zone and any known offset), because timelines built from several sources are only as good as their clocks (Module 2).

The Banyan case shows why central collection matters. The attacker cleared the Security log on WS-104 at 08:17Z. Locally, that destroyed the record of what had happened. Centrally, the events had already been collected, and the clearing itself was logged as event 1102, which became one of the strongest indicators in the case. Anti-forensic actions on a host are far less effective against an organisation that collects its logs elsewhere promptly.

In India, the CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022 require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations to maintain logs of their ICT systems securely for a rolling period of 180 days within Indian jurisdiction. That baseline is a minimum: a case may need specific logs preserved for much longer than routine retention.

## Legal hold

A **legal hold** (also called a litigation hold or preservation notice) is a formal instruction, normally issued by legal counsel, that information relevant to actual or reasonably anticipated litigation, regulatory investigation or dispute must be preserved, and that normal deletion, rotation and clean-up must be suspended for it. It applies to every form of the information: logs, emails, chat messages, tickets, case notes, disk images and backups.

The SOC's part in a legal hold is practical. Legal decides whether a hold is needed and what it covers; the SOC and IT operations implement it on the systems they run. That means identifying where the relevant data lives, stopping automated deletion for it (retention policies, mailbox clean-up, log rotation, scheduled reimaging of devices), confirming in writing that this has been done, and keeping the hold in place until legal releases it. A hold also binds the SOC's own records: the case notes, chat logs and tickets of the investigation itself may fall within it.

Two misunderstandings cause trouble. The first is waiting for a hold before preserving anything: the SOC's own evidence preservation should start at once, and a hold formalises and extends it. The second is assuming a hold is a technical setting; it is a legal obligation, and failure to honour it can have serious consequences in proceedings.

## Electronic evidence in Indian law

In India, electronic records are admissible as evidence under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 with effect from 1 July 2024. Section 63 of the new Act governs the admissibility of electronic records and, like section 65B of the old Act that it replaces, provides for a certificate accompanying the electronic record. Course 01 Module 7 notes that the prescribed certificate format refers to hash values; that detail, and how the certificate applies in a given case, is for legal advisers to determine. For the SOC analyst, the practical implication is clear: records exported with a documented method, a recorded hash and a complete custody record are exactly the material such a certificate needs, while records copied informally are not.

> [!CAUTION]
> The SOC preserves and documents evidence; it does not decide its legal sufficiency. Questions of admissibility, certification and disclosure belong to legal counsel.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"We will collect evidence after containment."** Containment chosen without evidence in mind (power-off, reimage) destroys the most volatile layers. Request memory capture with the isolation recommendation.
- **"The hash proves the evidence is genuine."** It proves the item is unchanged since the hash was recorded, nothing more. Completeness depends on the query and the source's integrity.
- **"The SIEM keeps everything."** Retention expires. Export case evidence and extend retention for the affected period.
- **"Screenshots are evidence."** A screenshot of a query result is a picture of evidence. Preserve the underlying records with a hash and custody entry.
- **"The manifest is the record."** As the WS-107 run showed, a tool can overwrite a manifest; the custody record, kept append-only, carries the history.
- **"Tidy up the analyst's working files."** Case notes and working copies may fall under a legal hold. Do not delete them until the case's retention rules allow it.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Evidence systems are targets. An attacker with administrative access may clear logs, as at Banyan, stop logging agents, or tamper with stored data. Defences include forwarding logs off the host promptly, alerting on log clearing and agent stoppage (event 1102 is a high-value detection, and the course's `rk_security_log_cleared.yml` rule detects it), storing evidence on write-once storage with tightly limited access, and keeping the custody record in a system with its own audit trail. Access to evidence stores should itself be logged and reviewed: an unexplained access is a break in the chain of custody.

Evidence also contains sensitive data. The WS-104 export holds user names, internal addresses and browsing history; a full disk image may hold personal data of customers. Preserved evidence must therefore be protected like any sensitive data set: encrypted at rest, shared on a need-to-know basis, and deleted when the case's retention period ends, in line with the DPDP Act's data protection obligations where personal data is involved.

## Best practices

- Preserve from the first hour, in order of volatility, in parallel with containment.
- Issue a clear "do not reboot, do not clean, do not reimage" instruction to the help desk and the user.
- Export raw records with a documented query, hash them with SHA-256 at collection time, and record a custody entry for every item.
- Verify the hash at every hand-over and before every use; record each verification.
- Keep custody records append-only, one entry per item and action, with UTC times.
- Extend retention for the affected sources and period; implement legal holds promptly when legal issues them.
- Record clock sources and offsets for every log source used in a timeline.

## Key takeaways

- Evidence must stay available, unchanged and explainable: order of volatility, hashing and chain of custody deliver those three properties.
- Collect from the most volatile layer down; isolation preserves every layer, power-off and reimaging destroy the top ones.
- A SHA-256 match proves an item is unchanged since its hash was recorded, not that it was complete; the documented query supplies reproducibility.
- In the lab, the export verified OK, a one-value change FAILED, a re-run reproduced the digest, and a second item exposed a manifest that is overwritten rather than appended.
- Preserve logs before retention removes them, implement legal holds when legal issues them, and leave admissibility questions (Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, section 63) to legal counsel.
