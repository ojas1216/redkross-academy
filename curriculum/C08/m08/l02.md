## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Module 5, Lesson 6 taught you how to escalate: a structured message that transfers ownership of a case to the IR lead and is complete only when it is acknowledged. That escalation is the first communication of an incident, not the last. Once an incident is declared, many more people need information: the teams who will contain it, the executives who must make business decisions, legal and compliance staff who must judge reporting duties, the affected users, and possibly regulators, customers and the police. Each needs different information, at a different time, through a different channel, and some must not receive certain information at all.

Poor communication is one of the most common reasons incidents go badly even when the technical work is sound. The classic failures are easy to recognise once named. An executive hears about the incident from a journalist. Three teams receive three different versions of the facts. A manager emails the compromised mailbox to ask what is happening, and the attacker reads it. A regulator's deadline passes because nobody told legal when the SOC first noticed the problem. A status update promises "contained" an hour before a second infected host is found.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 treats communication as part of the response itself. In the CSF 2.0 structure covered in Lesson 1, Incident Response Reporting and Communication (RS.CO) is a Category of the Respond Function and Incident Recovery Communication (RC.CO) a Category of Recover. Communication is not something done once the "real work" is finished; it is real work.

This lesson covers audiences, status updates, out-of-band channels, need-to-know and regulatory notifications. The SOC analyst is rarely the person who briefs the board or talks to the regulator. But almost every message that goes out during an incident is built on facts the SOC supplied, and the quality of those facts decides the quality of everything downstream.

> The Banyan Microfinance case from the course dataset is the running example. Event times come from `LAB_ASSETS/C08/banyan_events.jsonl`. SOC actions and their times (noticed 08:25Z, acknowledged 08:31Z, status updates) are illustrative and continue from Module 5, Lesson 6. IST is UTC+05:30.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | A person or group that needs information about the incident, defined by what they must decide or do. |
| Status update (situation report) | A periodic, structured summary of the incident's current state, sent to an agreed list at an agreed cadence. |
| Cadence | The agreed interval between status updates, normally set by severity and stated in every update ("next update 10:00Z"). |
| Incident log | The single, time-ordered record of facts, decisions, actions and communications for one incident, kept by a named scribe or in the case system. |
| Single source of truth | The one place (usually the case or incident log) that holds the authoritative current facts; every update is drawn from it. |
| Bridge | A call (or dedicated meeting room) where the incident's working group coordinates in real time. |
| In-band | Using the organisation's normal communication systems (corporate email, chat, ticketing). |
| Out-of-band (OOB) | Using a channel independent of the systems that may be compromised: a separate tenant or platform, phone bridges, pre-agreed numbers. |
| Need-to-know | The principle that information is shared only with those who require it to perform their role in the incident. |
| Codename | A neutral name for the incident used in messages and calendars so that its nature is not revealed to casual readers. |
| TLP (Traffic Light Protocol) | FIRST's labelling scheme for how far information may be shared: TLP:RED, TLP:AMBER+STRICT, TLP:AMBER, TLP:GREEN, TLP:CLEAR. |
| Holding statement | A short, pre-approved external statement acknowledging an issue without detail, prepared by communications and legal. |
| Tipping off | Letting the attacker (or, in an insider case, the subject) learn that they have been detected, through careless communication. |

## Who needs to know: internal and external audiences

The first communication task in an incident is to work out the audiences. A useful test for each candidate is: **what will this person decide or do with the information?** If there is no answer, they do not need it yet.

### Internal audiences

- **The IR lead (incident commander).** Owns the incident and coordinates all communication. Every other internal audience is served through, or with the agreement of, this role.
- **The SOC and technical responders.** Need full technical detail: indicators, hosts, queries, the timeline. They usually work from the case itself and the bridge.
- **IT operations, identity and network teams.** Need specific, actionable requests: "isolate WS-104", "reset and revoke sessions for divya and gopal", "block these two domains". They need just enough context to act correctly, plus explicit instructions about evidence ("do not reboot or reimage").
- **Executives and business owners.** Need impact, risk, decisions required and business workarounds, in plain language. In Banyan, the head of finance needs to know that a payments approver's laptop is out of action and that payments may need a second approver today.
- **Legal, compliance and the data protection function.** Need precise facts and times to assess obligations: when the incident was noticed, what systems and data may be involved, whether personal or financial data may have left. They decide reportability, not the SOC.
- **Human resources.** Involved when an employee's conduct is in question or when staff are affected. Not every incident needs HR; an insider case always does.
- **Corporate communications.** Prepares holding statements and customer or media messaging, always with legal.
- **The affected users and their managers.** Need to know what to do and not do (stop using the device, expect a password reset, report anything unusual), not the investigative detail.
- **The help desk.** Needs a script: callers reporting symptoms linked to the incident should be routed to the IR team, and the help desk must not "fix" affected machines. The fictional scenario in this course's case material, where a technician's helpful reboot destroyed memory evidence, is what happens when the help desk is forgotten.

### External audiences

- **CERT-In**, for incidents of the types listed in its Directions of 28 April 2022, reported through the organisation's designated point of contact (POC) within six hours of noticing.
- **Sector regulators**, for regulated entities. Banyan, as a microfinance company, would need to check its regulator's own requirements; these vary by sector and are for compliance to identify.
- **The Data Protection Board of India and affected Data Principals**, where a personal data breach falls under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Whether and how this applies is assessed by legal and the data protection function.
- **Law enforcement**, at the organisation's decision, typically through legal.
- **Customers and partners**, where their data or services are affected or contracts require notice.
- **Insurers**, if a cyber insurance policy requires prompt notification.
- **An IR retainer or external forensics firm**, engaged by the IR lead.
- **Information-sharing communities** such as an ISAC, under the appropriate TLP label, once the organisation agrees to share.

The common feature of every external audience is that the SOC analyst does not contact them directly. External communication goes through the designated roles: the POC for CERT-In, legal for regulators and law enforcement, communications for customers and media. The analyst's contribution is accurate facts delivered quickly to those roles.

The POC uses those facts in the CERT-In report.

**Security implications.** Routing through authorised roles limits the spread of sensitive detail, stops inconsistent statements and prevents an analyst's unverified hypothesis from appearing in a regulatory filing.

**Key learning.** The SOC feeds facts to the IR lead; only designated roles speak to external bodies.

## The incident log: one source of truth

Before looking at messages to other people, consider the record they are drawn from. Every incident needs one **incident log**: a time-ordered record of facts as they are established, decisions and who made them, actions and who carried them out, and communications sent. In a mature SOC this lives in the case-management system; in a smaller one it may be a controlled shared document kept by a named scribe. What matters is that there is exactly one, that everyone knows where it is, and that every entry has a UTC time and an author.

The log does three jobs. It is the **source for status updates**, so different audiences receive consistent facts. It is the **memory of the incident**, which the post-incident review (Lesson 6) depends on. And it is **evidence**: if a regulator, insurer or court later asks when the organisation knew something or why it chose to isolate a host at a particular time, the log answers.

A few entries from the Banyan log show the style. Each is short, dated, attributed and either a fact, a decision or an action, never a mixture:

```text
08:25Z  SOC-T1   FACT     R5 alert opened: Security log cleared on WS-104 (event 1102, 08:17Z). Time noticed.
08:31Z  IR-LEAD  DECISION Incident declared, codename HERON. IR lead owns. Severity SEV1.
08:40Z  SOC-T2   FACT     hunt.py H3: no other host contacted the case domains (proxy telemetry).
08:52Z  IR-LEAD  DECISION Isolate WS-104; block both domains and 203.0.113.66; reset divya, gopal.
09:05Z  IT-OPS   ACTION   WS-104 network-isolated via EDR.
09:20Z  SOC-T2   FACT     No requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example since 09:05Z.
```

The times after 08:25Z are illustrative. Notice that the analyst's entries are facts with their source ("proxy telemetry"), and that decisions are recorded under the name of the person who made them.

## Status updates: structure and cadence

Once an incident is running, people who are not on the bridge still need to know what is happening. Without a planned rhythm of updates, they ask, by phone and chat, constantly, and every question interrupts a responder. A **status update** at a known **cadence** answers the questions before they are asked and gives everyone the same picture.

### What a status update contains

A good update is short and always follows the same structure, so readers can find what they need:

| Section | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Header | Codename, update number, time (UTC and local), severity, current phase, author. |
| Summary | Two or three sentences: what is happening and the current state. |
| Changes since last update | What is new: findings, actions completed, scope changes. |
| Impact | Systems, users, data and business processes affected, with confidence. |
| Actions in progress and next steps | Who is doing what, with expected times. |
| Decisions needed | From whom, by when. |
| Next update | The time of the next update. |

The last line matters more than it looks. A promised time lets everyone stop asking. Missing it, even with nothing new to report, damages trust in the whole process; "no material change, next update 10:30Z" is a perfectly good update.

The update must keep the discipline of the escalation in Module 5, Lesson 6: **separate fact from hypothesis** and **label confidence**. The temptation in updates is to sound reassuring. "Contained" is a strong word. If the beacon has stopped but scoping is incomplete, the accurate statement is "The known command-and-control channel has stopped since WS-104 was isolated at 09:05Z; scoping of other systems continues."

### Cadence

Cadence is set by severity and phase, in the IR plan. A typical pattern is frequent updates in the first hours of a serious incident, when facts change quickly, then longer intervals as it stabilises. Each organisation sets its own intervals; what matters is that they are defined in advance, stated in each update, and changed only by the IR lead. A significant change, such as discovering a second infected host, triggers an immediate update outside the cadence.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows the cycle every status update goes through, from the incident log to the record of what was sent. It matters because updates written in a hurry, from memory, are where inconsistent facts and premature reassurances come from.

**Components.** Seven rounded boxes run from top to bottom. **Pull facts from the incident log** (slate, "single source of truth") is the starting point. **Separate confirmed from hypothesis** (blue, "label confidence") is the analytical discipline. **Write the update** (orange, "changes, state, actions, next update") is the drafting step using the fixed structure. **Tailor per audience** (violet, "technical, executive, user") and **Review** (violet, "IR lead; legal for anything external") are the control steps. **Send on an approved channel** (green, "out-of-band if in-band may be compromised") is delivery. **Log what was sent** (dark, "to whom, when, which version") closes the record.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows run downward in order; each step depends on the previous one. A dashed arrow, labelled **next update at the stated time, or sooner on a major change**, returns from the last step to the first, making this a loop rather than a one-off task.

**Execution flow.** Facts are taken from the log, never from memory or chat. They are sorted by confidence, written into the fixed structure, rewritten for each audience, reviewed, sent through the channel the incident plan approves, and logged. Then the cycle waits for the next scheduled time.

**Decision logic.** Two conditions shape the flow. If the update goes outside the organisation, legal must review it in the Review step. If there is any sign that email or chat is compromised, the Send step uses the out-of-band channel. The loop has its own condition: a major change restarts the cycle early.

**Technical interpretation.** Tailoring changes the presentation, never the facts. The executive version of "48,200,000 bytes uploaded to files-share-drop.example at 08:12Z" is "about 48 MB of data left the network to an unknown file-sharing service; content not yet known", and both versions must come from the same log entry.

**Practical example.** At 09:30Z the Banyan analyst drafts update 2 from the log: isolation at 09:05Z, no beacon since, gopal's credentials reset, scoping continuing. The IR lead reviews it, it is sent over the out-of-band bridge's chat, and the log records "Update 2 sent 09:32Z to HERON core list". The next is promised for 10:30Z.

**Security implications.** The Review step stops an analyst's hypothesis being presented as fact to executives or regulators. The channel condition stops an update from tipping off an attacker reading mail. Logging what was sent means the organisation can later show exactly what it knew and said at each time.

**Key learning.** Every update comes from the log, labels its confidence, is reviewed, goes out on a safe channel, is logged and promises the next one.

## Out-of-band communication

Module 5, Lesson 6 introduced the rule: do not discuss an incident on a channel the attacker may be able to read. During incident handling that rule becomes a practical question of what channel to use instead, and the answer must be prepared before the incident.

### When to go out of band

Move incident communication off normal systems when any of these is true or plausible:

- A **mailbox** involved in the incident may be compromised. In Banyan, the malicious document ran as divya, so anything in or reachable from her mailbox, including threads she is copied on, may be visible to the attacker.
- **Identity infrastructure** may be compromised: a directory or identity-provider administrator account, a new privileged account. An attacker with administrative access to the tenant can read anyone's mail and chat.
- The **collaboration platform** or its administrators are in scope.
- The incident is an **insider** case and the subject has access to normal channels or their logs.
- Normal systems are **unavailable**, as in ransomware that has encrypted servers.

In Banyan, a new local administrator account on one workstation does not by itself prove tenant compromise, but the compromise of a user with mail access and an attacker with an unexplained VPN session make out-of-band communication the prudent default until scoping says otherwise. The decision, and its reason, goes in the incident log.

### What an out-of-band channel looks like

An out-of-band channel is independent of the systems that may be compromised: separate accounts and authentication, ideally a separate provider. Common forms are a pre-arranged phone bridge, a collaboration space hosted outside the corporate tenant with separate credentials, and printed or offline contact lists with phone numbers. Whatever is chosen must meet three conditions:

1. **Prepared in advance.** Setting up a new channel during the incident, by emailing invitations from the compromised mail system, defeats its purpose.
2. **Identity-checked.** Participants must be sure who they are talking to. An attacker with mail access may know the bridge exists; join codes should not be sent in-band, and unfamiliar voices or new participants should be verified by calling back on a known number.
3. **Recorded.** Decisions made on the bridge still go into the incident log. Out of band does not mean off the record.

> [!WARNING]
> Never use an affected device or account to communicate about the incident. In Banyan, nobody should message divya on WS-104 or at her corporate mailbox about the case. Reach her by phone, through her manager, and give instructions verbally: do not use the laptop, do not delete anything, expect a replacement device.

## Need-to-know in practice

Need-to-know protects the investigation, the people involved (an employee whose account was used is a victim until proven otherwise) and the organisation. Practical techniques:

- **Use a codename.** "HERON" in a calendar invitation, ticket title or bridge name reveals nothing; "WS-104 data theft finance" reveals a great deal to anyone browsing the ticket queue.
- **Keep distribution lists small and named.** A core list for full updates, a wider list for summaries, and nothing to all-staff lists unless communications and legal decide to.
- **Give action teams what they need to act, not the whole story.** The network team needs the domains and the address to block, not the identity of the finance user.
- **Use restricted case access.** The incident case should be visible only to its working group; ordinary tickets for related tasks should not contain the details.
- **Label shared information with TLP.** When indicators are shared with partners or an ISAC, the TLP label tells recipients how far they may pass it on. The ISAC indicator for `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` arrived as TLP:AMBER, which limits sharing to the recipient's organisation and its clients on a need-to-know basis; it should not appear in a public statement.
- **Avoid tipping off.** Do not change visible things before containment is coordinated: blocking the beacon domain an hour before isolating the host tells the attacker they are seen while their foothold is still live. Communication and containment timing must be planned together, which Lesson 3 develops.

Need-to-know is not secrecy: people who must act must still be told enough to act correctly.

## Regulatory notifications: the SOC's part

The legal detail of Indian reporting obligations belongs to Course 01 and to the organisation's legal team. What the SOC must understand is how communication inside the incident makes timely notification possible.

**CERT-In.** Under the CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022, issued under section 70B(6) of the Information Technology Act, 2000, covered entities must report the incident types listed in the Directions within six hours of noticing them or being brought to notice of them, through their designated point of contact. Six hours is short. The report will be based on what is known at that moment, so the facts the SOC supplies must carry their confidence labels, and the SOC must keep supplying facts as the investigation develops.

**Personal data.** The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 requires a Data Fiduciary, in the event of a personal data breach, to intimate the Data Protection Board of India and each affected Data Principal. The form, content and timing are set by rules whose application legal must confirm at the time of the incident. In Banyan, the unknown content of the 48 MB upload is exactly the question that decides this, which is why update 2 called it "our main open question".

**Others.** Sector regulators, contracts and insurance policies may impose their own notifications; compliance identifies them. The SOC's duties are concrete:

1. **Get the time noticed to legal and the POC immediately**, in UTC and IST, together with the derived CERT-In six-hour point. For Banyan: noticed 08:25Z (13:55 IST), six-hour point 14:25Z (19:55 IST).
2. **Supply the facts reports need**: incident type as the evidence shows it, affected systems, indicators, time of first malicious activity, actions taken, and what data may be involved.
3. **State unknowns explicitly**, with what is being done to resolve them and when an answer is expected.
4. **Update legal whenever a fact changes** that could alter reportability or the content of a report: a second host, evidence that personal data was in the upload, or evidence that it was not.
5. **Never notify, estimate obligations, or promise outcomes yourself.** As Module 5 warned, legal interpretations in a case note mislead.

> [!CAUTION]
> A regulator's deadline is measured from the organisation's noticing, not from the end of the investigation. If the SOC sits on facts while it "gets them right", the people accountable lose time they cannot recover.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Reassuring too early.** "Contained" or "no data lost" before scoping is complete becomes a false statement that must later be retracted, often in front of a regulator.
- **Different facts for different audiences.** Tailoring changes language and detail, never the facts; every version comes from the log.
- **Discussing the incident in-band.** Emailing the compromised user, or creating the bridge invitation from a mailbox the attacker can read.
- **Unprepared out-of-band channels.** A channel created during the incident, with invitations sent in-band, is not out of band.
- **Missing a promised update.** Silence prompts interruptions and rumours; a "no change" update keeps trust.
- **Forgetting the help desk and the affected user.** Well-meaning fixes destroy evidence.
- **Holding facts back from legal** until they are certain, which consumes the reporting window.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Communication channels are part of the attack surface during an incident: an attacker who reads the investigation can accelerate or destroy evidence. Attackers also exploit the confusion of an incident with impersonation: a message apparently from the IR lead asking someone to "disable the EDR agent for testing" or to send credentials. Defences are mostly preparation: a tested out-of-band channel, pre-agreed identity checks, an IR plan that names who may instruct whom, and a rule that instructions affecting security controls are confirmed through a known channel. The SOC can also monitor the incident itself: watch for logons to mailboxes of incident participants from unusual sources, mailbox rule changes and access to the incident case by unexpected accounts. In Banyan, any new mailbox forwarding rule on divya's account during the incident would be a strong sign that the attacker was watching.

## Best practices

1. Keep one incident log with UTC times, authors and labels (fact, decision, action, communication).
2. Identify audiences by what they must decide or do; route everything through the IR lead.
3. Use a fixed status update structure with confidence labels and a stated next-update time, and keep the cadence.
4. Tailor wording per audience, never the facts.
5. Prepare the out-of-band channel, contact lists and identity checks before an incident, and switch to them on any sign of mail, chat or identity compromise.
6. Use codenames, small named lists and restricted case access; respect TLP labels.
7. Send legal and the POC the time noticed and six-hour point at once, and update them on every material change.
8. Never let the SOC notify external bodies or estimate legal obligations.

## Key takeaways

- Communication is part of the response (RS.CO and RC.CO in CSF 2.0), built on facts the SOC supplies.
- One incident log is the single source of truth; every status update is drawn from it, labels confidence and promises the next update.
- Audiences are defined by what they must decide or do; the IR lead coordinates them, and only designated roles speak externally.
- Use prepared, identity-checked out-of-band channels whenever mail, chat or identity systems may be compromised, and never contact the affected device or mailbox about the incident.
- Need-to-know, codenames and TLP protect the investigation and the people involved without leaving action teams uninformed.
- Under the CERT-In Directions, reportable incidents must be reported within six hours of noticing; the SOC's job is to give legal and the POC the time noticed and accurate, confidence-labelled facts at once.
