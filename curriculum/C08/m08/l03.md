## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Detection tells you that something is wrong. Containment is the first thing the organisation does about it. It is the set of actions that limit the harm an incident can still cause: stopping an intruder from reaching more systems, cutting off a command-and-control channel, preventing more data from leaving, and taking away the credentials an attacker is using. Containment is not the same as fixing the problem. The malware may still be on the disk, the account the attacker created may still exist, and the weakness that let them in may still be open. Those are the jobs of eradication and recovery. Containment buys time and stops the damage from growing while those jobs are planned and done properly.

In most organisations the SOC analyst does not press the containment buttons alone. The analyst, who understands the evidence best, **recommends**: which actions, in which order, against which hosts, accounts and indicators, with what risks. An authorised person, usually the incident lead, **decides**, and IT operations, identity or network teams **execute**. Containment interrupts real people and processes (isolating a laptop stops its owner working; disabling an account may stop a payments run), so someone with authority to accept that impact must make the call.

A good recommendation is specific (exact host, account and indicator), complete (every foothold the evidence shows), ordered (what happens together, what waits), honest about trade-offs and reversible where possible. A poor one is "please isolate the machine", sent by email, about one host, while the attacker still holds a VPN session and a privileged local account.

This lesson builds that skill on the Banyan Microfinance dataset: the menu of containment actions, short-term versus long-term containment, the risk of tipping off the attacker, who decides, and a recommendation for WS-104, gopal and svc_update. Lesson 4 deals with the evidence that containment must not destroy.

> [!NOTE]
> NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 (2012) described a lifecycle with a combined "containment, eradication and recovery" phase. Rev. 3 (April 2025) reorganises incident response around the CSF 2.0 functions, but the containment vocabulary used in this lesson remains standard in playbooks and in practice.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Containment | Actions that limit the further harm of an incident: spread, data loss, misuse of access, damage to systems. |
| Short-term containment | Fast, usually reversible actions that stop the immediate harm (isolate a host, block an indicator, disable an account) while investigation continues. |
| Long-term containment | Interim measures that let the business keep operating safely until eradication and recovery are complete (rebuilt or replacement systems, tightened access, enhanced monitoring). |
| Eradication | Removing the attacker's presence: malware, persistence, unauthorised accounts, and closing the entry weakness. |
| Recovery | Restoring systems to normal operation from known-good sources and confirming that the attacker has not returned. |
| Foothold | Any means by which the attacker can still act: a compromised host, a live session, a valid credential, a persistence mechanism, an open C2 channel. |
| Tipping off | Alerting the attacker that they have been detected, so that they change behaviour, accelerate, destroy evidence or hide. |
| Network isolation (EDR) | An endpoint security feature that blocks a host's network traffic except to the security management console, keeping the host running and reachable for investigation. |
| Session revocation | Invalidating existing sessions and tokens issued to an account, so that a password reset is not undermined by sessions that are already authenticated. |
| Containment authority | The pre-agreed statement of who may approve which containment actions, at which severity, and which actions the SOC may take on its own. |

## Why containment is a decision, not a reflex

The instinctive reaction to a compromised machine is to pull the plug. The professional reaction is to ask what the plug is connected to. Every containment action has three effects at once, and the analyst's job is to make all three visible before anyone acts.

**It reduces harm.** This is the reason for acting at all. An isolated host can no longer reach its command-and-control server or the file server next to it. A disabled account can no longer be used to sign in. A blocked domain can no longer receive exfiltrated data.

**It costs something.** Business costs (a user who cannot work, a process that stops), investigation costs (a powered-off host loses its memory, a deleted account loses its history) and response costs (a blocked domain may push the attacker to one you do not know yet).

**It sends a signal.** An attacker watching their access notices a silent beacon, a failed password or a closed session. What they do next may be worse than the activity you were trying to stop.

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 lists criteria for choosing a containment strategy that capture this balance: potential damage to and theft of resources, the need to preserve evidence, service availability, the time and resources needed, effectiveness (partial or full containment) and duration (emergency workaround or permanent solution).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Never let the search for perfect evidence delay containment of serious, ongoing harm. If data is leaving or systems are being encrypted right now, stopping it comes first. Evidence preservation (Lesson 4) shapes *how* you contain, not *whether* you contain.

## The containment menu

Containment actions fall into a few families, each with a characteristic strength, cost and signal.

### Host containment

**Network isolation through EDR** is the default first action for a compromised workstation in organisations that have endpoint detection and response. The EDR agent blocks all network traffic except the connection to its own management console. The host stays powered on, so running processes and memory are preserved for collection; the analyst and forensic team can still reach it through the console to collect evidence; and the attacker loses the network path to and from the host. Its costs are user disruption and the fact that a beacon that stops checking in is noticeable to the attacker.

**Switch-port or VLAN isolation** is the fallback without EDR; it usually also cuts the management channel, making live collection harder.

**Powering off** stops everything, including the attacker, but destroys volatile evidence: memory contents, running processes, open network connections and decrypted material that exists only in memory. It is rarely the right first choice. The exception is ongoing destruction, for example ransomware actively encrypting a file share, where every minute running costs more than the evidence is worth.

### Account and credential containment

**Disabling an account** stops new sign-ins with it. **Resetting a password** stops the old password working. Neither, on its own, necessarily ends sessions that are already authenticated: an attacker who holds a live VPN session, a valid web session cookie or an issued access token may keep working until that session expires. Effective credential containment therefore pairs a reset or disablement with **revoking sessions and tokens**, and, where multi-factor authentication methods may have been tampered with, reviewing and resetting the registered MFA methods. The exact mechanisms differ by identity platform, and your playbook should name them.

Account actions also carry business cost (disabling a payments approver mid-run is a financial decision) and a strong signal: the attacker's next sign-in fails.

**Local accounts** need special care. An account created on one workstation exists only in that machine's local account database, not in the central directory. Disabling it requires action on the host itself (through the EDR console, endpoint management or a forensic responder), and the same account name may need to be searched for on every other host.

### Network containment

**Blocking domains and IP addresses** at the proxy, DNS resolver or firewall stops known command-and-control and exfiltration destinations for every host at once, including hosts you have not yet identified as compromised. It is cheap, fast and reversible, and the business impact is usually negligible when the destinations are attacker-controlled. Its weakness is that it is only as good as your indicators: an attacker with other infrastructure can switch to it. Blocking also has a scoping benefit. With the block logging enabled, any other host that tries to reach the same destination identifies itself.

**Blocking a source IP** at the VPN or perimeter stops further attempts from a known address, but complements account containment rather than replacing it.

### Persistence and access containment

**Disabling persistence** (unauthorised accounts, scheduled tasks, services, startup entries) removes the attacker's way back in. Disabling is usually preferred to deleting during containment: a disabled account keeps its creation record, group memberships and profile for investigation, while a deleted one loses them. Full removal belongs to eradication, after evidence has been captured.

## Short-term and long-term containment

Widely used incident-handling training, such as the SANS six-step model, divides containment into two stages, and the distinction is useful even where your playbook uses other words.

**Short-term containment** happens in minutes to hours. Its goal is to stop the immediate harm with actions that are fast and, ideally, reversible: isolate the host, block the destinations, disable the accounts, close the sessions. Short-term containment accepts disruption because the alternative is continuing damage. It is designed so that evidence collection can proceed straight afterwards: an EDR-isolated host is still running and reachable for memory and disk capture.

**Long-term containment** covers the period, often days, between stopping the immediate harm and completing eradication and recovery. The organisation must keep working while the investigation finishes and clean systems are prepared. Typical long-term measures are: giving the affected user a clean replacement device rather than returning the original; moving a critical function to a standby system; applying a temporary firewall rule or conditional access policy; requiring step-up authentication for a group of accounts; and running enhanced monitoring (extra detections, shorter review intervals) on the affected users and hosts. Long-term containment measures are temporary by definition, so each needs an owner and an end condition, or they become permanent technical debt.

| Aspect | Short-term containment | Long-term containment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Stop harm now | Operate safely until eradication and recovery |
| Timescale | Minutes to hours | Days to weeks |
| Typical actions | EDR isolation, block indicators, disable accounts, revoke sessions | Replacement device, standby systems, temporary rules, enhanced monitoring |
| Evidence stance | Keep systems running for collection | Preserve images and records before rebuild |
| Who usually executes | SOC (if pre-authorised), IT ops, identity team | IT ops, application owners, identity and network teams |
| End condition | Harm stopped and footholds controlled | Systems rebuilt or restored and monitoring shows no return |

## The risk of tipping off

An attacker who has invested in access to an organisation watches that access. Commodity malware may simply stop working when blocked, but a hands-on-keyboard intruder notices when a beacon goes silent or a credential fails, and reacts. The reactions that matter to a responder are:

- **Acceleration.** Taking whatever data can still be reached, or triggering destructive payloads, before access is lost entirely.
- **Destruction of evidence.** Clearing logs, deleting tools and staged archives, removing accounts that would reveal how they operated.
- **Migration.** Moving to a foothold the defenders have not found: another compromised account, another host, a different command-and-control domain.
- **Dormancy.** Going quiet on the known footholds while keeping a hidden one, so that the defenders declare victory too early.

The practical rule that follows is: **contain all known footholds at the same time**, and do not take a visible action against one of them while the others remain open. Isolating the workstation but leaving the attacker's VPN session alive tells them which part of their access has been found and leaves them a route to react. Resetting a password an hour before isolating the host gives the attacker an hour of warning.

Tipping off also happens through communication: emailing a compromised user about the investigation, or discussing containment in a chat platform the attacker may be reading. Use the out-of-band channels from Lesson 2.

The opposite temptation also exists: delaying containment to observe the attacker, learn their infrastructure and contain more completely later. This can be legitimate in mature teams, sometimes at the request of law enforcement, but it means knowingly leaving an intruder active inside the organisation. It is a decision for the incident lead with legal and business owners, never for the analyst alone, and it is not appropriate when data is actively leaving or harm is ongoing.

> [!WARNING]
> Coordinated containment does not mean slow containment. If evidence shows serious harm in progress, such as a large upload under way or encryption starting, contain the active foothold immediately and accept the tip-off risk. Coordination is for the minutes you have, not a reason to wait.

**Purpose.** The figure is a decision aid. It sets six common containment actions side by side so that their trade-offs can be compared at a glance, which is exactly what an incident lead needs from a recommendation. It matters because the action that stops the attacker most completely is not always the right first choice: the best plan combines actions whose strengths cover each other's weaknesses.

**Components.** The table has five columns: **Action**, **Stops attacker**, **Keeps evidence**, **Business impact** and **Tip-off signal**. Each of the six rows is one action from the containment menu. **EDR network isolation** rates high for stopping the attacker on that host, high for keeping evidence (the host keeps running), medium for business impact and noticeable as a signal. **Block domain / IP** rates partial for stopping the attacker, high for evidence, low for business impact and low as a signal. **Disable + revoke sessions** rates high (for credential misuse), high for evidence, medium for impact and strong as a signal. **Disable persistence account** rates partial (it removes the way back, not current access), high for evidence because the account is kept, low for impact and medium as a signal. **Power off** rates high for stopping, low for evidence, high for impact and strong as a signal. **Wipe and reimage** rates high for stopping on that host, very low for evidence, high for impact and strong as a signal. Rows are tinted by their overall standing: green for the default first actions, orange for the action that needs care, and red for the two actions that destroy evidence.

**Relationships.** Reading across a row gives one action's profile; reading down a column compares actions on one criterion. The **Keeps evidence** column is where the two red rows stand out: they are the only options rated low or very low, which is why they are reserved for special cases.

**Execution flow.** In practice you assemble a plan by choosing a set of rows that together cover every foothold, then ordering them so that the signalling actions happen at the same time. For a typical workstation compromise that means EDR isolation, indicator blocks and account actions together, with persistence disabled through the isolated host's management channel.

**Decision logic.** The implicit rule is: prefer green rows; use the orange row with a stated reason; use red rows only when harm is ongoing and cannot be stopped another way (power off) or when evidence has already been captured (wipe and reimage, which belongs to recovery rather than containment).

**Technical interpretation.** Isolation and blocking act on the network path; disable-and-revoke acts on authentication; disabling persistence acts on the attacker's re-entry. They are complementary layers, not alternatives, which is why the table has no single winner.

**Practical example.** For the Banyan case, the green rows map to isolating WS-104, blocking the three attacker domains and 203.0.113.66, and disabling and revoking the sessions of the affected accounts. The orange row maps to disabling svc_update on WS-104. Neither red row is recommended.

**Security implications.** The **Tip-off signal** column is the reason for coordination. Four of the six actions send a noticeable or strong signal; taking them minutes apart rather than together gives the attacker time to react.

**Key learning.** No single containment action is best on every criterion; a sound recommendation combines several, keeps evidence, and times the signalling actions together.

## Who decides

Containment interrupts the business, so its authority must be settled before an incident, not argued about during one. Mature organisations write a **containment authority** into their incident response plan or playbooks, stating which actions the SOC may take on its own and which need approval, from whom, at which severity.

A common pattern is:

- **Pre-authorised SOC actions.** Low-impact, reversible actions the SOC may take immediately, often for any confirmed incident: blocking a confirmed-malicious domain or IP, isolating a standard workstation through EDR at the highest severities, disabling a clearly unauthorised account. The analyst still records each action and informs the incident lead.
- **Incident lead approval.** Actions with real business impact or wider consequences: isolating a critical user's device or a server, disabling a staff account, mass password resets, segmentation changes.
- **Business owner consultation.** Where an action stops a business process (a payments run, a production line, a customer-facing service), the owner of that process is consulted on timing and workarounds. They do not usually have a veto over stopping active harm, but they own the business continuity plan.
- **Executive and legal involvement.** Decisions such as deliberately delaying containment to observe the attacker, shutting down a business service entirely, or acting on a law-enforcement request.

The analyst's part is to make the decision easy: name the action, target, reason, impact, evidence consideration and suggested owner, so the incident lead can approve in one reply. Every approval and execution is recorded with a time in the incident log, which is itself evidence and the basis of the post-incident review (Lesson 6).

> [!TIP]
> If you are unsure whether an action is pre-authorised, treat it as not pre-authorised. Recommend it, state the time pressure, and escalate. A two-minute wait for approval rarely decides an incident; an unauthorised outage of a business-critical system can become an incident of its own.

The first arrow, **Recommendation: actions, order, impact**, goes from the analyst to the incident lead. **Execution flow.** Reading top to bottom gives the order: recommend, consult, approve, execute, confirm, verify. In a real incident each arrow carries a UTC time in the incident log; the course dataset contains the attacker's events, not the SOC's response, so no response times are shown.

**Decision logic.** The incident lead's step contains the decision: approve as recommended, approve with changes (for example a slightly later time to allow a workaround), or reject with reasons. The consultation with the business owner informs the timing and workaround, not whether active harm is stopped.

**Security implications.** The executors must act on all items in the same window; the arrow labelled "execute together" is where the tip-off rule is enforced. Verification closes the loop: an action that is approved but not confirmed is not yet containment.

**Key learning.** The analyst recommends and verifies, the incident lead decides, the business owner shapes the workaround and the operations teams execute, and every step is time-stamped.

## Worked example: containment advice for the Banyan incident

The Banyan Microfinance dataset (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/banyan_events.jsonl`, synthetic) records one intrusion on 3 November 2026. Earlier modules triaged it; here you turn the evidence into a containment recommendation. The first task is to list every foothold, because a plan that misses one is the plan that tips the attacker off.

### Step 1: establish the footholds from evidence

In a temporary copy of the kit (never the kit folder itself), load the dataset and query the three entities the incident lead asked about: the VPN activity behind R2, gopal, and svc_update.

`load` reads the JSONL file into a local SQLite database; `search` runs the quoted SQL against its `events` table and prints the columns separated by `|`. The `WHERE` clauses select the VPN source and the attacker's address, the VPN events for one user, and any event in which svc_update is the actor or the target.

Interpretation, fact by fact:

- **The VPN spray.** Eight different accounts failed from 203.0.113.66 at seven-second intervals, one attempt each: the shape of password spraying (ATT&CK T1110.003). Four minutes later gopal's account **succeeded** from the same address, with the VPN's country field unknown (`??` in the raw events). That successful session is a foothold: a valid credential with remote access to the network.
- **gopal's other activity.** A further success at 13:33Z from 198.51.100.104 (country `IN`) may be the real gopal working remotely, so a reset will interrupt a genuine user who must be given new credentials out of band. His own device, WS-107 (sales laptop), was on the internal network at 10.40.0.26 through the morning.
- **svc_update.** Created on WS-104 (event 4720) at 07:02Z and added to the local Administrators group (4732) at 07:03Z, with divya recorded as the acting account. It is a **local** account: disabling it in the central directory would do nothing.
- **WS-104 itself.** From earlier modules: `invoice.docm` from banyan-payroll-update.example at 06:12Z, Word spawning hidden encoded PowerShell at 06:14Z, 18 regular 220-byte requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example from 06:17Z to 07:42Z, a 48.2 MB upload to files-share-drop.example at 08:12Z, and the Security log cleared at 08:17Z.

Is the VPN session connected to WS-104? The evidence does not say: the activities overlap in time but share no host or address (divya's account was among those sprayed, but that attempt failed). Treat the VPN session as a **separate foothold** and state the link as an open question.

### Step 2: list footholds and map each to actions

| Foothold | Evidence | Short-term action | Owner | Evidence consideration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| WS-104 host | Office-spawned PowerShell, beacon, upload, log cleared | EDR network isolation (not power-off) | IT ops / SOC if pre-authorised | Keeps memory and disk for Lesson 4 collection |
| C2 and exfiltration infrastructure | Three `.example` domains in proxy logs | Block at proxy and DNS, with block logging | Network team | Block logs reveal any other host that tries |
| Spray source | 203.0.113.66 in VPN logs | Block at VPN and perimeter | Network team | Keep VPN logs for the address |
| gopal credential and VPN session | Spray then success at 06:46Z | Disable or reset, terminate VPN sessions, revoke tokens, review MFA methods | Identity team | Export VPN logs before session data ages out |
| divya credential | Account used to run the payload and create svc_update | Reset, revoke sessions and tokens | Identity team | Note: the reset does not clean WS-104 |
| svc_update local admin | 4720 / 4732 on WS-104 | Disable on the host through the EDR or management channel; do not delete | IT ops | Deletion would lose the account's profile and records |

### Step 3: decide the order and the timing

All six actions send some signal to an attacker, and three of them (isolation and the two staff-account actions) send a noticeable or strong one. The recommendation is therefore one coordinated window, preceded only by the steps that do not signal anything:

1. **Before the window (silent).** Confirm the SOC's own channels are out of band (Lesson 2). Export the relevant logs, as Lesson 4 describes, so that containment cannot age them out. Brief the identity, network and IT operations executors by phone or the out-of-band bridge. Ask the finance head for a payments workaround.
2. **In the window (together, within minutes).** Isolate WS-104; apply the domain and IP blocks; disable and reset gopal, terminate his VPN sessions and revoke tokens; reset divya and revoke her sessions; disable svc_update through the isolated host's management channel.
3. **Straight after.** Verify (Step 5), then hand over to evidence collection on WS-104 and to scoping hunts: search every host for an account named svc_update and for any traffic to the three domains.

One caveat: the upload completed at 08:12Z. Had it been noticed while still running, the advice would change: isolate WS-104 at once, accept the tip-off risk, and coordinate the rest immediately afterwards.

### Step 4: write the recommendation

```text
CASE-0001 CONTAINMENT RECOMMENDATION (for incident lead approval)
Coordinated window: all items together; executors briefed out of band.
1 WS-104       EDR network isolation. NOT power-off, NOT reimage.
               Impact: payments approver offline -> deputy approves;
               clean replacement device for divya.
2 Domains      Block banyan-payroll-update.example,
               cdn-telemetry-sync.example, files-share-drop.example
               at proxy + DNS; log block hits.
3 IP           Block 203.0.113.66 at VPN + perimeter.
4 gopal        Disable/reset, end VPN sessions, revoke tokens,
               review MFA methods. Contact gopal by phone.
5 divya        Reset, revoke sessions and tokens.
6 svc_update   Disable (not delete) on WS-104 via EDR console.
Evidence:      WS-104 events exported and hashed before the window;
               memory and disk capture after isolation (forensics).
Open question: link between VPN session and WS-104 unproven.
After:         verify silence; hunt for svc_update and the domains
               on all hosts; eradication and rebuild plan to follow.
Decision requested within 15 minutes.
```

Each line names a target, an action and, where it matters, the impact and evidence consequence; the open question keeps a hypothesis from being read as a finding.

### Step 5: verify

Containment is complete only when confirmed. Check the SIEM for successful requests to the three domains after the block (block hits are expected and useful), VPN authentication from 203.0.113.66, sign-ins by gopal or divya not made by the real users, and the EDR console's confirmation that WS-104 is isolated. Log each check and result.

## Long-term containment for Banyan

WS-104 stays isolated until memory and disk have been collected, and is then rebuilt from a known-good image rather than cleaned. divya works on a replacement device with enhanced monitoring of her account. Finance reviews any payments approved from divya's account after the first malicious activity, because a compromised approver's workstation is an obvious route to payment fraud; the SOC recommends that review and finance owns it. The VPN gains near-real-time detection on the logic of rule R2, and the organisation reviews whether VPN access needs phishing-resistant MFA. Each item gets an owner and an end date, and all of them feed the post-incident review in Lesson 6.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"Containment means powering it off."** Powering off loses memory, running processes and network state. Isolate instead unless destruction is under way.
- **"A password reset locks the attacker out."** Existing sessions and tokens may survive it. Revoke sessions, end VPN connections and review MFA methods in the same action.
- **"Contain the first host, then look for more."** Containing one foothold while others stay open warns the attacker.
- **"Delete the malicious account."** Disable it during containment; deletion destroys its history. Removal belongs to eradication.
- **"The analyst should just do it."** Unless the action is pre-authorised, the analyst recommends and the incident lead decides.
- **"Containment ends the incident."** It stops the harm growing; eradication, recovery and lessons learned still follow.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Containment advice is only as good as the preparation behind it: EDR with network isolation on every supported host, central domain and IP blocking with logging, a practised identity procedure for disable, reset, session revocation and MFA review, a written containment authority, out-of-band channels, and continuity plans for the critical users whose interruption containment will cause. The tabletop in Lesson 7 tests these without an attacker present.

After containment, detection keeps working. Block-log hits, sign-in attempts on reset accounts and the scoping hunt's results are how you learn that containment was incomplete. The containment actions themselves (isolation events, rule changes, account administration) are security-relevant changes and must be logged, so that the post-incident review can reconstruct exactly what was done and when.

## Best practices

- Map every foothold to an action before recommending any, and say which may still be unknown.
- Prefer reversible, evidence-preserving actions: isolate rather than power off, disable rather than delete.
- Do the silent steps first, then take all signalling actions in one window, unless harm is in progress.
- Write each line with target, action, impact, evidence consideration and owner, and ask for a decision by a stated time.
- Verify after execution and record every approval, action and check with a UTC time.
