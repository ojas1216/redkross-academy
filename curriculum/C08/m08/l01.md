## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Module 5 ended at the moment of escalation: a Tier 1 analyst decides that a group of alerts is serious enough to hand to the incident response (IR) lead, writes a structured escalation and waits for the acknowledgement that transfers ownership. This module starts on the other side of that hand-off. Once a case has been declared an incident, the work changes character. More people are involved, decisions have business consequences, legal and regulatory clocks may be running, and the SOC analyst is no longer the sole owner of the problem but one contributor among several.

Incidents are stressful, and stress makes people improvise. Improvisation is exactly what an organisation cannot afford during an intrusion: one team wipes a laptop that another team needed for evidence, a manager emails a compromised mailbox about the investigation, nobody records when the incident was noticed, and three weeks later nobody can say what was learned. An **incident response lifecycle** is the antidote. It is a shared model of the phases an incident passes through, what each phase must achieve, and what it hands to the next. When everyone in the room uses the same model, the IR lead can say "we are still in analysis; nobody contains anything until we have scoped it" and every participant knows what that means.

The lifecycle most widely used in playbooks is the one published by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology in **NIST Special Publication 800-61**, the *Computer Security Incident Handling Guide*. Its Revision 2 (August 2012) described four phases: Preparation; Detection and Analysis; Containment, Eradication and Recovery; and Post-Incident Activity. In April 2025 NIST published **Revision 3**, retitled *Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management: A CSF 2.0 Community Profile*. Rev. 3 supersedes Rev. 2 and changes the framing: rather than a stand-alone lifecycle, incident response is described as part of organisation-wide risk management using the six Functions of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0. The older phase names remain the everyday vocabulary of SOCs and playbooks, so this lesson teaches both: the four classic phases as the working model, and the CSF 2.0 framing as the way the current standard describes the same work.

Throughout, the running example is the Banyan Microfinance intrusion in the course dataset (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/banyan_events.jsonl`): a finance user opens a malicious document, Word launches hidden PowerShell, the workstation beacons to a rare domain, a local administrator account appears, 48,200,000 bytes leave for a file-sharing look-alike and the Security log is cleared, while a password spray against the VPN succeeds for a second user. You have already triaged and escalated these alerts in Modules 4 and 5. Here you will place each of those activities in the lifecycle and see what the SOC analyst does in every phase.

> [!NOTE]
> All Banyan events are synthetic and come from the execution-tested lab kit. Times shown with a `Z` are UTC; India Standard Time (IST) is UTC+05:30. SOC activity times such as "noticed 08:25Z" are illustrative and match Module 5, Lesson 6; the dataset contains events, not SOC actions.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Event | Any observable occurrence in a system or network: a logon, a process start, a web request. Most events are benign. |
| Adverse event | An event with a negative consequence or the potential for one, for example a failed logon burst or a blocked malware download. |
| Incident | An occurrence that actually or imminently jeopardises the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information or systems, or violates security policy. Declared by an authorised role, not by a single alert. |
| Precursor | A sign that an incident may occur in the future, such as a vulnerability scan against a web server or a threat advisory naming your sector. |
| Indicator | A sign that an incident may have occurred or may be occurring now, such as Word spawning PowerShell on a finance workstation. |
| Incident declaration | The recorded decision, by the role the playbook names, that a case is an incident. It starts the IR process and its clocks. |
| Containment | Actions that stop an incident from spreading or causing more harm, buying time for eradication: isolating a host, blocking a domain, disabling an account. |
| Eradication | Removing the attacker's presence: malware, persistence, unauthorised accounts, and the weakness they used. |
| Recovery | Restoring affected systems and services to normal operation from known-good state, with monitoring for re-entry. |
| Scoping | Determining every affected host, account and data set, so that containment covers the whole incident rather than the first visible part. |
| CSIRT | Computer security incident response team: the people (permanent or virtual) who handle declared incidents. The SOC is usually a key part of it, not the whole of it. |
| Incident commander / IR lead | The single person who owns decisions and coordination for one incident. |

The distinction between event, alert and incident matters because each is handled by a different process. Events are collected and stored (Module 2). Alerts are raised by rules over events and triaged by the SOC (Modules 4 and 5). Incidents are declared from cases and handled by the IR process this module describes. A SOC that calls every true-positive alert an "incident" floods the IR process; a SOC that never declares lets serious intrusions stay inside the triage queue.

## The classic lifecycle: four phases with feedback

Rev. 2 of SP 800-61 drew its lifecycle as four phases with two important feedback paths. Neither path is a detail. Real incidents loop: containment reveals new indicators that send the team back to analysis, and every incident's lessons are supposed to change preparation for the next one.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows the incident response lifecycle of NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 as a set of phases connected by the order in which work flows. It matters because it gives everyone in an incident the same map: which phase the team is in, what must be finished before moving on, and where the work is allowed to loop back.

**Components.** Four rounded boxes run left to right. **Preparation** (slate, "people, tools, playbooks, telemetry") is everything done before an incident. **Detection and Analysis** (blue, "alerts, triage, scoping, declare") is where the SOC spends most of its incident time. **Containment, Eradication and Recovery** (orange, "stop harm, remove attacker, restore") is the action phase; its three activities are grouped in one box because they overlap in practice. **Post-Incident Activity** (green, "lessons learned, evidence retention") closes the incident.

**Relationships and arrows.** The solid arrows show the forward flow: preparation enables detection, analysis leads to containment, recovery leads to review. Two dashed arrows show feedback. The dashed **new indicators, rescope** arrow runs from Containment, Eradication and Recovery back to Detection and Analysis. The dashed **improvements** arrow runs from Post-Incident Activity back to Preparation, closing the loop.

**Execution flow.** An incident enters through Detection and Analysis (Preparation is done in advance), moves into containment once it is scoped well enough to act, may bounce between analysis and containment several times, and ends with the post-incident review. Only then do the improvements reach Preparation, where they will shape how the next incident is detected.

**Decision logic.** The key decision is when to leave Detection and Analysis. Contain too early and you may alert the attacker while other footholds remain unknown; contain too late and harm continues. The dashed rescope arrow exists because that decision is always made with incomplete information.

**Technical interpretation.** Each arrow carries a concrete artefact. Preparation hands over telemetry, rules and playbooks. Analysis hands over a scoped list of hosts, accounts and indicators. Containment hands back any new indicators found while acting. Recovery hands over a timeline and a list of what failed or helped.

**Practical example.** In the Banyan case, analysis scopes WS-104 and gopal's VPN session. While WS-104 is being isolated, a responder might find a second beacon domain in memory; that is the rescope arrow, and the SOC re-runs its hunts for the new domain across all hosts before eradication continues. The post-incident review then notes that the beacon alert had the lowest priority in the queue, which becomes an improvement to detection tuning.

**Security implications.** Skipping the dashed arrows is how incidents recur. Without rescoping, the attacker keeps a foothold the team never saw. Without the improvements arrow, the same phishing domain pattern and the same detection gap succeed again.

**Key learning.** The lifecycle is a loop, not a line: analysis and containment alternate until the scope stops growing, and every incident must feed back into preparation.

## The SOC analyst in each phase

The lifecycle describes what the organisation does. The SOC analyst's job changes from phase to phase, and knowing which job you are doing at any moment is half of working well in an incident.

### Preparation: building what you will rely on

Preparation is everything that must exist before the first alert fires: an IR policy that says who may declare an incident and who may authorise containment; playbooks for common incident types; contact lists and on-call rotas; logging, retention and clock synchronisation; detection rules; case-management and evidence storage; and exercises that test all of it (Lesson 7 covers tabletops).

The SOC analyst is a major contributor to preparation, even though it feels like "normal work". Every log source onboarded in Module 2, every parser in Module 3, every rule tuned in Module 7 and every enrichment table used by `triage.py` is preparation for some future incident. In Banyan, four kinds of preparation decide how the incident goes:

- **Telemetry coverage.** Windows Security, Sysmon-style endpoint, proxy and VPN events are all centralised. That is why the clearing of the Security log at 08:17Z (event ID 1102) does not erase the story: the earlier events had already left the host.
- **Detections.** Rules R1 to R6 exist and fire. Without R1 (Office spawning a script interpreter), the first sign might have been the upload.
- **Context.** The asset table knows that WS-104 is a finance workstation and that divya is a payments approver; that context lifts the priority of every WS-104 alert.
- **Intelligence.** The ISAC feed already lists `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` with confidence 85 under TLP:AMBER, so the beacon can be recognised as known command and control rather than an unexplained curiosity.

Analysts also contribute by reporting gaps. If you notice during an ordinary shift that a log source has gone quiet, or that a playbook names a contact who has left, that report is preparation work.

### Detection and Analysis: the SOC's main phase

This is the phase in which the SOC does most of its incident work, and it begins before the incident exists. Detection is the alert queue; analysis is triage, validation and enrichment (Module 5) followed, once a case looks serious, by **scoping**: finding everything involved.

Rev. 2 makes a useful distinction between **precursors** and **indicators**. A precursor warns that something may happen: an advisory that payroll-themed phishing is targeting microfinance companies, or a scan of the VPN portal. An indicator shows that something may already be happening: Word starting PowerShell on WS-104. SOCs mostly work from indicators, but precursors are worth acting on when they are specific, for instance by adding a watch for look-alike payroll domains.

In the analysis phase the analyst's responsibilities are:

1. **Validate and correlate.** Confirm that the alerts reflect real activity and group them into a case. In Banyan, six alerts on WS-104 and one on gopal's VPN session become one case with a linked hypothesis.
2. **Build the timeline.** Order the facts in UTC with sources. The timeline is the backbone of every later decision and of any regulatory report.
3. **Scope.** Ask "where else?" for every indicator: other hosts contacting the domains, other accounts targeted by the same address, other machines with the same parent-child process pair.
4. **Escalate and support declaration.** Hand the case to the IR lead with the time noticed (Module 5, Lesson 6). The IR lead, or whichever role the policy names, declares the incident.
5. **Keep analysing after declaration.** The SOC remains the team that can query everything quickly; during the incident it answers the IR lead's questions and watches for new activity.

The Banyan timeline shows why this phase deserves care. The first malicious event (the `invoice.docm` download) is at 06:12Z, but in the illustrative SOC times the incident was noticed at 08:25Z. By then the upload of 48,200,000 bytes (08:12Z) and the log clearing (08:17Z) had already happened. The gap between the first malicious activity and noticing, more than two hours here, is the attacker's free time. Reducing it is the purpose of most SOC improvement work.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Declaration is a decision with consequences: it may start a CERT-In six-hour reporting clock (Module 5, Lesson 6) and it commits people and money. The SOC analyst provides the facts and the time noticed; the role named in the IR policy makes the declaration.

### Containment, Eradication and Recovery: advising and verifying

In most organisations the SOC does not itself isolate hosts, disable accounts or rebuild machines. IT operations, identity and network teams execute those actions on the IR lead's authority. The analyst's job in this phase is to **advise** and to **verify**:

- **Advise** on what to contain, in what order, and what each action will cost in evidence and business disruption. Lesson 3 is devoted to this. For Banyan the advice covers isolating WS-104, blocking `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` and `files-share-drop.example`, resetting divya's and gopal's credentials and revoking their sessions, and blocking 203.0.113.66.
- **Preserve evidence before it changes**, where that is safe: exporting and hashing the raw events (Lesson 4) before eradication removes the `svc_update` account or a rebuild wipes the disk.
- **Verify** that each action worked. After isolation, the beacon to `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` should stop; if it continues, isolation failed or there is a second infected host. After the credential reset, there should be no further VPN logons as gopal from 203.0.113.66.
- **Watch for re-entry** during recovery. A rebuilt WS-104 returning to the network is a moment to watch closely for the same domains, the same account name and the same parent-child pattern.

Eradication removes what the attacker left: the `svc_update` account, any malware and persistence, and the weakness used to get in (here, macro-enabled documents from the internet and a guessable VPN password). Recovery restores service, preferably by rebuilding from a known-good image rather than "cleaning" a compromised machine, and hands the user a working device.

### Post-Incident Activity: evidence for the lessons

After recovery, the team reviews what happened and what should change (Lesson 6). The SOC brings two things that nobody else has: the detailed timeline, and the detection view of the incident. Which alerts fired, in what order and with what priority? Which fired late or not at all? In Banyan, the beacon rule R6 had the lowest priority score in the queue (3.6) even though it showed command and control; that is a tuning lesson. The spray was detected only because it was followed by a success; detecting the failures alone might have given earlier warning. Post-incident work also includes retaining the evidence for as long as legal and regulatory duties require.

### Summary of the analyst's roles

| Phase | What the SOC analyst does | Banyan example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation | Onboard telemetry, build and tune detections, maintain enrichment, report gaps, take part in exercises | Central logging that survived the 08:17Z log clearing |
| Detection and Analysis | Triage, correlate, build the timeline, scope, escalate, support declaration | Seven alerts become one case; noticed 08:25Z |
| Containment, Eradication, Recovery | Advise on containment, preserve evidence, verify actions, watch for re-entry | Confirm the beacon stops after WS-104 is isolated |
| Post-Incident Activity | Supply the timeline and detection review, propose tuning and new rules | Raise the priority of R6 beaconing alerts |

## The current framing: SP 800-61 Rev. 3 and CSF 2.0

Rev. 2 treated incident response as a discipline of its own. Rev. 3 starts from the view that responding to incidents is part of managing cybersecurity risk as a whole, so it describes incident response recommendations using the structure of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. CSF 2.0 has six Functions: **Govern** (strategy, policy, roles, oversight), **Identify** (understanding assets and risks, and improving), **Protect** (safeguards), **Detect** (finding and analysing possible attacks), **Respond** (acting on a detected incident) and **Recover** (restoring assets and operations).

Rev. 3 maps the old lifecycle onto these Functions. Govern, Identify and Protect together carry what Rev. 2 called preparation. Detect, Respond and Recover carry the incident response itself. Lessons learned are no longer only a phase at the end: improvements are identified throughout and captured in the Improvement category of the Identify Function.

**Components.** Five rounded boxes are stacked from top to bottom. **Preparation** (slate, "Govern, Identify, Protect") groups the three Functions that make an organisation ready. **Detect** (blue) lists two CSF 2.0 Categories: DE.CM, Continuous Monitoring, and DE.AE, Adverse Event Analysis. **Respond** (orange) lists RS.MA (Incident Management), RS.AN (Incident Analysis), RS.CO (Incident Response Reporting and Communication) and RS.MI (Incident Mitigation). **Recover** (green) lists RC.RP (Incident Recovery Plan Execution) and RC.CO (Incident Recovery Communication). **Improvement** (violet, "ID.IM, inside Identify") is the Category where lessons are captured.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows run downwards: preparation enables detection, detection triggers response, response leads to recovery. Three dashed arrows labelled **lessons learned** run from Detect, Respond and Recover into Improvement, showing that learning can happen at any point, not only after closure. A dashed arrow labelled **improve all functions** returns from Improvement to Preparation.

**Execution flow.** During an incident, work moves down the solid arrows. While it does, each Function feeds observations into Improvement, which in turn changes governance, protection and monitoring for the future.

**Decision logic.** The diagram has no branches; its key idea is that improvement is continuous. A detection gap noticed during Detect is recorded at once rather than waiting for a review meeting weeks later.

**Technical interpretation.** Mapping the old phases: Detection and Analysis spans DE.CM, DE.AE and RS.AN; Containment and Eradication sit mainly in RS.MI; Recovery is RC.RP; incident management and coordination is RS.MA; communication appears twice, as RS.CO during response and RC.CO during recovery, which Lesson 2 builds on.

**Practical example.** In Banyan, R1 firing on WS-104 is DE.CM; triage enrichment is DE.AE; the IR lead's coordination is RS.MA; building the timeline and scoping is RS.AN; isolating WS-104 is RS.MI; telling legal and executives the status is RS.CO; rebuilding WS-104 is RC.RP. Spotting that R6 was under-prioritised, the moment it is noticed, is an input to ID.IM.

**Security implications.** The framing makes incident response a governed, risk-managed capability rather than a team that appears only in a crisis. Gaps in Govern (unclear authority to isolate a payments approver's laptop) show up as delays in Respond.

**Key learning.** Rev. 3 does not replace the work; it relocates it inside CSF 2.0, with preparation in Govern, Identify and Protect, response in Detect, Respond and Recover, and improvement running throughout.

## Using both vocabularies

In practice, most SOC playbooks still say "containment" and "eradication", and that is fine. Use the phase names for operational work and the CSF 2.0 names when mapping your capability to policy, audit or a maturity assessment. Other models exist and use similar steps. A widely taught six-step model names them Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery and Lessons learned; ISO/IEC 27035 is the international standard for information security incident management. The phases are recognisably the same in all of them, and an analyst who understands what each phase must achieve can work under any of them.

## Worked example: walking the Banyan case through the lifecycle

This example uses the lab kit in a temporary copy of `LAB_ASSETS/C08/` (never run it inside the kit folder itself). Loading the dataset and running the triage helper gives the queue you met in Module 5:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python triage.py
10.8  R5 log_cleared             host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 7.2  R2 vpn_spray_then_success  host=WS-107 (sales laptop) user=gopal (standard) ip=external (documentation range in this lab)
 7.2  R1 office_spawns_script    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 4.5  R4 large_upload            host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 3.6  R6 beacon_regular_small    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=None (?) ip=n/a
```

`minisiem.py load` reads `banyan_events.jsonl` into a local SQLite database; `triage.py` runs the six correlation rules, enriches each hit with asset and identity context and prints a priority score (base severity multiplied by rule confidence and asset criticality). There are no flags to explain; both scripts take no options.

**Detection.** Each line is an alert produced by monitoring that was built during preparation. The queue is the "detection" half of Detection and Analysis.

**Analysis: correlate and declare.** Six lines share WS-104 and divya; the analyst groups them into one case and links the R2 line (gopal, external address) as a related hypothesis. Following Module 5, the case is escalated, the IR lead acknowledges, and the incident is declared. From this point the case is run as an incident.

**Analysis: scope.** Before anyone isolates anything, the IR lead needs to know whether WS-104 is alone. The hunt script answers three scoping questions:

```terminal
$ python hunt.py
H1 beaconing candidates (>= 8 requests, interval CV < 0.2)
  WS-104 https://cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping n=18 mean_gap=300s cv=0.004
H2 parent-child pairs seen on only one host
  WINWORD.EXE -> powershell.exe hosts=1 n=1
H3 other hosts contacting known-bad domains
  none found
```

H1 looks for any regular, frequent connection pattern, not just the known domain, and finds only WS-104's 18 requests with a mean gap of 300 seconds and a coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean) of 0.004, which is almost perfectly regular. H2 confirms that the Word-to-PowerShell pair appears on exactly one host. H3 finds no other host contacting the case domains. Together these support a scoping statement such as: "Within the telemetry available, the endpoint compromise is limited to WS-104; the VPN account gopal is separately affected." Note the qualifier. Scoping is only as good as the telemetry, and the statement must say so.

**Containment, eradication and recovery.** With the scope stated, the analyst recommends a coordinated set of actions at once (isolate WS-104, block both domains and the spraying address, reset and revoke the two accounts), because containing one foothold while another stays open warns the attacker. After the actions, the analyst re-runs the searches: no further beacons, no further VPN logons from 203.0.113.66. Evidence is exported and hashed before `svc_update` is removed and WS-104 is rebuilt.

**Post-incident activity.** The timeline, the queue above and the hunt results go into the review. Two obvious questions for it: why did the beacon rule score lowest, and could the 06:42Z spray failures have raised an alert before the 06:46Z success?

> [!TIP]
> Keep a running note of which phase the incident is in, in the case itself. "Phase: analysis (scoping), containment not yet approved" at the top of the case stops well-meaning colleagues from acting early.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Treating the lifecycle as a straight line.** Teams that "finish" analysis and never return to it miss the second foothold that containment work uncovers.
- **Containing before scoping.** Isolating the first host while a second one keeps beaconing tells the attacker they have been seen. The exception is serious ongoing harm, such as active encryption, where containment cannot wait.
- **Scoping without stating limits.** "No other hosts affected" is not the same as "no other hosts seen in proxy logs". Say what data the conclusion rests on.
- **Thinking preparation is someone else's job.** Telemetry, detections and enrichment are the SOC's contribution to preparation, and their quality decides how fast the next incident is noticed.
- **Wiping before preserving.** Rebuilding is the right recovery, but done first it destroys the evidence that scoping and any legal process need.
- **Skipping the review because the incident was small.** Small incidents are the cheapest source of lessons.
- **Confusing Rev. 2 and Rev. 3.** Rev. 3 supersedes Rev. 2; citing the 2012 lifecycle as "current NIST" is inaccurate, though its phase names remain useful vocabulary.

## Security implications, detection and defence

The lifecycle is itself a security control, and attackers exploit its weak points. They act out of hours, when declaration and containment approval are slowest. They clear logs, which hurts organisations whose preparation did not centralise telemetry. They hold several footholds, so that piecemeal containment leaves them a way back. They return after recovery, because "cleaned" hosts and unchanged credentials are easy to re-enter.

Each of these is countered in a particular phase. Preparation provides 24x7 coverage of hard triggers, central and retained logs and pre-agreed authority to contain. Analysis provides scoping across all telemetry before action. Containment is coordinated and verified. Recovery rebuilds from known-good images, resets credentials and keeps heightened monitoring for the case indicators. The post-incident review turns each gap into a tracked change. For Indian organisations, preparation also includes the obligations of the CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022 that the course has already covered: logs retained for 180 days, clocks synchronised and a designated point of contact able to report within six hours of noticing.

## Best practices

1. Use one lifecycle vocabulary in your playbooks, and record the current phase in every incident case.
2. Treat preparation as continuous SOC work: telemetry, rules, enrichment, contacts and exercises.
3. Scope before you contain, unless ongoing harm forces immediate action; state the data your scope rests on.
4. Recommend containment as a coordinated set, and verify each action by re-running the detecting searches.
5. Preserve evidence before eradication and rebuild; never let preservation delay stopping serious harm.
6. Feed every lesson into Improvement as soon as it is noticed, not only at the final review.
7. Be able to translate between the classic phase names and the CSF 2.0 Functions and Categories that Rev. 3 uses.

## Key takeaways

- The NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 lifecycle has four phases, Preparation; Detection and Analysis; Containment, Eradication and Recovery; and Post-Incident Activity, with feedback from containment to analysis and from review to preparation.
- SP 800-61 Rev. 3 (April 2025) supersedes Rev. 2 and frames incident response within CSF 2.0: preparation in Govern, Identify and Protect, response in Detect, Respond and Recover, and improvement throughout.
- The SOC analyst builds preparation, does most of detection and analysis, advises on and verifies containment, and supplies the timeline and detection review afterwards.
- Scoping comes before containment where possible, and every scoping statement must name the data it rests on.
- In Banyan, more than two hours passed between the first malicious event and noticing; shortening that gap is what good preparation buys.
