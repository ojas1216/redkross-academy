## Introduction and conceptual foundation

An incident is not finished when the attacker is removed and the systems are rebuilt. Until the organisation has understood why the incident happened, why it was not stopped sooner and what will change, the same weaknesses are still in place, waiting for the next operator who finds them. The **post-incident review** (also called a lessons-learned review, retrospective or post-mortem) is the structured process that turns one bad day into lasting improvement.

The idea is old and well established. NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 made "post-incident activity" the final phase of its incident response lifecycle and recommended a lessons-learned meeting after major incidents. Revision 3 (April 2025) places incident response inside the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, where improvement is a standing activity of the Identify function (the Improvement category) informed by incidents, tests and exercises, rather than a phase that happens once at the end. The two views agree on the essential point: an incident that produces no change was paid for and wasted.

A good review produces four things:

1. **A reconstructed timeline** that everyone accepts as the factual record: what the attacker did, what the defenders saw, decided and did, and when.
2. **An analysis of causes**: the root cause that started the incident and the contributing factors that let it go as far as it did.
3. **Actions**: specific changes, each with one owner, a due date and a way to check that it was done and worked.
4. **Metrics**: measured intervals (dwell time, time to notice, time to acknowledge, time to contain) that show how the response performed and allow comparison over time.

All four depend on one cultural condition: the review must be **blameless**. People will only describe their real decisions, including their mistakes, if describing them is safe. A review that hunts for someone to blame learns only what people are willing to admit.

This lesson builds all four outputs for the Banyan incident, CASE-0001, using the synthetic dataset in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/`. Lesson 5 established that the incident was SEV1 of record; here you ask why it reached that point, measure the response with timestamps taken from the data, and write the actions that would make the next attempt fail earlier.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Post-incident review | A structured examination of an incident after it is contained, producing a timeline, causal analysis, actions and metrics. |
| Blameless review | A review that treats errors as products of the system people worked in, and asks why decisions made sense at the time rather than who is at fault. |
| Timeline reconstruction | Building one ordered, sourced record of attacker activity and defender activity, in UTC, from logs, case notes and interviews. |
| Root cause | The earliest point in the chain where a change under the organisation's control would have prevented the incident from starting. |
| Contributing factor | A condition that did not start the incident but allowed it to progress further, last longer or cause more harm. |
| Action item | A specific change with one owner, a due date and a verification method, recorded in a tracker. |
| Dwell time | The time an attacker was active before the organisation noticed: from the first malicious event to the time noticed. |
| Time noticed | The moment the organisation first became aware of the incident; also the start of the CERT-In six-hour reporting clock. |
| Hindsight bias | The tendency, once the outcome is known, to believe the warning signs were obvious at the time. |
| Detection opportunity | An event that an existing rule matched or could have matched, at which the incident could have been noticed earlier. |

## Blameless review: why and how

### Why blame defeats learning

Imagine the Banyan review opening with the question "Why did divya open the document?" The answer is already known, it is uncomfortable, and it leads nowhere useful: the next person will open the next convincing document, because opening invoices is part of a payments approver's job. Worse, every participant now knows what the meeting is for. The Tier 1 analyst who worked the queue in priority order, and therefore reached the R5 alert only at 08:25, will describe the shift in the most defensive terms possible. The detection engineer who rated the beacon rule R6 at severity 2 will explain why that was reasonable rather than examine it. The review ends with a warning to staff and an unchanged system.

The blameless approach, borrowed from aviation safety, healthcare and site reliability engineering, starts from a different assumption: people generally make decisions that seem reasonable given what they know, the tools they have and the pressures on them at the time. If a reasonable person made a decision that turned out badly, the useful question is what about the situation made that decision reasonable. Change the situation and you change the decision for everyone, not just for the person who happened to be there.

### What blameless does not mean

Blameless does not mean that nothing was wrong, that no-one is responsible for improvement, or that deliberate misconduct is ignored. Accountability moves from the past to the future: each action has a named owner who is accountable for delivering it. Deliberate wrongdoing, such as an insider who disabled logging on purpose, is a matter for a separate disciplinary or legal process and should be taken out of the review, not mixed into it.

### Practices that make it work

- **Ask "how" and "what", not "why did you".** "What did the queue look like at 07:00?" and "What information did you have when you chose the next alert?" produce facts. "Why didn't you look at R1?" produces defence.
- **Reconstruct the local view.** For each decision, record what the person could see at the time, not what the timeline shows now. This is the direct antidote to hindsight bias.
- **Name systems, roles and controls, not people, in findings.** "The queue ordered R6 last" rather than "the analyst ignored the beacon".
- **Use a facilitator who was not in the response.** A neutral facilitator keeps the discussion on the system and makes sure quieter participants are heard.
- **Separate the review from performance evaluation.** If the review document is used in appraisals, people will stop being candid in the next one.

> [!TIP]
> A useful test for every finding: would a different competent person, in the same seat with the same information, plausibly have done the same thing? If yes, the finding belongs to the system, and so does the action.

## Running the review

A review is a short project with three stages.

**Before the meeting.** The incident lead appoints a facilitator and a scribe, and schedules the meeting soon after containment, while memories are fresh, typically within days. The SOC prepares a draft timeline from the case record and the logs, and circulates it as a pre-read with a request for corrections. Legal is consulted on how the review is documented and shared, because findings can be sensitive and the incident may be reportable; in some organisations legal asks to direct the review.

**The meeting.** The agenda follows the outputs: confirm the timeline, walk through the decisions, identify what helped and what hindered, agree the causes, and agree actions and owners. Participants include everyone who did meaningful work in the response (the Tier 1 and Tier 2 analysts, the IR lead, IT operations who executed containment, detection engineering), the business owner of the affected function (for Banyan, the finance operations manager) and, where relevant, legal and the data protection function.

**After the meeting.** The facilitator writes the review report, actions go into the tracker with owners and dates, and a follow-up date is set to check that the actions were done and work. The report and the metrics feed the SOC's quarterly reporting (Module 1) and the next tabletop exercise (Lesson 7).

## Root cause and contributing factors

### Two different questions

"Root cause" is a useful term with a misleading implication: that every incident has one cause, and that finding it is the goal. Security incidents almost never work like that. An intrusion succeeds when several defences each fail or are absent at the same moment. The review therefore asks two different questions.

- **What started it?** The root cause is the earliest point in the chain where a change under the organisation's control would have stopped the incident from beginning. It should be expressed as a condition of the system, not as a person's act.
- **What let it go so far?** Contributing factors are the conditions that allowed the incident to progress, last longer or do more harm. Each one is a separate opportunity to have stopped it, and most of the review's actions come from here.

A practical way to reach a root cause is to keep asking "what allowed that?" from the outcome backwards, stopping at the first answer that the organisation can change and that is supported by evidence. For Banyan: the data left because an attacker controlled WS-104; the attacker controlled WS-104 because PowerShell ran from a document; PowerShell ran because a macro-enabled document from the internet was able to start a script interpreter on that host. That last condition is within the organisation's control (Office macro and script-execution policy) and is supported directly by the Sysmon event at 06:14. "Because divya opened it" is not a root cause: it is a person's normal work, and the next person will do the same.

### Evidence discipline

Every causal statement in the review must cite evidence, and the review must separate what the data shows from what it suggests. The Banyan data shows that event 4720 and 4732 were recorded under divya's session, which means that session could create a local administrator; it does not show *why* the session had that right (divya may be a local administrator, or the attacker may have escalated). The review records the fact, lists the question, and assigns someone to answer it from the endpoint configuration. Writing "users have local admin rights" as a finding would be inventing a cause.

## Actions with owners

An action is useful only if it is specific, has one owner (a role and a named person in the real report), has a due date, and states how completion and effectiveness will be checked. "Improve awareness" fails every test. The Banyan actions, from a review held on 10 November 2026 (fictional):

| # | Action | Addresses | Owner | Due | Verified by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | Block macros in documents from the internet and stop Office applications starting script interpreters on finance hosts | Root cause | Endpoint engineering lead | 2026-11-24 | Test document in the lab fails to start PowerShell; R1 hit count on finance hosts |

| A5 | Alert on, or require approval for, large uploads to domains first seen in the last 30 days | Upload allowed | Network and proxy team | 2026-12-15 | Replay: 08:12 upload produces an alert or a block |
| A6 | Add look-alike domain monitoring for Banyan's names and block newly registered look-alikes at the proxy | Look-alike reachable | Threat intelligence analyst | 2026-12-15 | Monthly look-alike report |
| A7 | Add timestamped fields for containment approval and execution to the case template | Metrics gap (below) | SOC manager | 2026-11-17 | Next SEV1/SEV2 case has both fields |

Notice that the actions include changes to the SOC itself (A3, A4, A7), not only to other teams' controls. A review that only asks other teams to improve has not been honest about detection and response. Notice also what is absent: there is no action "retrain divya". Phishing awareness may still be part of a broader programme, but it is not a fix for a control gap that any recipient could fall through.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Track actions to completion. The follow-up date matters more than the meeting: an action list that is not reviewed at 30 and 90 days is mostly forgotten, and the next review of a similar incident will find the same factors.

## Metrics from the dataset

Metrics turn the timeline into numbers that can be compared across incidents. Module 1 defined MTTD, MTTA and MTTR across many cases; a review computes the same intervals for one incident, from real timestamps. A short script in your working copy of the kit reads the minisiem database (it is a helper you write, not part of the kit):

```python
"""Post-incident metrics for CASE-0001 from the minisiem database."""
import sqlite3
from datetime import datetime

con = sqlite3.connect("minisiem.db")
q = lambda sql: con.execute(sql).fetchone()[0]
t = lambda s: datetime.fromisoformat(s.replace("Z", "+00:00"))

first_bad = q("SELECT MIN(ts) FROM events WHERE url LIKE '%banyan-payroll-update%'")
first_exec = q("SELECT MIN(ts) FROM events WHERE source='sysmon' AND lower(parent_image) LIKE '%winword%'")
last_bad = q("SELECT MAX(ts) FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND event_id=1102")
noticed = "2026-11-03T08:25:00Z"   # illustrative SOC time (Module 5 Lesson 6)

def gap(a, b):
    m = int((t(b) - t(a)).total_seconds() // 60)
    return f"{m} min ({m // 60} h {m % 60:02d} min)"

print("first malicious event ", first_bad)
print("first rule-matching   ", first_exec, "(R1)")
print("last attacker action  ", last_bad, "(R5)")
print("time noticed          ", noticed, "(illustrative)")
print("attacker active span  ", gap(first_bad, last_bad))
print("dwell time            ", gap(first_bad, noticed))
print("first alert to notice ", gap(first_exec, noticed))
```

```terminal

first malicious event  2026-11-03T06:12:00Z
first rule-matching    2026-11-03T06:14:00Z (R1)
last attacker action   2026-11-03T08:17:00Z (R5)
time noticed           2026-11-03T08:25:00Z (illustrative)
attacker active span   125 min (2 h 05 min)
dwell time             133 min (2 h 13 min)
first alert to notice  131 min (2 h 11 min)
```

The script takes each attacker timestamp from the data rather than typing it in, so the numbers can be re-derived by anyone with the evidence export. `fromisoformat` is given an explicit `+00:00` offset in place of the trailing `Z`, so every subtraction is between timezone-aware UTC times.

Interpret each number carefully.

- **Dwell time, 133 minutes.** From the first malicious event (the download at 06:12:00Z) to the time noticed. By industry standards this is short, but it was long enough: the attacker's active span was 125 minutes, so dwell time exceeded it. Every action, including the upload and the log clearing, happened before the SOC noticed.
- **First alert to notice, 131 minutes.** The first event that a kit rule matches came two minutes after the download. If R1 runs in near-real time, the SOC had a detection opportunity for 131 minutes. This number, not dwell time, is the one the SOC controls directly, and it is what A3 targets.
- **Notice to acknowledgement, 6 minutes** (08:25Z to 08:31Z, both illustrative). Good, and evidence that the paging process works; it does not offset the 131 minutes.
- **Time to contain: not computable.** The case record has no timestamp for containment approval or execution. Module 1 warned that a metric can only be computed from timestamps that are captured; this review discovered that the most important response metric for a SEV1 cannot be measured. A7 fixes it.
- **Time to report.** The six-hour CERT-In clock ran from 08:25Z to 14:25Z (19:55 IST). The review should record when the report was actually sent, from the point of contact's record, and compare.

> [!WARNING]
> Never compute dwell time from the first *alert* instead of the first *malicious event*: it hides exactly the period a review exists to examine. And never quote a dwell time without stating which two timestamps it runs between.

## The review report

The written report is short and structured, so that leadership can read the summary and engineers can find their actions:

1. Summary: what happened, severity of record, impact, in five lines.
2. Timeline: the merged table and figure, with sources.
3. What went well: for Banyan, the R5 hard trigger was escalated within minutes, the evidence was exported with a hash and custody record, and the escalation package separated fact from hypothesis.
4. Root cause and contributing factors, each with its evidence.
5. Actions: the tracker extract with owners and dates.
6. Metrics: with definitions and the timestamps used.
7. Open questions: what the data cannot answer (the content of the upload, why the beacon stopped at 07:42, whether CASE-0002 is linked).

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"The root cause was user error."** It names a person, not a condition, and produces no change in the system. Ask what allowed the action to cause harm.

**"One root cause, one action."** The Banyan incident passed six gaps; fixing only the macro policy leaves the detection and egress gaps for the next technique.

**"The timeline is obvious, skip it."** Without a sourced timeline, metrics are guesses and arguments about decisions have no common ground.

**"Hindsight shows the beacon was obvious."** It is obvious on the reconstructed timeline. In the live queue it was a priority 3.6 alert below six others. Reconstruct what was visible at the time.

**"Actions are done when the ticket is closed."** An action is done when its verification passes, for example when replaying the dataset produces the expected alert.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Post-incident reviews are among the most valuable sources of detection engineering work, because they come with confirmed attacker behaviour and real data to test against. Every Banyan detection action can be verified by replaying `banyan_events.jsonl` through the changed logic (the testing practice of Module 7). Reviews also contain sensitive material: attacker infrastructure, control weaknesses, personal data and the organisation's response times. Store the report with restricted access, share indicators externally only under the appropriate TLP marking, and keep it consistent with the regulatory reports already sent. Under the CERT-In Directions and the DPDP Act, regulators may ask further questions; a review whose timeline and metrics match the reports is far easier to defend than one that contradicts them.

## Best practices

- Hold the review soon after containment, with a neutral facilitator and a pre-read draft timeline.
- Keep the timeline in UTC with sources for every row, and keep attacker and defender lanes on one clock.
- State root cause and contributing factors as conditions supported by evidence; list open questions separately.
- Give every action one owner, a due date and a verification method, and include actions for the SOC itself.
- Compute metrics from captured timestamps with a reproducible query, state their definitions, and treat a missing timestamp as a finding.
- Follow up at 30 and 90 days, and feed the incident into the next tabletop exercise.
