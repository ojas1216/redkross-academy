## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Module 5 taught you to rate an *alert*: how much harm the detected behaviour would cause if the alert is true, how a rule's base severity becomes an alert severity, and how linked alerts produce a case severity. That lesson ended at a doorway. Once a case is confirmed as an incident, the question changes from "how bad is this behaviour?" to "how bad is this incident for the organisation, and how hard should we respond?". This lesson walks through that doorway.

An incident severity is not a bigger alert severity. It is a different kind of statement, made by a different person, for a different audience. The alert severity helps an analyst choose what to look at next. The incident severity decides who is woken up, whether an incident commander is appointed, whether executives and legal are briefed, how often status updates go out, how much disruption the organisation will accept to stop the attacker, and how the incident will be counted in next quarter's metrics. Because so much hangs on it, it has to be produced by a method that two incident leads on different nights would apply the same way. That method is the **severity matrix**.

A severity matrix combines three questions:

1. **Impact**: what has the incident done, or what is it reasonably shown to be doing, to the organisation's services, information and ability to recover?
2. **Scope**: how much of the organisation is involved: one host or account, a team, or a critical service and many systems?
3. **Urgency**: how much time is there before further harm happens, and is the attacker still active?

Module 5 Lesson 1 introduced the three impact questions (functional, information, recoverability) as a checklist. Here you turn the checklist into a defined scale, combine it with scope in a matrix, add urgency and floor rules, and apply the method to the Banyan incident, at the moment it was noticed and at each earlier stage.

> [!NOTE]
> The Banyan severity matrix in this lesson is a teaching design for the course's fictional SOC, not a published standard. Real organisations write their own, and the structure shown here (impact levels, scope bands, urgency, floor rules) is what you should look for when you read one. All data comes from the synthetic `banyan_events.jsonl` in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/`. The time the SOC noticed the incident (08:25Z) is the illustrative time used in Module 5 Lesson 6; the dataset itself contains events, not SOC activity.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Incident severity | The organisation's rating of how serious a confirmed incident is, which sets the scale and tempo of the response. Owned by the incident lead. |
| Severity matrix | A documented table that derives a base severity from an impact level and a scope band, so that the same facts always give the same rating. |
| Functional impact | The effect on the organisation's ability to deliver its services and business functions. |
| Information impact | The effect on the confidentiality, integrity or availability of information, especially personal, financial or proprietary data. |
| Recoverability | The effort, resources and time needed to return to normal operations, and whether full recovery is possible at all. |
| Impact level | One value (1-4 in the Banyan playbook) that summarises the three impact dimensions, taken as the highest of the three. |
| Scope | The breadth of the incident: the number of hosts, accounts, teams or services involved, and whether a critical service is among them. |
| Urgency | The time pressure: whether harm is ongoing or imminent, or the incident is contained and only recovery remains. |
| Floor rule | A rule that sets a minimum severity for a defined situation (for example confirmed attacker access) regardless of the matrix result. |
| Severity of record | The highest severity the incident reached. It is the value used for reporting, metrics and the post-incident review, and does not fall after containment. |
| Operating severity | The severity currently driving staffing and update cadence. It may fall after verified containment, with a recorded reason. |
| Reasonable worst case | The most serious interpretation that the evidence actually supports, used when a fact (such as the content of an upload) is not yet known. |

## Why incidents need their own severity

Consider three situations a Banyan analyst could face in one week. A phishing email reaches thirty mailboxes, and two people click; their browsers are blocked by the proxy and no code runs. A developer's laptop is found running a cryptocurrency miner. A payments approver's workstation shows a macro-launched PowerShell, a new administrator account, a 48.2 MB upload to an unfamiliar site and a cleared Security log. All three are true positives that High alerts could have produced, yet they deserve a mail clean-up, a rebuild, and a full response with legal and executives respectively.

Alert severity cannot make that distinction, for three reasons.

**It describes one behaviour, not the incident.** The phishing wave touches thirty mailboxes but stops at the click; the Banyan workstation touches one host but reaches the attacker's objectives.

**It is set before the investigation.** Incident severity is revised as scoping, forensics and the business reveal which data the host held, whether the account was used elsewhere and whether a process has stopped.

**It answers a different audience.** An executive, a legal adviser or a regulator needs to know what the organisation has lost or might lose, so the scale is defined by consequences for the organisation rather than by detection logic.

The incident lead (sometimes called the incident commander) owns the incident severity. The analyst proposes a rating with reasons, because the analyst knows the evidence best, but the decision belongs to the person accountable for the response. This mirrors the separation of roles in Module 5 Lesson 6, where the analyst flags reportability and the designated point of contact decides it.

## The three impact dimensions in depth

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 (2012), the long-standing *Computer Security Incident Handling Guide*, described three factors for prioritising incidents and gave each a small set of categories. Revision 3 (April 2025) reorganised incident response guidance around the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and does not prescribe these tables, but the categories remain widely used in playbooks because they are short, consequence-based and easy to apply. The Banyan playbook adopts them and maps each to a number from 1 to 4.

### Functional impact

Functional impact asks what the incident has done to the organisation's ability to work. NIST's categories run from **None** (no effect on the ability to provide services) through **Low** (all critical services are still provided to all users, but with lost efficiency) and **Medium** (a critical service cannot be provided to a subset of users) to **High** (some critical services can no longer be provided to any users).

Two points need care. First, the impact of the *response* counts: if the isolated workstation is the one from which payments are approved, that function is degraded until a workaround exists. Record both the attacker's impact and the response's. Second, functional impact is the easiest dimension to observe and the easiest to over-weight; quiet data theft often has none at all.

### Information impact

Information impact asks what has happened to data. NIST's categories are **None** (no information was exfiltrated, changed, deleted or otherwise compromised), **Privacy breach** (sensitive personally identifiable information was accessed or exfiltrated), **Proprietary breach** (unclassified proprietary information, such as protected critical infrastructure information, was accessed or exfiltrated) and **Integrity loss** (sensitive or proprietary information was changed or deleted).

The Banyan playbook turns these into levels by sensitivity and by how strongly the evidence shows that data was affected:

| Level | Banyan definition |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | No evidence that information was accessed, changed or removed. |
| 2 | Information was exposed to the attacker (for example an attacker session on a system holding data) but there is no evidence of removal or change. |
| 3 | Information of limited sensitivity was removed or changed, or removal of sensitive data is possible but weakly evidenced. |
| 4 | Personal, financial or proprietary information was removed or changed, confirmed or strongly indicated by the evidence. |

At Banyan Microfinance, a lender, personal data about borrowers and financial records sit on many systems. A privacy breach also brings in the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, under which personal data breaches must be intimated to the Data Protection Board and to affected individuals; the timing and content are for legal and the data protection function to determine. The analyst's job is to state what the evidence shows about which data may be involved, as precisely as possible.

### Recoverability

Recoverability asks how hard the way back will be. NIST's categories are **Regular** (recovery time is predictable with existing resources), **Supplemented** (predictable with additional resources), **Extended** (unpredictable; additional resources and outside help are needed) and **Not recoverable** (recovery is not possible, for example when sensitive data has been exfiltrated and published; the organisation can only investigate and manage the consequences).

Recoverability is where evidence destruction and attacker persistence show up. A host whose Security log was cleared, which carries a new administrator account and which has been talking to an external server, cannot be "cleaned" with confidence; it must be rebuilt, and the question of what else the attacker touched may need forensic help. Data that has left the organisation can never be recalled. Both push recoverability towards Extended or Not recoverable, even when the number of affected systems is small. The Banyan playbook maps the four categories directly to levels 1 to 4.

### One impact level, not three added together

The Banyan playbook takes the **highest** of the three levels as the incident's impact level. It does not add or average them. The reason is simple: an incident that is catastrophic on one dimension and harmless on the others is still catastrophic. A ransomware attack that encrypts every server but steals nothing (functional 4, information 1, recoverability 3) should not be rated lower than a moderate incident that scores 3 on everything. Averaging would hide the dimension that matters; adding would make scores depend on how many dimensions happen to be touched rather than on how bad the worst one is.

The impact levels are named to keep conversation precise: **Severe (4)**, **Significant (3)**, **Limited (2)** and **Minimal (1)**.

## Scope and urgency

### Scope

Scope measures breadth: how much of the organisation the incident reaches. The Banyan playbook uses three bands.

- **Contained**: one host, or one account, or one host and the account that uses it.
- **Several**: two to ten hosts or accounts, or the systems of a single team or department.
- **Widespread**: a critical business service (core banking, the payments platform, email for everyone, the identity provider), or more than ten hosts or accounts, or several departments.

Scope is not the same as impact. A single compromised domain controller is narrow in host count but, because it controls identity for everyone, it is treated as Widespread; the playbook lists such systems by name so that the decision does not depend on the analyst's memory. Scope is also the dimension most likely to grow during an investigation. The first host is where the SOC noticed the attacker, not necessarily where the attacker started or stopped, and every scoping hunt (for the same indicators, the same technique, the same account) can move the band.

### Urgency

Urgency measures time pressure. It is the dimension that separates "serious, and we have days" from "serious, and every minute counts".

- **Immediate**: harm is happening now or is expected within hours. Examples: an attacker session or beacon is live, data is being moved, files are being encrypted, an attacker holds privileged access that has not been revoked.
- **Pressing**: the attacker may still have access, but no current activity is observed and further harm is expected over days rather than hours.
- **Deferred**: the incident is contained and containment has been verified; what remains is eradication, recovery and review.

In the Banyan playbook, **Immediate** urgency raises the base severity by one level (to SEV1 at most). **Pressing** leaves it unchanged. **Deferred** never lowers the severity of record; it may only lower the *operating* severity, which governs staffing and the frequency of updates, and only with a recorded reason. Lesson 6 explains why the peak value must survive: a post-incident review that sees "SEV3, closed" for an incident that was SEV1 for six hours will draw the wrong conclusions about detection and response.

Urgency is not the reporting deadline. The CERT-In six-hour clock (Directions of 28 April 2022) runs whatever the severity; it belongs on the task list with an owner, not in the rating.

### Floor rules

A matrix built from impact and scope under-rates an attacker who holds access but has done no visible damage yet, such as a VPN session obtained by password spraying. Floor rules fix this by setting a minimum. The Banyan playbook has three:

1. Confirmed attacker execution on a host or confirmed attacker use of valid credentials: **at least SEV2**.
2. Destructive or extortion activity (encryption, wiping, a ransom note): **SEV1**.
3. A possible personal data breach: **at least SEV2**, so that legal and the data protection function are engaged early.

Floor rules are deliberately few. Each one is a judgment the organisation has made in advance, in calm conditions, so that nobody has to argue it at three in the morning.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows the derivation of an incident severity as a small pipeline with defined inputs. It matters because an incident severity that cannot be traced back to its inputs is an opinion, and opinions differ between shifts, between people and under pressure. With the pipeline, a reviewer can ask "which input moved this incident to SEV1?" and get an answer.

**Components.** The top row holds three blue boxes, the facts gathered by investigation: **Functional impact** ("none / low / medium / high"), **Information impact** ("none / privacy / proprietary / integrity") and **Recoverability** ("regular / supplemented / extended / not recoverable"). Their sub-labels are the NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 categories. The second row holds the orange **Impact level** ("highest of the three, 1-4"), the slate **Scope** ("contained / several / widespread"), the red **Urgency** ("immediate / pressing / deferred") and the violet **Floor rules** ("e.g. confirmed attacker access: at least SEV2"). Below them is the violet **Base SEV from the matrix** ("impact x scope"), and at the bottom the dark box **Incident severity of record** ("SEV1-SEV4, owned by the IR lead").

**Relationships and arrows.** The three blue boxes all point into Impact level: they are reduced to one value, and the sub-label says how (the highest, not the sum). Impact level and Scope both point into Base SEV, which is the matrix lookup shown in the next figure. Base SEV points into the severity of record, and two further arrows modify it: Urgency, labelled **adjust by one level**, and Floor rules, labelled **never below**.

**Execution flow.** Investigation answers the three impact questions; the highest answer becomes the impact level; the matrix combines that with scope into a base severity; urgency raises it by one level if harm is immediate; and finally the floor rules are checked, so that the result is never below the minimum they set. The incident lead records the outcome with the inputs that produced it.

**Decision logic.** Each box is a decision with a small set of defined answers, which is what makes the method repeatable. Two asymmetries are built in. Urgency can raise the severity of record but never lower it. Floor rules can only raise the result. Both protect against the most dangerous failure, under-rating, while the "highest of three" reduction protects against an incident being diluted by its harmless dimensions.

**Technical interpretation.** The pipeline separates *what has happened* (impact and scope) from *how fast we must move* (urgency) and from *policy decided in advance* (floor rules, hence violet). When a fact changes, only its box is revisited.

**Practical example.** For the WS-104 case at 08:25Z, investigation answers functional Low, information 4 (a 48,200,000-byte upload from a payments approver's machine to an unfamiliar domain) and recoverability Extended (cleared log, new administrator account). The impact level is therefore Severe. Scope is Contained (one host, one user). The matrix gives SEV2; urgency is Immediate because the attacker's administrator account still exists and the host is not isolated, so the severity of record becomes SEV1.

**Security implications.** A traceable derivation resists two pressures: the pressure to downgrade an incident because a high rating is inconvenient, and the pressure to upgrade every incident to the top so that it gets attention. Both destroy the meaning of the scale. Recording the inputs is also what makes the post-incident review of severity decisions possible.

**Key learning.** Incident severity is derived, not felt: the worst of three impacts, combined with scope in a matrix, raised by urgency and protected by floor rules, with every input recorded.

## The matrix

The base severity comes from a single lookup. Rows are impact levels, columns are scope bands, and each cell holds the severity before urgency and floor rules are applied. The figure shows the Banyan matrix with the two Banyan cases placed where their 08:25Z assessments put them.

{{figure C08-M08-L05-F2}}

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The table is the heart of the method: the place where impact and breadth meet. It matters because it makes the trade-off between "how bad" and "how wide" explicit and fixed in advance. Without it, one incident lead would treat a severe single-host incident as the top level and another would reserve the top level for estate-wide events, and the organisation's severity statistics would mean nothing.

**Components.** The first column, **Impact level**, lists the four levels from the top: **Severe (4)** in red, **Significant (3)** in orange, **Limited (2)** in blue and **Minimal (1)** in slate. The other three columns are the scope bands: **Contained (1 host or account)**, **Several (2-10 or one team)** and **Widespread (critical service or many)**. Each cell is a base severity from SEV1 (most serious) to SEV4. Three cells carry markers for the worked example: **SEV2 (CASE-0001 base)** in the Severe row under Contained, **SEV1 (if CASE-0002 merges)** in the Severe row under Several, and **SEV4 (CASE-0002 base)** in the Limited row under Contained. The note under the table restates the adjustments: Immediate urgency raises one level, floor rules set a minimum, and a contained incident keeps its peak severity of record.

**Relationships.** There are no arrows; the relationships are positional. Moving down a column lowers severity as impact falls; moving right along a row raises it as scope widens. The diagonal is deliberate: a Severe incident on one host (SEV2) equals a Significant one across a team.

**Execution flow.** Find the row from the impact level, find the column from the scope band, read the base severity, then apply urgency and floor rules as in the previous figure. For CASE-0001: Severe row, Contained column, SEV2; Immediate urgency raises it to SEV1.

**Decision logic.** The matrix contains no "maybe" cells. Uncertainty is handled before the lookup, by rating each input on the reasonable worst case the evidence supports and recording the confidence. The markers show the most important decision the incident lead will make about the Banyan incident: whether CASE-0002 belongs to the same incident. If it does, scope moves from Contained to Several, and the Severe row gives SEV1 even before urgency is considered.

**Technical interpretation.** No cell in the Minimal row reaches SEV2 and no cell in the Severe row is below SEV2. That bounds the influence of scope: breadth alone cannot make a trivial incident serious (a phishing wave that stopped at the proxy stays low however many mailboxes it reached), and narrowness alone cannot make a severe one minor.

**Practical example.** CASE-0002, the password spray followed by a VPN login as gopal at 06:46:00Z, has no observed information or functional impact in the dataset and a regular recovery, so it sits at Limited and Contained: SEV4. The marker reminds the reader that this is only the base value; the floor rule for confirmed attacker use of valid credentials lifts it to at least SEV2 as soon as gopal confirms the login was not his.

**Security implications.** Publishing the matrix inside the SOC and to stakeholders makes severity predictable, and predictability is what lets people act on a severity without debating it. It also invites criticism in calm times: "a payments approver's machine leaking data should always be SEV1" is an argument for a floor rule, settled once.

**Key learning.** Read the base severity from impact and scope, then adjust; the markers show that the biggest severity decision is often a scoping decision, not an impact one.

## Worked example: the Banyan incident at 08:25Z

At 08:25Z, the illustrative time the SOC noticed the R5 alert, the analyst has the WS-104 timeline from the earlier modules and the separate VPN finding. Every value below is tied to an event in the dataset.

| Input | CASE-0001 (WS-104, divya) | CASE-0002 (VPN, gopal) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Key evidence | 06:12:00Z `invoice.docm` from `banyan-payroll-update.example`; 06:14:00Z Word started hidden encoded PowerShell; 06:17:00Z-07:42:02Z 18 requests of 220 bytes to `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`; 07:02:00Z `svc_update` created, 07:03:00Z added to Administrators; 08:12:00Z upload of 48,200,000 bytes to `files-share-drop.example`; 08:17:00Z Security log cleared | 06:42:00Z-06:42:49Z eight users fail once each from `203.0.113.66`; 06:46:00Z VPN success as gopal from the same address, country `??` |
| Functional | Low (2): one workstation; isolation will interrupt payment approvals until a workaround exists | None (1): no service affected |
| Information | 4: data removal strongly indicated from a payments approver's machine; content unknown | 2: an attacker session existed; the dataset holds no activity from it |
| Recoverability | Extended (3): log cleared, persistence, rebuild and forensic help needed | Regular (1): reset password, revoke session and tokens |
| Impact level | Severe (4) | Limited (2) |
| Scope | Contained (one host, one user) | Contained (one account) |
| Base SEV | SEV2 | SEV4 |
| Urgency | Immediate: administrator account still present, host not isolated, attacker active until at least 08:17Z | Immediate until the session is confirmed ended and revoked |
| Floor rules | Rule 1 (confirmed execution) and rule 3 (possible personal data breach): at least SEV2 | Rule 1 applies once gopal confirms the login was not his |
| Severity of record | **SEV1** | **SEV3**, rising to **SEV2** on confirmation |

In the CASE-0001 column the functional answer is honest (no service has stopped), and the information answer carries the weight on the reasonable worst case. Nobody yet knows what the 48,200,000 bytes contained, but an attacker in control of a payments approver's session, a fresh administrator account, an upload to a never-used domain and a cleared log strongly indicate data theft. The analyst records "content unknown; reasonable worst case; confidence moderate". If forensics later cleared the upload, the information value would be revised, but the severity of record would still truthfully show SEV1.

Recoverability is rated Extended rather than Not recoverable: there is no sign of publication or extortion at 08:25Z, and the host can be rebuilt. A reviewer could argue for the higher level, but it would not change the result, because the impact is already Severe from the information dimension.

The CASE-0002 column shows why floor rules exist. The matrix alone gives SEV4, yet the spray pattern (eight users, one attempt each, seven seconds apart, then one success) strongly suggests an attacker holds gopal's password. Until gopal confirms by phone that the session was not his, the case stands at SEV3 with Immediate urgency; after that, the floor rule lifts it to SEV2.

### The merge decision

The two cases are linked by timing only: the spray began at 06:42:00Z, 28 minutes after the PowerShell on WS-104, and one of the failed accounts was divya's, tried at 06:42:21Z while she was logged on at WS-104. That is suggestive, not proof. Running them as one incident moves scope to Several and the base severity to SEV1; running them as linked incidents keeps CASE-0002 a separate SEV2. Either is defensible if recorded. Ignoring the link is not: containing WS-104 alone would warn an operator who still holds a VPN identity, so containment for both is planned together.

## Severity changes over time

Incident severity is not set once. It rises as evidence arrives and, for the operating severity only, falls after verified containment. The Banyan dataset lets you see what the matrix would have produced at each stage if the SOC had been working each alert as the matching event occurred. This is a retrospective exercise: in the scenario nobody looked until 08:25Z, and Lesson 6 examines why.

| Time (UTC) | New evidence | Assessment | Severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 06:14:00 | R1: Word starts hidden encoded PowerShell on WS-104 | Information 2, recoverability 2 (rebuild), impact Limited, Contained: SEV4; Immediate: SEV3; floor rule 1 (confirmed execution) | SEV2 |
| 06:46:00 | R2: VPN success as gopal after a spray (separate case) | As CASE-0002 above | SEV3, SEV2 on confirmation |
| 07:03:00 | R3: `svc_update` created and added to Administrators | Recoverability 3 (persistence), impact Significant, Contained: SEV3; Immediate: SEV2 | SEV2 |
| 08:12:00 | R4: 48,200,000 bytes uploaded to `files-share-drop.example` | Information 4, impact Severe, Contained: SEV2; Immediate | SEV1 |
| 08:17:00 | R5: Security log cleared | Recoverability already 3; confirms attacker privilege and intent | SEV1 |
| After verified containment | Host isolated, accounts disabled, sessions revoked | Urgency Deferred: operating severity may fall to SEV2; severity of record stays SEV1 | SEV1 of record |

Three lessons follow. The incident was SEV2 from its first alert, so a SOC that took R1 at 06:14 would have run a SEV2 response nearly two hours before the data left. SEV1 arrived with the upload, the first irreversible harm. And staffing can relax after containment, but the record keeps the peak.

## Severity, reportability and communication

Severity and reportability are separate decisions made by different people. Severity is the incident lead's judgment of seriousness; reportability is the designated point of contact's and legal's judgment of whether a legal obligation applies. They are correlated but not the same. A SEV3 incident can be reportable to CERT-In if its type is on the list in the Directions, and a SEV1 outage caused by a non-malicious failure may not be a cyber incident at all. The analyst's contribution to both is the same: accurate facts, the time noticed, and an early flag. Coupling them ("we only consider reporting for SEV1") is a design error that can make an organisation miss a six-hour deadline.

Severity is also a communication device. Each level should carry its consequences in writing (who is paged, whether a bridge opens, the update cadence), and every status update should give the severity, a one-line reason and what would change it, so the rating stays a hypothesis under test.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Record every severity change with the time, the person, the old and new values, the inputs that changed and the reason. This is the incident-level version of the reclassification record from Module 5, and it is the raw material for the post-incident review in Lesson 6.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"Incident severity is the highest alert severity in the case."** The WS-104 case contains no alert above base severity 4 (High), yet the incident is SEV1. Incident severity comes from consequences for the organisation, assessed across the whole case.

**"Count the hosts."** One payments approver's workstation leaking data outranks a dozen kiosks with adware.

**"Wait until we know what was taken."** Forensics may take days. Rating on the reasonable worst case the evidence supports, with the confidence recorded, lets the response start at the right scale; waiting for certainty means responding late.

**"Downgrade after containment."** Lowering the severity of record makes the incident look smaller in every later report. Lower the operating severity if staffing allows, and keep the peak on record.

**"Severity decides reportability."** Reportability depends on the incident type and the law, assessed by the point of contact and legal; it must be considered for every confirmed incident.

**"Add up the scores."** Summing functional, information and recoverability scores rewards incidents that touch many dimensions mildly and dilutes ones that are catastrophic on one. Take the highest.

## Security implications, detection and defence

A severity matrix is a control on the response itself. Its job is to make sure that serious incidents receive serious responses quickly and that the organisation's attention is not exhausted by minor ones. It fails in predictable ways. **Inflation**: if every incident is SEV1, executives stop reading the updates, and the one that matters is lost. **Suppression**: if a high severity brings unwelcome scrutiny, people learn to find reasons to rate lower; the recorded inputs and a review of severity decisions in every post-incident review are the defence. **Stale inputs**: the playbook's list of critical services and the data held on each system must be maintained, or scope and information impact will be rated from memory.

Slow severity decisions help attackers. The Banyan operator moved from execution to persistence in 48 minutes and on to clearing the log in 74; an hour of debate gives away a stage of the intrusion.

## Best practices

- Write the matrix, the level definitions, the urgency rules and the floor rules into the incident response playbook, with examples from your own estate, and review them after every SEV1 and SEV2.
- Name critical services and the systems that hold personal or financial data in the playbook, so scope and information impact are looked up rather than remembered.
- Rate every input on the reasonable worst case the evidence supports, and write the confidence and the open question beside it.
- Keep severity of record and operating severity as separate fields; never overwrite the peak.
- Assess reportability separately for every confirmed incident.

## Key takeaways

- Incident severity is the organisation's rating of a confirmed incident; it is owned by the incident lead and sets the scale and tempo of the response, unlike alert severity, which orders an analyst's queue.
- Impact has three dimensions (functional, information, recoverability, with the NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 categories as a well-known scale); the impact level is the highest of the three, never the sum.
- The matrix combines impact with scope; urgency raises the result by one level when harm is immediate, and floor rules set minimums for situations such as confirmed attacker access.
- Rate unknowns on the reasonable worst case the evidence supports and record the confidence; revise as facts arrive, but keep the peak as the severity of record.
- For Banyan, CASE-0001 is Severe and Contained (SEV2 base) with Immediate urgency, giving SEV1; CASE-0002 is SEV4 by the matrix but at least SEV2 by floor rule once the login is confirmed as not gopal's; merging them would make scope Several.
- Severity and reportability are separate decisions; every confirmed incident needs both, and the time noticed travels with each.
