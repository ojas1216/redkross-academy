## Case 2: the VPN identity lead

In the Harbourline Finance exercise, VPN failures occurred across several accounts, and one account subsequently authenticated from the same address. The case has enough evidence to justify identity review and potential protective work. It does not include client attribution, resource use or a confirmed connection to the FIN-21 workstation case. Your task is to build a defensible identity handoff from the actual sample records. Perform the investigation on paper or in a local worksheet. No remote login, credential testing or access to any service is needed.

The distinction between a suspicious authentication and a proved account compromise matters operationally. If an identity is at risk, the owning team can verify and respond promptly. At the same time, the case should remain careful about which account, service, device and actions were observed. A report that treats a related workstation as the VPN client may cause unnecessary disruption and leave the actual session unaddressed. A report that ignores the authentication because device data is missing can also fail the response. Use a bounded recommendation with a clear next owner.

## Preserve the observed facts

A04 is a source summary of eight distinct accounts with failures from 203.0.113.60 during 08:10 through 08:11 UTC. A05 is a successful authentication for STAFF-382 at 08:14 from the same address. Read these as two records with different granularity. The first summarises distinct accounts and the interval, while the second identifies one successful authentication. The supplied record does not say which individual failed accounts are inside the summary. Do not assume the seven other identities, and do not infer that STAFF-382 necessarily appeared in the failure set. Ask the authorised source owner to verify individual records where the exercise permits them.

The recorded address is an input for correlation and next checks. It does not establish the physical location or ownership of a device. Shared gateways, legitimate egress and diagnostic activity can complicate address interpretation. The records do not assert any of those benign explanations, so treat them as hypotheses to check. A burst across distinct identities followed by a success can support a working risk hypothesis and greater urgency. It is still separate from a conclusion about access to specific applications or the identity of the operating person.

B24 marks the analyst's inability to access provider session activity. The result therefore says unavailable, not clear. B25 is a routine different sign-in from known corporate egress. B26 concerns a different identity and address with one failure and no related success in the supplied window. Do not inflate the volume of the original cluster with these rows. They remain useful as contrast cases when explaining what should or should not qualify for the detective pattern. Keep the scope explicit: original cluster address, the distinct-account count, the time relationship, and the identity on the observed success.

## Test two reasonable hypotheses

The first working hypothesis is that the pattern reflects unauthorised account activity. Evidence in its favour includes the concentration of failures across identities and the later success from the same source. Its caveats include source ownership, authentication method and the missing session and client data. The case can justify prompt verification and a protective review. To strengthen it, ask the owning team for the authentication details, known egress, session or client metadata and any unexpected activity after login. An independently verified user or administrator statement can help check whether the success was expected.

The second hypothesis is that an approved shared source or unusual operational event explains the pattern. Ask whether the address maps to a sanctioned gateway or maintenance activity during this period. A general statement that shared egress exists does not explain the exact event. Check timing, the source's approved use and evidence of why multiple identities failed. The fact that an unrelated routine sign-in appears in B25 demonstrates that benign events exist in the queue. It should not automatically confirm the explanation for A04 or A05.

Make your hypotheses disconfirmable. If an authorised record identifies the same address as a typical corporate exit but the success involved an unknown client and a user's denial through a trusted channel, the legitimate egress evidence does not clear the session. Conversely, more detailed authorised records may show a scheduled maintenance test with repeated failures and a verified approved login. That would narrow the risk. Both outcomes require specific evidence from the systems and owners involved. Keep tentative theories in the case so the next shift knows which questions already have useful context.

## Decide what to do without device attribution

Separate the identity decision from an endpoint action. The identity owner can verify whether the sign-in is legitimate, determine whether sessions should be reviewed or invalidated under policy and check for unexpected account changes. These decisions do not require treating FIN-21 or any other device as the VPN client. The endpoint owner already has a distinct case with strong evidence about a workstation; that investigation can continue independently. If a future source supplies an actual shared identifier, the incident lead can review correlation explicitly.

Write the request in terms of an authorised action and its verification. In the exercise, tell the identity and VPN teams the observed address, account, times, distinct-account count and missing data. Ask for an owned decision on the legitimacy of the session and any appropriate protective step. If the team acts, request a record of when and in which service it took effect. A password-related decision and a session decision can have different results. Keep the scope and any remaining access paths distinct. Do not indicate that the responder performed a change when only a request was drafted.

If employee contact would help, use an independently known route. The analyst can ask whether the user expected a login around the stated time and which work service they were using, without requesting authentication secrets. If the user denies the activity, record the statement, route and confidence and provide it to the identity owner. This is a useful observation but still not a system record of session use. If the user confirms it and other evidence still looks concerning, retain the observed facts and seek corroboration. A stable case can support several sources in tension without discarding any one of them prematurely.

## Avoid scope inflation in the handoff

Compare three claims. ?Eight distinct accounts experienced failures from a recorded VPN source? is reported by A04. ?One account authenticated subsequently from the same address? is reported by A05. ?That account carried out the FIN-21 activity? is unsupported in the supplied records. The first two can be used to prioritise the identity question. The last would require matching evidence that actually connects the two threads. Do not replace the missing link with an inventory association or a broad time window.

Distinguish confirmed structural scope from inferred impact. A count of failed account identifiers does not say those accounts were compromised. A success does not establish which actions followed, and a user account name does not independently identify who operated the session. If your report says customers or particular services were affected, identify the evidence for that conclusion. In this case no such evidence is supplied. The business impact can be described as potential access risk that merits verification by the owning team.

Use a controlled vocabulary for source gaps. ?Not collected? means the environment lacks that source. ?Unavailable to this reviewer? means a source may exist but cannot currently be examined. ?No match in checked records? requires a completed source search and documented boundary. B24 supports the second statement, not the last. These states tell the next analyst whether to request access, collect a different source or review an actual result. They also keep the record honest when an important case contact is unavailable at shift change.

## Compare counterfactual records

Imagine an authorised provider export later records the same session reading a particular internal service. Preserve it as a new source observation tied to the session identifier and period. It could support a revised impact assessment for that service while still leaving the contents and actions unverified. Ask the appropriate service owner what the new record actually describes. Do not claim the same access occurred in other services merely because the account was recognised by all of them.

In a different variation, the VPN owner provides a documented test plan explaining the failure burst and verifies that the later success was part of an authorised check. Ask whether the source, account and window match exactly, and whether provider activity introduces anything inconsistent. If the documentation accounts for the observed pattern, downgrade or close the specific lead according to the approved review criteria. Retain the original alert and why its initial escalation was reasonable before the evidence arrived. Avoid global suppression based on one verified event.

In a third variation, one sensitive identity action is acknowledged but no provider confirms effective session state. The lead still needs an evidence request. The request can be tracked as pending implementation or verification separate from the assessment of the original authentication. Use this variation to practise explaining why a case may be urgent even when the currently visible response is incomplete. The analyst does not have to invent confidence in order to justify further work.

## Practical handoff and acceptance

Prepare a five-line identity summary with the VPN evidence, current assessment, missing records, proposed owner and next verification. Give timestamps in UTC and distinguish the source summary from its underlying details. Identify one observation that can support urgency and one claim you explicitly cannot attribute. Add a separate status for B25 and B26. An incoming lead should be able to explain why those rows remain outside the active identity case unless new evidence links them.

A peer should be able to repeat the reasoning on the supplied records. Ask them to locate the latest action status and the evidence for its limits. Ask them what record would link the two active threads. Ask how the priority changes if an approved provider explains the bursts but not the success. These questions test whether the handoff supports a real decision rather than copying a pattern label. Close the worksheet by writing the condition under which the next shift should update confidence and the owner who can make that assessment.

## Draw an evidence boundary for source requests

An authorised session request should name the observed success and provide a time range with a reason. A request spanning the entire identity history is difficult to review and can expose unrelated personal data. Start with the relevant VPN event, the known time bounds and the fields needed to answer whether the session was recognised and what service activity is observable. If the source owner needs to broaden the search, record why and how the output will be handled. Keep the case reference so later analysts can find the approved result.

Give a peer two possible replies. One says that a provider search completed and no related resource events appeared in its defined scope. The other says that the reviewing account lacked permission to perform the search. Both can leave the case with no observed resource activity, but only the first supports a bounded negative finding. Write distinct next actions. For the completed search, compare its coverage with the services that matter. For the denied lookup, route a request to the authorised owner. The right result depends on provenance as much as wording.

Finally, verify that your case response state is distinct from the analysis of legitimacy. Even if the lead authorises an action to reduce risk, the decision does not establish who operated the account. If a verified result later shows session revocation in one service, retain the scope of that result and ask what other services remain unverified. A current threat assessment and an effective protection record can evolve independently. Giving both their own field prevents a shift summary from implying that any trust question is resolved just because an operation completed.

## Key takeaways

- Preserve source, count, address, time and authentication outcome as observed.
- Treat unavailable session telemetry as a gap rather than a negative result.
- Route identity risk to the proper owners without inventing a device link.
- Record what evidence would confirm, revise or close the particular lead.
