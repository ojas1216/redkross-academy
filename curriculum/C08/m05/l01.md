## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every alert that reaches a SOC queue carries a label such as *Critical*, *High*, *Medium* or *Low*. Module 1 showed what that label sets in motion: the queue position, the service-level timers, who is paged. This lesson is about the label itself. Where does it come from, what exactly does it claim, who is allowed to change it, and how does an analyst decide that the label on a particular alert is wrong?

The working definition used throughout this module is short:

> **Severity is the analyst's statement of how much harm the detected behaviour would cause if the alert is true.**

Three parts of that sentence carry the weight. *Harm* means consequence for the organisation: lost money, exposed data, stopped services, an attacker who now holds more access than before. *The detected behaviour* means the specific thing the rule saw, not the worst thing that could conceivably be happening on that host. *If the alert is true* means severity deliberately sets aside the question of whether the alert is right. That question belongs to **confidence**, and the question of what to work on first belongs to **priority**, which combines severity, confidence and context and is the subject of Lesson 2.

Keeping these apart is not pedantry. When severity absorbs confidence ("this rule is noisy, so call it Low"), a dangerous behaviour detected by an imperfect rule is hidden at the bottom of the queue. When asset context is added to severity and again to priority, it is counted twice. When a vendor's default is never revisited, severity describes the vendor's average customer, not your organisation.

The lesson works from the course lab kit in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/`. Its triage helper, `triage.py`, holds a base severity for each of the six minisiem rules, and its two Sigma rules carry their own `level` field. Comparing the two, and asking why each rule has the value it has, is the practical core of the lesson.

> [!NOTE]
> All data in the lab kit is synthetic. Banyan Microfinance is a fictional company, and every address in the dataset comes from a documentation range or a private lab range.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Severity | Expected harm if the alert is a true positive. A property of the detected behaviour in its context, independent of whether the rule is right. |
| Base severity | The default severity a detection engineer assigns to a rule when writing it. Stored with the rule (for example `RULE_META` in `triage.py` or `level` in a Sigma rule). |
| Alert severity | The severity of one specific alert after triage has applied what the analyst learned. Starts at the base severity and may be raised or lowered with a reason. |
| Case (incident) severity | The severity of a case that groups several alerts, assessed from the combined consequence rather than copied from one alert. |
| Confidence | How likely the rule's alerts are to be true positives, usually from its measured history. Answers "is it real?", not "how bad is it?". |
| Priority | The order in which work is taken from the queue. Combines severity, confidence and context (Lesson 2). |
| Severity scale | The named levels a SOC uses and the written definition of each, with examples. |
| Severity inflation | The drift of rules and alerts towards the top of the scale until the top level no longer means anything. |
| Reclassification | A recorded change of an alert's or case's severity, with the time, the person, the old and new values and the reason. |

## What severity measures

Severity answers a counterfactual question: suppose this alert is exactly what it claims to be, what follows? Answering it well means looking past the event itself to what the event *enables*. A cleared Security log on a workstation destroys nothing that a working SIEM has not already collected, yet it is rated highly in almost every SOC. The reason is what it implies: someone with administrative rights on that host wants to hide what they did, which means there probably *is* something to hide, and the attacker has already reached the privilege level needed to clear the log.

Experienced analysts weigh four dimensions when they judge the consequence of a behaviour.

**Position in the intrusion.** The same organisation is in a very different situation when an attacker is knocking on the door (repeated failed logins), has stepped inside (a successful login with stolen credentials, a macro that starts PowerShell), has entrenched themselves (a new local administrator, a scheduled task, a beacon), or is acting on objectives (a large upload to an unfamiliar site, encrypted files, cleared logs). MITRE ATT&CK tactics give a shared vocabulary for this position. Later positions are usually more severe because more of the attacker's work is already done and less time remains to stop it. This is a heuristic, not a law: a successful initial access on a domain controller outranks reconnaissance-stage noise anywhere.

**Breadth.** One account, one host, a department, or the whole estate. A behaviour that can be repeated cheaply across many systems, such as a password spray against the VPN, has a larger potential blast radius than one that needs hands-on work per host.

**Reversibility.** A blocked login can be forgotten. A changed password can be changed back. Data that has left the network cannot be recalled, and destroyed logs cannot be reconstructed from the host. Irreversible consequences push severity up.

**What it touches.** Regulated personal data, payment authority, production services, security tooling. This dimension is about *what the behaviour does to* such things (an upload of customer records, a change to the payments approval path), not merely *which machine* the alert came from; host criticality is context, discussed under "Where asset context belongs" below.

Severity is a judgment about consequence, not about how unusual the event is (rarity is evidence for confidence), how often it fires, or how much work it will take.

### Severity is not CVSS

Analysts from vulnerability management sometimes reach for the Common Vulnerability Scoring System. CVSS scores the properties of a *vulnerability*: how it can be exploited and what a successful exploitation could affect. An alert describes *behaviour that may already have happened*. An exploitation attempt against a CVSS 9.8 vulnerability on a system that is patched is a failed attempt, often of low consequence; a perfectly ordinary login with a stolen password, which has no CVSS score at all, may be the start of a serious incident.

## Designing the severity scale

A scale is only useful if two analysts on different shifts, looking at the same alert, would choose the same level. That requires three things: a small number of levels, a definition of each written in terms of consequence, and anchoring examples drawn from the SOC's own rules.

**Number of levels.** Four levels (Critical, High, Medium, Low) are the most common choice, and the course lab uses a numeric 1-4 range for rule base severity. Some formats add a fifth, purely informational level: Sigma, the open rule format used for the kit's YAML rules, defines `informational`, `low`, `medium`, `high` and `critical`. Informational detections are useful as context or as contributors to a risk score, but they should not normally create work in the analyst queue. More than five levels tends to produce arguments about the difference between adjacent levels rather than better decisions.

**Definitions in consequences.** "High: serious" is not a definition. "High: an attacker has, or is very likely to have, gained execution or valid credentials inside the environment, or a control that protects evidence or detection has been disabled" is a definition: an analyst can test an alert against it. Each definition should also say what it is *not* ("a blocked attempt with no evidence of success is not High").

**Anchoring examples.** Each level should list several of the SOC's real rules as examples. When a new rule is written, the detection engineer compares it with the anchors instead of reasoning from scratch. When analysts disagree about an alert, the anchors settle the argument.

**Decision logic.** A detection engineer placing a new rule reads down the definitions until one fits the behaviour *if true*, then checks that the rule is comparable with that row's anchors. The analyst uses the same table in reverse during triage: if what they have learned no longer matches the row the alert is in, the alert should be reclassified.

**Technical interpretation.** Why is Critical "case only"? Because a single detection rarely proves active harm on its own. Critical is reserved for a judgment made about a case once evidence shows that harm is happening, for example confirmed exfiltration or ransomware encryption in progress. Keeping it out of base severities prevents inflation and keeps the top of the queue meaningful. The kit itself stores only numbers from 1 to 4; mapping its 4 to High is the convention this module adopts, and the kit's Sigma rule for log clearing, which carries `level: high`, agrees with it. (The other Sigma rule does not agree so neatly, as the worked example shows.)

**Practical example.** A detection engineer writes a new rule for a scheduled task created by an Office process. Reading the definitions, the behaviour is persistence after suspicious execution: Medium. The anchors confirm it sits alongside R1 and R3. It is given value 3, not 4, and the decision is recorded in the rule's change history.

**Security implications.** A scale with vague definitions invites inflation, which buries real High alerts among dozens of mislabelled ones; it also invites deflation of awkward rules to protect SLA figures. Both are visible in data: periodically compare the distribution of base severities with the dispositions alerts actually receive.

**Key learning.** Severity levels must be defined by consequence and anchored with the SOC's own rules; reserving the top level for case-level judgment is what keeps it meaningful.

## Setting a rule's base severity

Base severity is set by the detection engineer when the rule is written, and reviewed whenever the rule changes or its alerts are consistently re-rated by analysts. The reasoning should be written down next to the rule, because the next engineer will otherwise have to guess why a value was chosen. Here is that reasoning for the six minisiem rules, using the dimensions from the previous section.

| Rule | What it detects | Base severity | Reasoning (if true) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R2 `vpn_spray_then_success` | At least five accounts failing from one source address, then a success from the same address | 4 | Valid credentials are in an outsider's hands and they are inside the remote-access boundary (ATT&CK T1110.003 password spraying followed by T1078 valid accounts). |
| R5 `log_cleared` | Windows event 1102, Security log cleared | 4 | An actor with administrative rights is destroying local evidence; on-host history is lost irreversibly. |
| R1 `office_spawns_script` | Word starting PowerShell or cmd.exe | 3 | Probable malicious-document execution (T1204.002, T1059.001); code is running, but scope and success are still unknown. |
| R3 `new_local_admin` | Events 4720 (account created) or 4732 (member added to a local group) | 3 | Possible persistence or privilege change; also routine for administrators. |
| R4 `large_upload` | Proxy upload above 10,000,000 bytes | 3 | Possible exfiltration, which is irreversible; but large uploads have many benign causes. |
| R6 `beacon_regular_small` | Ten or more requests to one URL with almost constant size | 2 | Possible command-and-control rhythm; alone it shows no harm, only a pattern. |

Two points in this table repay careful thought.

First, look at R2. The rule does not fire on the spray alone; it requires a success afterwards. That condition is what lifts it to 4. A pure spray detection, with no success, would describe an attempt against the boundary and would sit lower. Detection engineers often write both rules: a low-severity attempt rule that feeds context and a high-severity success rule that creates work. The severity follows the consequence, and the consequence changes at the moment a login succeeds.

Second, notice the rules where severity and confidence pull in different directions. R4 (`large_upload`) has a base severity of 3 but a confidence of only 0.5 in `triage.py`, because large uploads are common and mostly legitimate. A tempting shortcut is to rate it 2 "because it is usually nothing". That would be the confusion described in the introduction. If a large upload *is* exfiltration, it is serious and irreversible, so the severity stays at 3; the doubt is expressed in the confidence value, where it belongs, and Lesson 2 shows how the two combine.

> [!TIP]
> A quick test for a proposed base severity: finish the sentence "If every alert from this rule were true, we would..." If the honest ending is "open an incident and call the on-call responder", the rule is not Low, however noisy it is. Fix the noise with tuning and confidence, not by lowering severity.

## Three severities, not one

Severity is often treated as a single field that is set once. In practice a mature SOC distinguishes three values, each owned by a different person at a different moment:

1. **Base severity** belongs to the rule. It is set by detection engineering and changes only through rule review.
2. **Alert severity** belongs to one alert. It starts equal to the base severity and is adjusted by the analyst during triage when the facts of this particular alert differ from the typical case the rule was written for.
3. **Case severity** belongs to the case. It is assessed when several alerts are linked, and reflects the combined consequence of everything the case contains.

**Components.** At the top is **Rule base severity** (slate), with the detail "set by detection engineering". Below it is **Alert created** (blue), "starts at base severity". The violet decision box **Triage: do the facts match?** carries the detail "compare with the rule's typical case" and has three outgoing branches. One leads to **Raise** (red), "corroborated, succeeded, later stage, wider scope"; one to **Keep** (blue), "typical case, no new facts"; and one to **Lower** (green), "approved change, blocked, test system". All three lead into **Recorded reclassification** (orange), "who, when, old, new, reason". From there an arrow goes to **Linked into a case** (violet), and finally to **Case severity** (dark), "combined consequence, may be Critical".

**Relationships and arrows.** The arrows run forward in time: a rule's base value seeds an alert, an alert's reviewed value feeds a case. The three branch arrows from the decision are labelled **raise**, **keep** and **lower**, and every one of them passes through the recorded-reclassification box, including "keep". Keeping the base value is also a decision and is noted in the triage record.

**Execution flow.** The rule fires, the alert inherits the base severity, the analyst triages and records the outcome, the alert is grouped with related alerts, and the case receives its own severity.

**Decision logic.** Facts that raise an alert's severity include corroborating alerts on the same entity, evidence that the behaviour succeeded rather than merely being attempted, signs that the intrusion has progressed to a later stage, and a scope wider than the rule assumed (several hosts or accounts). Facts that lower it include an approved change or maintenance window that explains the behaviour, evidence that the attempt was blocked, or proof that the target was a test system with no real data. The note under the diagram rules out one tempting branch: lowering severity because the queue is long, or because a deadline is close, is not a legitimate reason.

**Technical interpretation.** Case severity is not simply the maximum of the alerts' severities. A case made of several Medium alerts can be Critical when together they show a complete chain, and a case containing one High alert can be Low once it is shown to be an approved activity.

**Practical example.** In the Banyan dataset, WS-104 produces R1 (3) at 06:14, two R3 alerts (3) at 07:02 and 07:03, R6 (2), R4 (3) at 08:12 and R5 (4) at 08:17. Individually none is above High. Linked, they describe a document executing PowerShell, a new account `svc_update` created and added to a local group, a regular beacon, a 48.2 MB upload to an unfamiliar site and the Security log being cleared. That sequence describes probable data theft followed by evidence destruction on a payments approver's workstation, and the case is rated Critical.

**Security implications.** The reclassification record is what makes severity trustworthy. It lets a reviewer see why a Medium became Critical and catches the opposite pattern, a High lowered without a reason, which is how real incidents are quietly closed.

**Key learning.** Rule, alert and case each have their own severity; every change between them needs a recorded reason, and a case's severity is judged from the whole chain.

## Where asset context belongs

One design choice deserves explicit attention, because SOCs make it differently and confusion follows when they make it twice. Should a High rule on a finance workstation and the same rule on a test kiosk carry different *severities*, or the same severity and different *priorities*?

There are two coherent answers.

- **Context in priority (the model used in the course lab).** Severity describes the behaviour; asset criticality, user role and other context are applied in a separate priority calculation. `triage.py` follows this model: `RULE_META` holds severity and confidence per rule, `ASSETS` holds criticality per host, and the three are multiplied in `priority()`. The advantage is clarity: severity means the same thing on every host, and its statistics are comparable across the estate.
- **Context in severity.** Some SOCs fold asset context into the alert severity itself, so the same rule produces High on a domain controller and Medium on a kiosk. This suits organisations whose SLA contract is written per severity and must reflect business impact directly.

Either can work. What fails is a hybrid: the analyst raises severity *because* the host is critical, then the formula multiplies by criticality again, counting the host twice. Whichever model the SOC chooses must be written into the severity definitions and the triage playbook.

## From alert severity to incident severity

Once a case is confirmed as an incident, its severity drives notification, the size of the response and, in India, the question of whether a CERT-In reporting obligation applies (the timing rules were covered with SLAs in Module 1). At this point the question widens from "how bad is this behaviour?" to "how bad is this incident for the organisation?".

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2, the long-standing incident handling guide, suggested weighing three kinds of impact: **functional impact** on business operations, **information impact** on the confidentiality, integrity or availability of data, and **recoverability**, meaning the effort and resources needed to recover. Revision 3, published in 2024, reorganised the guidance around the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 rather than repeating that scheme, but the three questions remain a sound checklist for any incident severity assessment:

- **Functional:** is any business service degraded or stopped, and for how many users?
- **Information:** has personal, financial or proprietary data been viewed, changed or taken? For personal data, this is also the point at which the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 becomes relevant to the response.
- **Recoverability:** can the organisation return to normal with its own resources and in a predictable time, or does it need outside help, or is something lost for good?

For the WS-104 case, the functional impact is limited to one workstation, but the information impact is potentially severe (a 48.2 MB upload from a payments approver's machine to an unfamiliar domain), and recoverability is poor because the host's own Security log has been cleared. The information and recoverability answers drive the Critical rating, not the number of hosts.

## Worked example: auditing the kit's severities

Module 1 used `triage.py` to produce a queue. Here the same kit is used for a different job: auditing the severity values themselves, as a detection engineer or senior analyst would during a quarterly rule review. Work in your own copy of the extracted `LAB_ASSETS/C08/` folder.

**Step 1: read the stored values.** `RULE_META` in `triage.py` holds base severity and confidence for each rule. Printing it from the kit folder gives:

```terminal
$ python -c "from triage import RULE_META, ASSETS; print(RULE_META); print(ASSETS)"
{'R1': (3, 0.8), 'R2': (4, 0.9), 'R3': (3, 0.6), 'R4': (3, 0.5), 'R5': (4, 0.9), 'R6': (2, 0.6)}
{'WS-104': ('finance workstation', 3), 'WS-107': ('sales laptop', 2)}
```

`python -c` runs the quoted Python statement; importing from `triage` works because the script's `main()` only runs when it is executed directly. The first line is the per-rule tuple *(base severity, confidence)*; the second is the asset table, which does not enter severity at all in this model. Already the separation of concerns from the previous section is visible in the data structures.

**Step 2: compare with the rule files.** Two of the rules also exist as Sigma rules in the kit. `rk_security_log_cleared.yml` (R5) carries `level: high`, matching the value 4 that this module maps to High. `rk_office_spawns_shell.yml` (R1) also carries `level: high`, but `RULE_META` gives R1 a 3, which this module maps to Medium. The two sources disagree.

This is a realistic finding. In production SOCs severity often lives in several places: the vendor's or community rule, the SIEM's alert configuration, the case-management template and the SOAR playbook. When they disagree, the analyst sees one value in the SIEM and another in the case, and SLA timers may follow whichever system started them. The fix is a single source of truth, usually the rule repository, with the other systems reading from it.

**Step 3: decide which value is right.** Use the definitions in Figure 1. If R1 is true, a Word document has started PowerShell on a user's workstation: code execution has happened, but there is not yet evidence of credential theft, persistence or loss of evidence. That matches the Medium definition. The Sigma author's `high` is a reasonable general-purpose rating (Sigma rules are written for many organisations, and `high` signals "investigate promptly"), but in a scale that reserves High for foothold, credential compromise or evidence loss, 3 fits better. Record the decision and update the Sigma file's `level` to `medium` in your own copy, or record why the difference is intentional. Either outcome is acceptable; leaving it unexplained is not.

**Step 4: check the spread.** Of six rules, two are at 4, three at 3 and one at 2, and none at 1. The kit's rules were chosen to catch stages of the Banyan intrusion, so the concentration is expected. In a real review, count how many alerts each level produced over the last quarter and what fraction were true positives. A level where most alerts close as benign is a sign of severity inflation or a confidence problem being expressed as severity.

**Step 5: re-rate one real alert.** Take the R3 alert at 07:02 on WS-104: user `divya` created the account `svc_update`, and one minute later added it to a local group. Base severity 3. Facts from the same host: R1 fired 48 minutes earlier, and the identity table records `divya` as a payments approver, not an IT administrator, so account creation is outside her normal role. Both facts point to the "raise" branch of Figure 2: the alert is corroborated and the behaviour is out of role. A defensible reclassification note reads: "Raised 3 to 4 (Medium to High): account creation by a non-administrative user, 48 minutes after R1 on the same host; treated as possible persistence. Linked to the WS-104 case." Compare that with the R3 entry in the kit's `SUPPRESS` list, which covers the account `itadmin` under change CHG-1042: there the context explains the behaviour, so the same rule's alerts would be suppressed rather than raised.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Who may change severity should be written down. A common rule: analysts may raise any alert's severity on their own judgment, but lowering a High or Critical alert or case requires a second person (the shift lead) and a recorded reason. Raising is cheap and safe; lowering is where incidents get lost.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Confusing severity with confidence.** "This rule is noisy, so it is Low." Noise belongs in confidence and tuning. A noisy rule for a dangerous behaviour is a High rule that needs work.
- **Copying vendor or community severities unchanged.** Defaults describe an average organisation. Review them against your own definitions when the rule is imported.
- **Counting the asset twice.** Raising severity because the host is critical and then multiplying by criticality in the priority formula.
- **Severity by volume.** A burst of 500 identical alerts is one behaviour seen many times, not a more severe behaviour. Deduplicate and treat the burst as one alert with a count; if the volume itself is meaningful (a spray), write a rule for that pattern.
- **Treating "informational" as a dumping ground.** Rules moved to informational to make the queue quieter often still detect real behaviour. If they no longer create work, make sure they still feed an entity risk score or a hunt.
- **Assuming case severity is the maximum alert severity.** A chain of Medium alerts can be a Critical incident; one High alert can be an approved activity.
- **Silent reclassification.** Changing the field without a note removes the audit trail that quality reviews and post-incident reviews depend on.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Severity decisions are themselves a security control, and attackers benefit from their weaknesses. Activity that produces only Low alerts may never be read. Adversaries who work slowly, use legitimate administration tools and stay below upload thresholds are, in effect, exploiting the SOC's severity scale. Three defences address this.

**Aggregate low-severity signals.** Low and informational detections should contribute to a risk score per user or host, so that several weak signals on one entity surface together. Lesson 2 develops this.

**Monitor the severity system.** Report monthly on the distribution of base severities, the number of reclassifications in each direction, and the true-positive rate per level. An analyst who downgrades High alerts far more often than colleagues is a quality issue to investigate, and rule severity changes should be reviewed like code.

**Protect the evidence of severity.** The reclassification record should be written to the case system's audit log, not just to an editable field, so that it cannot be rewritten after an incident.

## Best practices

- Define each level by consequence, with what it is not, and anchor it with the SOC's own rules.
- Reserve the top level for case-level judgment so that it stays rare and meaningful.
- Keep severity, confidence and context as separate inputs and decide, once, where asset context is applied.
- Write the reasoning for each base severity next to the rule.
- Review the severity distribution and the true-positive rate per level each quarter.

## Key takeaways

- Severity is the expected harm *if the alert is true*; confidence (is it real?) and priority (what first?) are separate questions.
- Judge consequence by intrusion stage, breadth, reversibility and what the behaviour touches.
- A good scale has few levels, consequence-based definitions and anchoring examples from the SOC's own rules.
- Base, alert and case severity are owned by different people at different times; every change needs a recorded reason.
- A case's severity reflects the whole chain: in the Banyan lab, alerts rated 2 to 4 combine into a Critical case.
- Apply asset context once, either in severity or in priority, never in both.
- Severity values living in several tools will drift apart; keep one source of truth and audit it.
