## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every alert you triage ends in a decision: close it, keep working it, or escalate it. Lessons 1 to 6 of this module were about making that decision well. This lesson is about the part that outlives the decision: the **case note**, the written record of what you looked at, what you found, what you concluded and why. In a SOC the note is not paperwork that follows the work. It *is* part of the work, because nobody else can see inside your head, and the alert queue is a team activity spread across shifts, tiers and months.

Think about who reads a case note after you write it:

1. **The next analyst.** Your shift ends with the case still open. If your note says "looked suspicious, checked some stuff, escalating", the Tier 2 analyst who picks it up must redo everything you did.
2. **The incident handler.** If the case becomes an incident, the timeline, containment plan and regulator report are built from analyst notes. A wrong time or an unmarked guess becomes a wrong statement to management or to CERT-In.
3. **The reviewer and the auditor.** Weeks later, a reviewer samples closed alerts to check that closures were justified. They can only judge what is written down.
4. **You, later.** Three weeks after a night shift you will not remember why you closed an alert.

A **defensible** case note survives all four readers. That does not mean long. It means every statement is traced to evidence or clearly marked as interpretation, every time is unambiguous, every evidence item can be found again and shown unchanged, and the decision visibly follows from what was found.

This lesson uses the course's Banyan Microfinance dataset, whose fictional intrusion of 3 November 2026 produced seven rule hits. You will rebuild a weak note about it into a defensible one tied to evidence exported with the kit's `evidence_export.py`. Lesson 8 then asks you to write such notes for the whole triage queue.

> [!NOTE]
> Course module 8 (Incident handling) covers evidence preservation and chain of custody in depth, and module 10 covers hunt documentation. This lesson covers the analyst's case note during triage: the record you write for each alert or small case, before and alongside any incident process.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Case | A unit of investigation in the SOC's case or ticket system. It may hold one alert or several related alerts that share entities and time. |
| Case note | A dated, attributed entry in a case recording what the analyst checked, what was found, what was concluded and what happens next. |
| Case record | The whole case: header, notes, entities, timeline, evidence references, actions and closure. |
| Fact (observation) | A statement directly supported by a cited piece of evidence, such as a log event, a query result or a file hash. |
| Inference | A conclusion drawn from facts by reasoning. It may be well supported, but it can be wrong and must carry a confidence level. |
| Disposition | The outcome label for an alert: true positive, benign true positive, false positive or undetermined. |
| Evidence reference | Enough information to find an item again and prove it is unchanged: item name, location, SHA-256 hash, the query or method that produced it, and who produced it when. |
| UTC | Coordinated Universal Time, the single reference clock for SOC records. India Standard Time (IST) is UTC+05:30. |
| ISO 8601 | The international date-time format, such as `2026-11-03T06:12:00Z`, where the trailing `Z` means UTC. |
| Append-only | A record that is extended but never silently edited. Corrections are new entries that point at the entry they correct. |

## The structure of a defensible case note

Weak notes are rarely the result of laziness: they come from writing for yourself in the moment instead of for a reader who was not there. A fixed structure prevents that by giving every kind of information its own place and its own rules. SOCs use different case tools (TheHive, a SOAR platform, a ticketing system, the case module of a SIEM), and each has its own fields. Underneath, the content of a good case record is remarkably consistent. The structure below follows the case documentation model used in this course's library material and maps onto any of those tools.

**Header.** Case ID, title, current severity and status, owner, and the time the case was opened (and closed) in UTC. The title should name the activity and the main entity, for example "WS-104: Word spawned hidden PowerShell, beaconing to rare domain", not "Suspicious alert".

**Summary.** Three to five sentences that a manager could read alone: what happened, how confident you are, what has been done, what is pending. It is the only section rewritten as the case evolves; everything else grows by appending.

**Entities.** Every host, user, IP address, domain, URL, process and file hash involved, each with its role: victim host, compromised account, attacker infrastructure, delivery domain. Entities are what the next analyst will pivot on.

**Timeline.** One line per significant event: UTC time, source, what happened, why it matters. Only facts go here, each traceable to an event.

**Analysis.** Your reasoning: which hypotheses you considered, what supports and what contradicts each, what you ruled out and how, and your confidence. This is where inferences live, clearly separated from the timeline.

**Actions.** What was done, by whom, approved by whom, and when: containment requests, user contact, blocks, escalations, tickets raised to other teams.

**Evidence.** Each exported or captured item with its location, SHA-256 hash, the query or method that produced it, and the custody entry.

**Disposition and next steps.** The disposition label, the reason for it in one or two sentences, and either the closure statement or the owned next actions.

**Relationships.** The order is not arbitrary. Entities feed the Timeline (every timeline line names an entity), the Timeline feeds the Analysis (every inference should point back to timeline lines), and the Analysis justifies the Disposition. Evidence underpins the Timeline: each fact in it should be reproducible from an evidence item. Actions are separate from Analysis because "what we did" must never be confused with "what we think".

**Execution flow.** You rarely write the sections in order. In triage you usually start with Entities and a few Timeline lines, add Analysis as you validate, record Evidence as soon as you export anything, and write the Summary and Disposition last. The structure is about where things go, not the order of typing.

**Decision logic.** The Main reader column tells you how to write each part. The Summary serves a manager and the next analyst, so it is plain language. The Timeline serves whoever builds the incident report, so it is terse and exact. Evidence serves the auditor, so it must be precise to the character: one wrong hex digit in a hash makes a reference useless.

**Technical interpretation.** In most case platforms these sections map onto structured fields (observables, tasks, custom fields) plus free-text logs. Use the structured fields where they exist: entities typed as observables can be searched across cases automatically, which free text cannot.

**Practical example.** For the Banyan intrusion, the Entities row lists WS-104 (victim host), `divya` (user on the victim host), `banyan-payroll-update.example` (delivery domain), `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` (suspected command and control) and `svc_update` (account created on the host). The Evidence row holds the 32-event export of WS-104 with its SHA-256 hash.

**Security implications.** A record that mixes facts and analysis, or lacks evidence references, can lead to wrong containment, wrong regulator statements and closures that a reviewer cannot defend.

**Key learning.** Give every kind of information its own section: facts in the timeline, reasoning in the analysis, proof in the evidence list, and the decision at the end, justified by the rest.

## Per-alert notes and case records

In triage you write two kinds of entry. A **per-alert note** is short and attached to one alert: what the rule detected, what you checked, what you found, and the disposition. When several alerts share entities and time, you group them into one **case**, and the case record grows from the per-alert notes.

A per-alert note can follow a fixed five-line pattern that fits in a ticket comment:

```text
2026-11-03T08:41Z  analyst.t1  Alert R1 office_spawns_script, WS-104 / divya
CHECKED: proxy and sysmon events WS-104 06:00-06:20Z (query in evidence list, item E1)
FOUND:   06:12:00Z download invoice.docm from banyan-payroll-update.example (proxy);
         06:14:00Z WINWORD.EXE started powershell.exe -NoP -W Hidden -EncodedCommand (sysmon);
         06:17:00Z first request to cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping (proxy)
ASSESS:  High confidence true positive: macro document executed hidden encoded PowerShell,
         followed within 3 min by traffic to a rare domain. Payload content not decoded (not in data).
NEXT:    Grouped into CASE-0001 with R6/R3/R4/R5 on WS-104; escalated to T2 per criteria (Lesson 6).
```

`CHECKED` states the scope you examined (here 06:00-06:20Z and nothing else); `FOUND` lists observations with UTC times and sources; `ASSESS` holds the inference, its confidence and what is *not* known; `NEXT` says what happens now and who owns it. The opening UTC time and analyst identifier make the note attributable.

The facts in that example are real: they come from the lab kit's dataset (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/banyan_events.jsonl`), queried with `minisiem.py`. The note time (08:41Z) and analyst name are illustrative, because the dataset contains events, not analyst activity.

## Facts versus inferences

The most important discipline in a case note is keeping what you **observed** apart from what you **concluded**. Both belong in the note. The problem is never that an analyst draws inferences; triage is impossible without them. The problem is an inference written in the grammar of a fact, so that the next reader cannot tell which is which.

A fact in a SOC note has three properties: it describes something recorded by a system, it names the source, and anyone with access can check it by repeating your query. "At 2026-11-03T06:46:00Z the VPN logged a successful logon for `gopal` from 203.0.113.66" is a fact. Run the query and the event is there.

An inference goes beyond the record. "The password for `gopal` was guessed by the spraying source" is an inference. It is a strong one: the same address failed against all eight accounts at 06:42Z, seven seconds apart, and then succeeded for `gopal` at 06:46Z. But the logs do not record *how* the password was obtained. It might have been phished earlier, reused from another breach, or guessed. The note should say what the evidence supports and how strongly.

Here are the observations around the VPN alert in the Banyan dataset, retrieved with the course SIEM (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py`, run in a working copy of the kit after `python minisiem.py load`):

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, user, outcome, src_ip FROM events WHERE source='vpn' AND src_ip='203.0.113.66' ORDER BY ts"
ts | user | outcome | src_ip
2026-11-03T06:42:00Z | asha | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:07Z | ben | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:14Z | chen | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:21Z | divya | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:28Z | eshan | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:35Z | farah | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:42Z | gopal | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:42:49Z | hema | failure | 203.0.113.66
2026-11-03T06:46:00Z | gopal | success | 203.0.113.66
```

The `search` subcommand runs the quoted SQL against the `events` table and prints a header row and one row per result, with ` | ` between columns. Every line here is a fact. From these facts an analyst can reasonably infer, with high confidence, that 203.0.113.66 ran a password spray (one attempt per account across many accounts in under a minute) and that the `gopal` credential is compromised. Two further claims are tempting and not supported by this output: that the spray is connected to the phishing on WS-104, and that the attacker's VPN session reached any internal host. The note must either leave them out or record them as open hypotheses.

The triage helper adds a trap of its own. `triage.py` prints the VPN alert as `host=WS-107 (sales laptop) user=gopal`. VPN events in this dataset carry no host field, so the helper fills one in from a hard-coded table, `PRIMARY_HOST`, that maps each user to their usual workstation. `WS-107` is therefore an enrichment lookup, not an observation: it is `gopal`'s assigned laptop, and nothing in the VPN event says the session came from it or went to it. An analyst who copies `host=WS-107` into the timeline has turned a lookup into a fact. The correct record is "user `gopal` (primary device WS-107 per asset mapping; VPN events carry no host)".

**Purpose.** The figure sets the two kinds of statement side by side using the Banyan alerts, so that you can see the difference in wording and in what each kind of statement needs. It shows the core habit of defensible note-writing: never let an inference borrow the authority of a fact.

**Components.** There are two columns separated by "vs". The left column, in green, is titled *Observed fact (cite the source)*. Each item is a statement taken from a log event or query result, with its UTC time. The right column, in violet, is titled *Inference (state confidence)*. Each item is a conclusion drawn from the fact in the same position on the left, and each carries a confidence word or an "unknown" marker.

**Relationships.** Read the columns item by item. The first pair links the 06:14:00Z sysmon event (WINWORD.EXE starting a hidden, encoded PowerShell) to the inference that a macro document executed code, held at high confidence. The second pair links eighteen 220-byte requests at a 300-second mean gap to the inference of command-and-control beaconing, also high. The third pair links the 06:46:00Z VPN success after a spray to a compromised `gopal` credential, high confidence, with the method unknown. The fourth pair links the 48,200,000-byte upload to "probable exfiltration", medium confidence, because the content of the upload is not in the data. The last pair is the tempting one: "VPN session and phishing linked" stays a hypothesis with low confidence, because no event connects them.

**Execution flow.** In practice you collect the left column first, during validation, then write the right column when you reason about what the facts mean. Facts do not change when you learn more; inferences do, and when they change you append a new analysis entry rather than editing the old one.

**Decision logic.** The confidence words drive decisions. A high-confidence inference about a compromised credential justifies an immediate escalation and a reset request; a low-confidence link between two cases justifies a hunt or a question, not a statement in a management report.

**Technical interpretation.** Each left-column item can be reproduced with one `minisiem.py search` query, which makes it independently checkable. The right-column items cannot be reproduced by a query; they can only be argued. That is the practical test: "could someone re-run something to see this?" If yes, it is a fact; if no, it is an inference.

**Practical example.** A Tier 2 analyst taking over the case at shift change reads the right column and knows exactly which questions are still open: what the upload contained, and whether the two cases are linked. Nothing on the left needs to be re-verified before work continues.

**Security implications.** Inferences that masquerade as facts propagate. An unmarked "the attacker came in through the VPN" could steer containment toward the VPN and away from the workstation where the code actually ran.

**Key learning.** Facts cite a source and can be re-run; inferences state a confidence and say what is unknown. Write both, and never blur them.

### Words that signal confidence

Use a small, consistent vocabulary for confidence and agree it across the team, so that "likely" means the same thing in every note. A simple three-level scale works well in triage:

| Word | Use when |
| --- | --- |
| Confirmed / high confidence | Several independent sources agree and no plausible benign explanation remains. |
| Probable / medium confidence | The evidence points one way, but a key piece is missing or a benign explanation has not been ruled out. |
| Possible / low confidence | A plausible hypothesis that the evidence does not contradict, but does not establish either. |

Pair each confidence word with the reason: "Probable exfiltration (medium): 48.2 MB uploaded to a domain seen nowhere else in the proxy data; content unknown."

## Timestamps in UTC

A SOC correlates events from many sources, often in different time zones and often with clocks that drift. The only workable rule is that **every time in a case record is in UTC, written in ISO 8601 with an explicit marker**: `2026-11-03T06:12:00Z`. The `Z` (or `+00:00`) is not decoration; without it, a reader cannot tell whether a time is UTC or local.

The Banyan dataset follows this rule: every event's `@timestamp` ends in `Z`. Banyan's staff work in India Standard Time, UTC+05:30, so the phishing download at 06:12:00Z happened at 11:42 IST, late morning in the office. When local time matters (the user says "I opened an invoice just before lunch", or a deadline runs on local business hours), write both: `06:12:00Z (11:42 IST)`. Never write the local time alone.

Three further rules keep timelines honest. **Record which timestamp you used**: event time on the host, collector receipt time and SIEM index time can differ by seconds or hours when a pipeline is backed up. **Keep the precision you were given**: the beacon requests have second-level times (the last is `07:42:02Z`), and that regularity was part of the evidence. **Note known clock problems**, with the offset and whether you corrected for it. India's CERT-In directions of April 2022 require organisations to synchronise clocks to the NTP servers of NIC or NPL (or sources traceable to them), precisely so that logs from different systems can be compared.

> [!TIP]
> Put the UTC time at the start of every timeline line and every note entry. Sorting then works, scanning works, and a reader never has to hunt for the clock.

## Evidence references with hashes

A note says "the export shows 32 events on WS-104". The next reader needs to know which export, where it is, and whether it is still the same file. An **evidence reference** answers all three. The course kit's `LAB_ASSETS/C08/evidence_export.py` produces exactly the material a reference needs: it exports the raw events for one host and time window from the course SIEM, writes a SHA-256 manifest and appends a chain-of-custody entry. Run in a working copy of the kit, after loading the dataset:

```terminal
$ python evidence_export.py CASE-0001 WS-104
exported 32 events -> evidence\CASE-0001\WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl
sha256 8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf
$ cat evidence/CASE-0001/custody.jsonl
{"case": "CASE-0001", "item": "WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl", "sha256": "8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf", "events": 32, "query": {"host": "WS-104", "start": "2026-11-03T06:00:00Z", "end": "2026-11-03T09:00:00Z"}, "action": "exported from minisiem", "by": "analyst.t2", "utc": "2026-11-16T16:07:40Z"}
```

The two positional arguments are the case ID (it names the output folder) and the host whose events are exported. The script's default window is 06:00 to 09:00 UTC on 3 November; it writes the events as JSON Lines in time order, writes `SHA256SUMS` in the format that `sha256sum -c` reads, and appends one JSON line to `custody.jsonl`. The `utc` field records when *you* ran the export, so yours will show your own time; the hash will match, because the query, ordering and line endings are fixed. On Windows the script prints the path with backslashes, as shown.

Verifying the item before you rely on it is one command, and a single changed character is caught:

```terminal
$ cd evidence/CASE-0001
$ sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS
WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl: OK
$ sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS
WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl: FAILED
sha256sum: WARNING: 1 computed checksum did NOT match
```

The second check was run after deliberately changing the upload size `48200000` to `482000` in the exported file (the original was then restored from a backup copy). `-c` reads each `hash  filename` line in the manifest, recomputes the hash of the named file and reports `OK` or `FAILED`. On Windows, PowerShell's `Get-FileHash -Algorithm SHA256` gives the same digest, printed in upper-case hex.

In the case note, the reference is then a short block in the Evidence section:

```text
E1  WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl  (32 events, WS-104, 2026-11-03T06:00:00Z to 09:00:00Z)
    SHA-256 8521c97cab10c667d87b01db92fa9c45a5f130dfc314fb49c622eaf1f2919acf
    Location: case store, evidence/CASE-0001/   Produced by: evidence_export.py, analyst.t2
    Custody: custody.jsonl entry 1   Verified: sha256sum -c OK before analysis
```

Timeline lines then cite the item: `06:14:00Z sysmon WINWORD.EXE started powershell.exe (E1)`. A reviewer can open E1, check the hash and find the line. Notice what the reference does *not* need to contain: the events themselves. The note points to the evidence; it does not try to be the evidence.

> [!WARNING]
> An export selected by host contains only events that have that host field. The same script run for `WS-107` (`python evidence_export.py CASE-0002 WS-107`) exports 10 events, all of them proxy requests, because VPN events in this dataset carry no host. If the VPN logons are evidence in your case, record them with their own reference (the exact query and a hash of its saved output) and write down the gap. An evidence list that silently omits a source is worse than one that names what is missing.

## Writing for the next analyst and for audit

The two readers who matter most after you are the colleague who continues the case and the reviewer who checks it later. They need different things from the same record.

### The next analyst

The next analyst needs to **continue without repeating**. Three habits serve them.

**Record what you checked, including what came back empty.** "Searched proxy logs for any other host contacting `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, `banyan-payroll-update.example` or `files-share-drop.example`: none found" saves the next person from running the same search, and it bounds the case: as far as proxy data can show, the activity is limited to WS-104. A negative result is a finding. Write it down with its scope, including the gap: hosts that bypass the proxy would not appear.

**Quote queries exactly.** Paraphrases hide mistakes. "Checked WS-104 for non-beacon activity" could have been run as `... AND NOT (url LIKE '%ping%')`, a filter that silently drops every event with no URL (Windows and Sysmon events included) because a comparison with NULL in SQL is neither true nor false. If the exact query is in the note, the next analyst can spot the flaw; if only the paraphrase is there, the flaw is invisible.

**End with owned, specific next steps.** "Further investigation required" is not a next step. "T2: decode the PowerShell command from the endpoint (not in SIEM data); IR lead: decide isolation of WS-104; T1: ask Sales manager to confirm whether `gopal` used the VPN at 06:46Z" is.

### The reviewer and the auditor

The reviewer needs to see that the **decision followed from the evidence and the procedure**. That means the disposition is justified in the note's own words ("Closed as benign true positive: group change by the `itadmin` build account matches approved change CHG-1042"), the times of escalations and approvals are recorded, and nothing has been silently rewritten.

Case notes should be **append-only**. If you discover that an earlier entry was wrong, do not edit it. Add a new, dated entry: "Correction to 08:41Z entry: the first beacon request was at 06:17:00Z, not 06:19Z." An explicit correction tells the reader what changed and why, and preserves the fact that a decision was once made on wrong information.

A note is also a record that other people will read for years, so:

- **Minimise personal data.** Record the usernames, hosts and roles the investigation needs, not the content of a user's personal mail or browsing. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 applies to personal data the SOC processes.
- **Never record secrets.** Note that a password or token was exposed and where, so it can be rotated; never the value itself.
- **Carry sharing labels forward.** Enrichment from TLP:AMBER intelligence makes the note TLP:AMBER too.
- **Write neutrally about people.** "User `divya` opened the attachment" is a fact; "the user was careless" is an opinion that helps nobody.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A case note can become evidence in a regulator report, an insurance claim or a court case. Write every entry as if it will be read aloud by someone who was not there.

## Worked example: rebuilding a weak note

Here is a note of the kind the course library's scenario exercises warn about, written at the end of a busy shift about the WS-104 activity:

```text
Malware on Divya's PC. Looks like she clicked a phishing mail this morning
(11:40ish) and the attacker got in via VPN as well. Created a backdoor admin,
stole data, wiped logs. Contained. Closing as TP.
```

Every sentence fails at least one test from this lesson. "This morning (11:40ish)" is an approximate local time. "Phishing mail" is an inference: the data shows a web download, not an email. "Got in via VPN as well" presents a low-confidence link as fact. "Stole data" asserts the content of an upload nobody has seen. "Contained" names no action, approver or time. No evidence is referenced, and "closing" an active compromise with open questions is premature.

Rebuilt from the dataset (times and facts are real; the note times, analyst names and actions are illustrative):

```text
CASE-0001  WS-104: macro document -> hidden PowerShell -> beaconing, admin account, large upload, log cleared
Severity: High   Status: Escalated to T2/IR   Owner: analyst.t1   Opened: 2026-11-03T08:41Z

SUMMARY
On 2026-11-03 finance workstation WS-104 (user divya, payments approver) downloaded a macro
document from a look-alike domain; Word then started hidden, encoded PowerShell and the host
beaconed to a rare domain every ~300 s. A local account svc_update was created and added to
Administrators, 48.2 MB was uploaded to a file-sharing look-alike, and the Security log was
cleared. High confidence of compromise. Upload content and initial-access email not yet known.

ENTITIES
WS-104 victim host | divya user on victim host | banyan-payroll-update.example delivery domain
cdn-telemetry-sync.example suspected C2 | files-share-drop.example upload destination
svc_update account created on WS-104

TIMELINE (UTC, event time from source; all from E1)
06:12:00Z proxy   GET https://banyan-payroll-update.example/invoice.docm (812 bytes out)
06:14:00Z sysmon  WINWORD.EXE -> powershell.exe -NoP -W Hidden -EncodedCommand
06:17:00Z proxy   first of 18 requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping, 220 bytes each
07:02:00Z windows 4720 account svc_update created (subject divya)
07:03:00Z windows 4732 svc_update added to Administrators (subject divya)
07:42:02Z proxy   last beacon request in data
08:12:00Z proxy   upload to files-share-drop.example/upload, bytes_out 48200000
08:17:00Z windows 1102 Security audit log cleared (subject divya)

ANALYSIS
- Code execution from macro document: high (download 2 min before Office->PowerShell, hidden + encoded).
- C2 beaconing: high (18 requests, mean gap 300 s, CV 0.004 per hunt.py; domain on ISAC-A feed, conf 85).
- Persistence via local admin: high (4720/4732 under divya's session after execution; no logon by
  svc_update seen in data).
- Exfiltration: probable/medium (48.2 MB to a domain seen nowhere else in proxy data; content unknown).
- Anti-forensics: high (1102 five minutes after upload).
- Link to VPN spray / gopal (CASE-0002): possible/low; no event connects them. Kept as hypothesis.
- Ruled out: other hosts contacting the three domains (proxy search: none). Gap: non-proxied traffic.

ACTIONS
08:55Z escalated to T2 and IR lead per escalation criteria (analyst.t1)

EVIDENCE
E1 WS-104_2026-11-03_events.jsonl, 32 events, SHA-256 8521c97c...2919acf (full hash in custody.jsonl),
   evidence/CASE-0001/, verified OK

DISPOSITION / NEXT
True positive, open. T2: endpoint triage of WS-104 (decode PowerShell, check svc_update use).
IR lead: isolation decision. Legal/DPO: personal-data assessment once upload content known.
```

Compare the two. The rebuilt note is longer, but nothing in it is padding: every line either states a fact with a time and source, states an inference with a confidence, or assigns work. The beacon statistics (`CV 0.004`) come from the kit's `hunt.py` output and the feed confidence from `ioc_match.py`, both cited by name, so a reader knows where the numbers came from. In a real case record the full hash belongs in the Evidence section; it is shortened here only for width, with a pointer to where the full value lives.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local or unmarked times | Timelines from different sources interleave wrongly | UTC with `Z`; add local time in brackets only when needed |
| Enrichment written as observation | A lookup (primary device, asset owner) becomes "evidence" | Label the source: "per CMDB", "per asset mapping" |
| Verdict without reasoning | Reviewer cannot judge the closure | One sentence of justification per disposition |
| Paraphrased queries | Flawed filters cannot be spotted | Paste the exact query text |
| Omitting negative results | Next analyst repeats the work; scope is unknown | Record what was checked and found empty, with the gap |
| Editing earlier entries | Hides what was believed when decisions were made | Append dated corrections |
| Evidence "attached" without hash | Integrity cannot be shown | Name, location, SHA-256, method, custody entry |
| Writing "clearly malicious" | Confidence without content | A confidence level plus the reason |

A persistent misconception is that a good note is a long note. Length is a poor proxy. A benign true positive closed with three precise lines ("R3 hit for the `itadmin` build account; matches the documented suppression for change CHG-1042, valid to 2026-12-31; benign true positive, no action") is defensible. A two-page narrative that never states what was checked is not.

## Security implications, detection and defence

**Notes feed detection improvement.** The analysis section of a closed case is where detection engineers learn that the R2 hit carried an inferred host, or that a rule's threshold was right. Notes that just say "FP" teach nothing.

**Notes are sensitive data.** A case record lists compromised accounts, vulnerable hosts and the SOC's own blind spots. Protect the case platform like the SIEM: role-based access, MFA, audit logging of reads and changes, and separation from the systems under investigation.

**Notes underpin obligations.** CERT-In's April 2022 directions require specified incidents to be reported within six hours of being noticed. That clock can only be met, and later defended, if notes record in UTC when activity was first seen and what was known at the time.

## Best practices

1. Start every entry with the UTC time and your analyst identifier, and write as you go, not at the end.
2. Keep facts in the timeline and inferences, each with a confidence level and reason, in the analysis.
3. Label enrichment with its source and paste exact queries with their time range, including empty results and gaps.
4. Reference evidence by name, location, SHA-256, method and custody entry; verify the hash before relying on it.
5. Give next steps an owner; append corrections; minimise personal data and never record secrets.
6. Before handing over, re-read the note as the next analyst: could you continue without asking a question?

## Key takeaways

- A case note is written for readers who were not there: the next analyst, the incident handler, the reviewer and your future self.
- Defensible means traceable: every fact cites evidence, every inference states its confidence, every time is UTC, and every evidence item can be found and verified by hash.
- A fixed structure (header, summary, entities, timeline, analysis, actions, evidence, disposition) gives each kind of information one place.
- Enrichment is not observation: `host=WS-107` on the VPN alert is a lookup from `PRIMARY_HOST`, not something the VPN logged.
- Record what you checked and found empty, quote queries exactly, and end with owned next steps.
- Append corrections, minimise personal data, never store secrets, and protect the case system as carefully as the SIEM.
