## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A SOC's detections can only ever be as good as the questions it has thought to ask and the data it has chosen to collect. Every environment has places where an attacker could act without raising an alert. Some of those places are known and accepted; most are simply unknown. A **coverage gap** is any behaviour that matters to the organisation and that the SOC would not detect, whether because the telemetry is missing, because a needed field is not parsed, because no rule exists, or because the rule that exists has never been shown to work.

Lesson 3 built the tool for seeing gaps: a coverage matrix with separate columns for data, rule and test, and the warning against "coverage theatre". This lesson is about using it. It answers three practical questions:

1. **What kind of gap is it?** A data-source gap and a logic gap look the same on a heat map (an uncovered cell) but have entirely different owners, costs and fixes.
2. **Where are the real gaps in our environment?** Not the gaps a vendor brochure lists, but those you can prove by querying your own data, as you will do for the Banyan dataset.
3. **Which gaps should be closed first?** There are always more gaps than engineering time. Prioritising them by the threat they hide, the damage they allow and the cost of closing them turns a long, discouraging list into a plan.

The lab work uses `LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py` and the Banyan dataset `banyan_events.jsonl`, in your own working copy. Every output shown was produced there. The dataset was designed for teaching, so its gaps are partly deliberate; that makes it a good subject, because every gap described below can be demonstrated with a query rather than asserted.

> [!NOTE]
> The most dangerous property of a coverage gap is that it is silent. A rule that cannot see its data does not fail loudly; it returns zero hits, which looks exactly like a quiet, safe day. "No alerts" is only evidence of "no attack" for behaviour you have proven you can see.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Coverage | The set of behaviours relevant to the organisation that the SOC would detect, with evidence (data collected, rule present, rule tested). |
| Coverage gap | A relevant behaviour that would not be detected. |
| Data-source gap | The telemetry that would reveal the behaviour is not collected at all, so no rule can detect it. |
| Field gap | The telemetry is collected, but a field the logic needs is missing, unparsed or unreliable. |
| Scope gap (partial collection) | The telemetry exists for some hosts, accounts or time periods but not for others. |
| Logic gap | The data and fields are available, but no rule looks for the behaviour, or the rule is narrower than the behaviour. |
| Untested coverage | A rule exists but has never been shown to fire on a known-bad case and stay quiet on a near miss. |
| Compensating detection | A rule on a neighbouring step of the attack that would catch the attacker even though this step is uncovered. |
| Threat model | The organisation's documented view of which adversaries and techniques are likely to be used against it and what they would target. |
| Gap register | The maintained list of known gaps with type, owner, priority, decision (close, accept, compensate) and review date. |

## Data-source gaps versus logic gaps

Every detection rests on three conditions, introduced in Lesson 3: the behaviour must leave an observable trace, the trace must be collected with the fields the logic needs, and logic must recognise it. A gap is a failure of one of these conditions, and the condition that fails tells you what kind of gap you have.

### Data-source gaps

A **data-source gap** exists when the telemetry is not there. No rule, however clever, can detect a DNS tunnel if DNS queries are not logged, or lateral movement between servers if neither the servers' authentication logs nor network flow records are collected. Data-source gaps share three properties:

- **They are owned outside detection engineering.** Closing one usually needs an infrastructure change: enabling a log, deploying an agent, configuring forwarding, buying a licence or storage. The SOC can request and justify it but rarely implements it alone.
- **They are expensive and slow.** New telemetry has a cost in volume, storage, parsing (Module 3), retention (Module 3, Lesson 7) and privacy review. It needs a project, not a pull request.
- **They are broad.** One new source often closes many gaps at once. Process creation with command lines, for instance, supports detections for execution, persistence, defence impairment and discovery.

Two variants are easy to miss because the source *appears* to be collected. A **field gap** is collection without the field the logic needs: Lesson 3 found that minisiem's table has no `group` column, so R3 cannot tell an addition to Administrators from any other local group change, even though the raw event contains the group. A **scope gap** is collection that does not cover every host, account or hour: an agent missing from some servers, a log source that stops overnight, a cloud account never connected. Both are data problems, fixed in the pipeline, but they show up only when someone measures collection against an inventory, as Module 2, Lesson 8 did with source-health checks.

### Logic gaps

A **logic gap** exists when the data is there and nobody is looking. The telemetry is collected and parsed, but no rule recognises the behaviour, or the rule that exists is narrower than the behaviour it claims to cover. Logic gaps are the opposite of data-source gaps in each respect:

- **They are owned by detection engineering** and closed with a rule, a test and a review, through the change path of Lesson 5.
- **They are cheap and fast.** The data is already paid for. A logic gap can often be closed in days.
- **They are narrow.** Each rule usually closes one gap, or one variant of one.

A logic gap also includes rules that are too narrow. R1 matches only `WINWORD.EXE` as the parent, so an Excel or PowerPoint macro that starts PowerShell is a logic gap inside a technique the matrix might show as covered. The data (process creation with parent) is present; the logic simply does not ask.

### Why the distinction matters

The distinction decides who acts, how fast, and with what argument. A detection engineer who tries to close a data-source gap with a rule wastes effort; a manager who funds a new log source to close a logic gap wastes money. The distinction also affects how you report. "We have no detection for T1071.004 (DNS used for command and control)" invites the question "why not write one?", whereas "we collect no DNS telemetry, so DNS-based command and control is invisible" states the real problem and its real owner.

The question of which process created `svc_update` at 07:02 stops at the second: process telemetry exists but ends at 06:14, so it is a **Field or scope gap**. ## Finding the real gaps in the Banyan dataset

Gaps are found by measuring, not by reading a vendor's list of supported log types. The method has three steps: inventory what is actually collected, check the scope of each source against the hosts and the time period, and then walk the attack (or the threat model) step by step asking what each step would leave in the data you have.

### Step 1: inventory what is collected

Load the dataset in your working copy and list each source with the event types it carries:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, COUNT(DISTINCT json_extract(raw,'$.event_id')) AS ids, group_concat(DISTINCT event_id) AS event_ids FROM events GROUP BY source"
source | ids | event_ids
proxy | 0 | None
sysmon | 1 | 1
vpn | 0 | None
windows | 5 | 4624,4625,4720,4732,1102
```

`json_extract()` reads a field from the raw JSON kept in the `raw` column; `group_concat(DISTINCT ...)` lists the distinct event IDs per source. The inventory is short, and its shortness is the first finding:

- **Four sources only:** web proxy, Sysmon-style endpoint, Windows Security and VPN. There is **no DNS** (resolver or endpoint), **no network flow or firewall** data, no mail gateway, no EDR, no cloud or identity-provider logs. Module 4, Lesson 5 already noted the absence of DNS.
- **Sysmon carries only event 1**, process creation. No network connections, file creation, registry or image-load events are collected, and there is no PowerShell script block logging (event 4104), so the content of the encoded command at 06:14 can never be recovered.
- **Windows Security carries five event IDs**: logons (4624), failed logons (4625), account creation (4720), group membership (4732) and log clearing (1102). Nothing about services, scheduled tasks, Kerberos or process creation (4688) is present.

Every missing source is a candidate data-source gap. Whether it matters depends on the threat model, which is why the list is not yet a priority order.

### Step 2: check scope against hosts and time

A source can be present and still blind for part of the estate or the day. Module 2, Lesson 8 measured source health per host; here the question is narrower: *during the incident*, what could each source see? Start with the compromised workstation after the moment of execution:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, COUNT(*) AS n FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND ts > '2026-11-03T06:14:00Z' AND ts <= '2026-11-03T08:17:00Z' GROUP BY source"
source | n
proxy | 24
windows | 3
```

Between the PowerShell start at 06:14 and the log clear at 08:17, WS-104 produced 24 proxy events and three Windows Security events (the 4720, the 4732 and the 1102), and **no Sysmon events at all**. As Module 2 showed, this is a property of how the generator writes routine endpoint events (all within about two hours of each user's logon), not an outage. For coverage the consequence is the same as a real outage: from 06:14 onwards, no process creation is recorded on any host. The SOC can see *that* `svc_update` was created at 07:02, but not which process created it; it can see the 48.2 MB upload at 08:12, but not which program sent it; it can see that the Security log was cleared at 08:17, but not by what. This is a **scope gap** in an otherwise valuable source, and it hides most of the attacker's hands-on activity.

The second scope check follows the VPN. R2 established that `gopal`'s password was used from the spraying address at 06:46. What did that session do?

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, COUNT(*) AS n FROM events WHERE src_ip='203.0.113.66' GROUP BY source"
source | n
vpn | 9
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT source, host, COUNT(*) AS n, MIN(ts) AS first, MAX(ts) AS last FROM events WHERE user='gopal' AND ts > '2026-11-03T06:46:00Z' GROUP BY source, host"
source | host | n | first | last
proxy | WS-107 | 19 | 2026-11-03T07:22:00Z | 2026-11-03T11:28:00Z
vpn | None | 1 | 2026-11-03T13:33:00Z | 2026-11-03T13:33:00Z
```

The spraying address appears in nine VPN events (eight failures and the success) and nowhere else. After 06:46, `gopal` appears only in routine proxy traffic from his own workstation WS-107 and in a second VPN login at 13:33 from a different address, `198.51.100.104`. The VPN records carry no internal host and no assigned tunnel address, and there are no flow, firewall or internal authentication logs to follow the tunnel inside. So the most important question about the R2 alert, *what did the attacker reach with the valid account?*, cannot be answered from this data. That is a **data-source gap**, and it turns a high-confidence detection into an incomplete case.

### Step 3: walk the attack through the data

The last step takes each behaviour in the threat model (for this exercise, the Banyan storyline and its obvious variants) and asks whether the data contains it and whether a rule looks for it. Lesson 3's matrix did this for the observed techniques. Two further queries show logic gaps inside data that is already collected. First, the delivery:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, host, user, url, bytes_out FROM events WHERE source='proxy' AND (lower(url) LIKE '%.docm' OR lower(url) LIKE '%.xlsm' OR lower(url) LIKE '%.doc' OR lower(url) LIKE '%.zip')"
ts | host | user | url | bytes_out
2026-11-03T06:12:00Z | WS-104 | divya | https://banyan-payroll-update.example/invoice.docm | 812
```

The proxy recorded the macro-enabled document download two minutes before R1 fired, and it is the only such download of the day. A rule for downloads of macro-enabled Office files (perhaps combined with a domain first seen that day) would have alerted at the moment of delivery. This is a pure **logic gap**: the data and fields are present and only the rule is missing. The same is true of the two failed console logons (4625) by `ben` and `hema` at 04:05; they are benign typos, but there is no rule for repeated local failures at all, and a guessing attack against a workstation would go unflagged.

Second, the variants. R1 covers Word only, so Excel, PowerPoint or a script host starting PowerShell is a logic gap within T1059.001. R6 sees only beaconing that passes through the proxy, so an implant that resolves its server over DNS and connects directly, or tunnels over DNS itself (T1071.004), would be invisible: there is no DNS and no flow data to see it, which makes that variant a data-source gap. R4 sees uploads only through the proxy for the same reason.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> A gap analysis is only as good as its inventory. Run Steps 1 and 2 against what the SIEM actually contains, per host and per hour, not against the list of sources someone believes is configured. Module 2, Lesson 6 showed how a collection delay can make an event arrive too late for a rule; that is a scope gap in time even though the event is eventually stored.

## Prioritising gaps by threat

The Banyan analysis has already produced more gaps than a small SOC can close in a quarter, and a real estate will produce dozens. Closing them in the order they were found, or starting with the cheapest, wastes effort on gaps that do not matter. Prioritisation weighs each gap against four questions.

1. **How likely is the hidden behaviour, for us?** Gaps that hide techniques used by the adversaries in your threat model (the groups and malware that target your sector, the techniques seen in your own incidents and exercises) rank above gaps that hide exotic techniques. For a microfinance company, credential attacks on remote access, phishing with malicious documents and data theft are central; techniques against industrial control systems are not.
2. **How much harm does it allow?** A gap at a decisive step (valid-account access, privilege, exfiltration, impairing defences) is worse than a gap at a step with little consequence on its own.
3. **Is there a compensating detection?** If an adjacent step is reliably detected, the gap is less urgent. The missing delivery rule is softened by R1, which fires two minutes later. The VPN follow-on gap has no such compensation: nothing downstream would reliably catch an attacker using `gopal`'s session inside the network.
4. **What does it cost to close, and how much else does it close?** A logic gap closed by one rule is cheap. A data-source gap is expensive but may close several gaps at once: full process telemetry for the whole day, with command lines, supports detections for persistence, impairment and discovery all together.

Combine the answers into a simple, explainable ranking. Many teams score likelihood and impact on a three-point scale, multiply them, and then adjust for compensating detections and cost; the exact arithmetic matters much less than writing down the reasoning so that it can be challenged. Avoid false precision: a score of 7.4 against 7.1 does not justify an order, but "high, no compensation" against "high, compensated by R1" does.

The outcome for each gap is one of three decisions, recorded in a **gap register** with an owner and a review date:

- **Close**: request the telemetry, fix the pipeline or write the rule.
- **Compensate**: accept the gap for now because another detection or control covers the risk, and record which one.
- **Accept**: a documented risk decision by someone with the authority to make it, with a date for reconsidering it.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"No alerts means we are safe."** Zero hits from a rule with no data is a gap, not a clean bill of health.
- **Treating a listed source as a covered source.** A source that ends at 06:14, misses some hosts or lacks a field is a partial source. Measure it.
- **Writing rules to close data-source gaps.** No query can see DNS that is not logged. Raise the gap with the telemetry owner instead.
- **Prioritising by cost alone.** Cheap logic gaps get closed; the expensive data gaps that hide the worst behaviour stay open indefinitely.
- **Counting the whole of ATT&CK.** Gaps should be measured against your threat model, not against every technique in the knowledge base.
- **Forgetting compensations change.** If R1 is tuned or broken, the delivery gap it compensates becomes urgent. Link compensating detections to the gaps they cover.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Attackers do not need to know your gap register to exploit it; they exploit the common gaps that most organisations share, such as missing DNS logging, unmonitored remote access and endpoint telemetry that stops at the edge of the managed estate. Treat the gap register as sensitive, because it is a map of where to act unseen, and restrict who can read it.

Gaps also open over time. Agents are uninstalled, log sources are reconfigured, parsers change and rules go silent. Coverage must therefore be monitored, not just assessed: alert on sources that stop reporting or drop below their baseline (Module 2), on rules whose hit counts fall to zero after a change (Lesson 5), and re-run the inventory and scope queries on a schedule. Emulation in an authorised lab, generating known benign artefacts of a technique and confirming they reach the SIEM and fire the rule, is the strongest evidence that a gap is closed; it must only be done on systems you are authorised to test.

Under India's CERT-In directions of April 2022, organisations must enable logs of their ICT systems and retain them for a rolling 180 days within India. A data-source gap can therefore be a compliance issue as well as a detection one, which often helps the business case for closing it.

## Best practices

- Maintain a gap register with type, threat hidden, compensation, priority, owner, decision and review date.
- Classify every gap by the first condition it fails: data, field or scope, logic, or evidence.
- Measure collection per source, host and hour against the asset inventory; never assume.
- Walk your threat model step by step through the data, including the obvious variants of each technique.
- Prioritise by likelihood and harm, adjusted for compensating detections, then by cost and breadth.
- Report data-source gaps to the telemetry owner as risks, in business language.
- Re-assess after every incident, exercise, major tuning change and telemetry change.

## Key takeaways

- A coverage gap is a relevant behaviour the SOC would not detect; it is silent by nature, so it must be measured.
- Data-source gaps (telemetry not collected) and logic gaps (data present, no rule) have different owners, costs and fixes; field and scope gaps are data problems hidden inside sources that seem present.
- The Banyan data has no DNS, flow, mail or EDR telemetry; Sysmon records only process creation and nothing after 06:14; the VPN cannot be followed inside the network.
- The macro document download at 06:12 and local failed logons are logic gaps in data already collected.
- Prioritise gaps by the threat they hide, the harm they allow and compensating detections, then by cost; one data source can close many gaps.
- Record every gap and decision in a register, keep it confidential, and re-measure coverage continuously.
