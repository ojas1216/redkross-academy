## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every earlier lesson in this module assumed that the events you need are still in the SIEM when you go looking for them. Ingestion, parsing and normalisation put events into storage; correlation, dashboards and search read them back. **Retention** is the decision about how long each event stays, in what form, on what kind of storage, and what happens to it at the end. It is the least glamorous part of SIEM engineering and one of the most consequential, because its failures are invisible until the day an investigation needs data that has already been deleted.

Consider the questions an analyst asks during a real case. *When did this host first talk to that domain? Has this account ever logged on from outside India before? Was the administrator account created last night, or has it existed for six months?* Each question reaches back in time, and each answer is only as good as the oldest event still held. An intrusion is usually discovered some time after it starts: the attacker gains a foothold, waits, moves, and only later does something loud enough to trigger an alert. If the SIEM keeps thirty days of data and the foothold was gained forty days ago, the root cause is gone. The SOC can describe what the attacker did last week, but not how they got in, which means it cannot be confident that the way in is closed.

Against this sits cost. Security telemetry is large, it grows every year, and fast searchable storage is expensive. Keeping every event on the fastest tier for years is rarely affordable, and keeping it for weeks is rarely sufficient. The answer used by almost every production SIEM is **tiering**: recent data on fast storage where analysts search it constantly, older data on progressively cheaper and slower storage, and a defined point at which it is deleted. Legal and regulatory requirements set floors under some of those periods (in India, the CERT-In Directions of April 2022 require covered entities to keep logs of their ICT systems for a rolling 180 days), while privacy law pushes the other way: personal data should not be kept longer than its purpose needs.

This lesson explains the four reasons a SOC retains telemetry, how hot, warm, cold and archive tiers work and what each costs, the legal and regulatory requirements you must design around, and the forensic value that is lost when retention is set by budget alone. It finishes with a worked experiment on the course lab kit in which you purge part of the Banyan Microfinance dataset and watch the incident's root cause disappear while its noisy final stages remain.

> [!NOTE]
> Retention is a policy decision that the SOC informs but does not make alone. Legal, compliance, privacy, IT and finance all have a stake. The SOC's job is to state clearly what investigations need and what is lost under each option.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Retention period | How long a class of data is kept before it is deleted, usually counted from the event's timestamp or its ingestion time. |
| Hot tier | Fast storage (typically SSD-backed, fully indexed) holding the most recent data, which is written to and searched constantly. |
| Warm tier | Storage for recent but no longer written data; still indexed and searchable, on cheaper hardware or with fewer replicas. |
| Cold tier | Infrequently searched data on low-cost storage; searchable, but slower, sometimes with reduced redundancy. |
| Frozen / archive tier | The cheapest storage (often object storage or offline media); data must be restored, rehydrated or mounted before it can be searched. |
| Rehydration | Restoring archived data into a searchable tier for an investigation; takes time and sometimes costs money. |
| Rollover | Closing the current write index (or bucket) and starting a new one when it reaches an age or size, so that data ages in units. |
| Lifecycle policy | Rules that move data between tiers and finally delete it, based on age or size. |
| Legal hold | An instruction that suspends deletion of specific data because of litigation, investigation or a regulator's request. |
| Immutability (WORM) | Write-once, read-many storage that prevents modification or deletion until a set date, protecting retained logs from tampering. |
| Lookback | How far back a detection rule, hunt or baseline needs data in order to work. |
| Dwell time | The time between an attacker's initial access and their detection. |

## Why a SOC retains telemetry

It helps to separate the reasons, because each sets a different minimum and each needs data in a different form.

**Detection lookback.** Many detections compare the present with the past. "First time this user has logged on from this country", "rare process on this host", "beaconing over the last 24 hours" and the baselines used in hunting (Module 10) all need history. A rule that asks whether a domain has been seen in the estate in the last 30 days cannot work with 7 days of data. This kind of retention needs data that is **fast to search**, because rules run every few minutes.

**Investigation and scoping.** When an alert fires, the analyst reconstructs the story backwards: what happened before, on this host, by this user, from this address? Scoping then asks whether the same indicator appears anywhere else, at any time. Because intrusions are found after they begin, investigation needs data **older than the dwell time**, which industry reporting measures in days to weeks for the median case and in months for the slowest. This data is searched less often but must be complete, including the raw original events.

**Legal, regulatory and contractual obligations.** Laws, regulators, industry standards and contracts can require logs to be kept for minimum periods, in particular places, and to be produced on request. This kind of retention needs **integrity and provable completeness** more than speed: you must be able to show the logs were not altered and cover the required period.

**Operational and audit needs.** Capacity planning, SLA reporting and audit of analyst actions draw on history too, but much of this can be met with **summaries** rather than every raw event.

These purposes overlap but do not coincide. A good retention design starts by writing down, for each class of data, which of the four purposes it serves and for how long, and only then chooses storage.

## Storage tiers: hot, warm, cold and archive

A SIEM stores events in time-ordered units: indices in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch-based platforms (including the Wazuh indexer), buckets in Splunk, tables or partitions in cloud analytics services. Because each unit covers a period of time, it can be moved as a whole once that period has passed. A **lifecycle policy** says, for each unit, when it moves to the next tier and when it is deleted. Elasticsearch's index lifecycle management, for example, defines hot, warm, cold, frozen and delete phases; Splunk ages buckets from hot through warm and cold to frozen. The names differ slightly by product, but the pattern is the same.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows the path that every stored event follows from the moment it is indexed to the moment it is deleted, and what changes at each step: speed of search, cost of storage and who typically uses the data. It matters because each arrow is a configurable decision, and a wrong decision at any arrow either wastes money or loses evidence.

**Components.** The flow has five steps. **Hot** ("days to weeks: SSD, fully indexed, written now") is where the indexer is writing and where almost all searches and rule executions land. **Warm** ("weeks to months: read-only, cheaper nodes") holds data that is no longer being written but is still searched routinely during investigations. **Cold** ("months: low-cost disks, slower search") holds data that is searched occasionally, for scoping and hunts. **Frozen / archive** ("months to years: object storage, restore first") holds data for legal and long-tail forensic needs; it is the cheapest and the slowest. The last step, **Delete**, is shown in dark because it is the irreversible outcome: once a unit is deleted (and no legal hold applies) the evidence it held is gone for good.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each arrow is a transition triggered by the lifecycle policy, usually by age since rollover. The arrows point one way only: data never moves back up automatically. Moving archived data back into a searchable tier is a separate, deliberate action called rehydration.

**Execution flow.** A new event is written to the current hot index. When that index reaches its rollover age or size, it is closed and a new one begins. Days later the policy moves the closed index to warm, then to cold, then to frozen, and finally deletes it. Each index is a unit, so a whole day's (or a whole size block's) events move together.

**Decision logic.** At every arrow the policy asks one question: has this unit reached the age set for this transition? A legal hold overrides the final arrow: a unit under hold is not deleted, however old it is. Choosing the ages is where purpose meets budget: the hot period should cover the lookback of your detections, the searchable periods (hot plus warm plus cold) should cover realistic dwell times, and the total should meet the strictest legal requirement.

**Technical interpretation.** Moving down the flow typically reduces replicas, moves data to cheaper media, makes it read-only, and in the frozen tier may keep only a snapshot in object storage that is mounted or restored on demand. Search still works, but slower, and some platforms charge for retrieval.

**Practical example.** A SOC sets hot to 14 days, warm to 90, cold to 180 and archive to one year. A rule needing 7 days of baseline runs entirely on hot data; an investigation into an intrusion that started 60 days ago searches warm data; a CERT-In request for a five-month-old log is answered from cold; a lawsuit about an event from ten months ago needs a restore from archive.

**Security implications.** Every tier must keep the same access controls and integrity protection as hot storage, and deletion should be possible only through the policy, never by an individual. Older tiers are a target for an attacker who wants to erase history, and an inviting place for weak controls because "nobody looks there".

**Key learning.** Tiering trades search speed for storage cost as data ages; set the ages from what detections, investigations and the law need, not from what is left in the budget.

### How the tiers are implemented

Three mechanisms make tiering work in practice.

**Time-based units and rollover.** Because data is written into one current unit at a time, the platform can close that unit when it reaches a set age (for example one day) or size, and start a fresh one. From then on the closed unit is read-only, which makes it cheap to compress, move and eventually delete as a whole. A practical consequence is that deletion is never exact to the minute: a unit is deleted when its *newest* event passes the retention age, so some events are kept slightly longer than the policy states. Retention statements should therefore be written as minimums.

**Changing the physical storage.** Hot data usually sits on fast local disks with one or more replicas so that a node failure does not interrupt search. Warm and cold tiers use larger, slower disks and fewer replicas. The archive tier commonly writes snapshots to object storage, which is cheap per gigabyte and durable but cannot be searched directly without being mounted or restored.
**Reducing what is kept.** Not every byte must be kept at full fidelity for the full period. Common techniques are compression (security events are highly repetitive text and compress well), dropping fields that no detection or investigation uses, and keeping **summaries** (daily counts per host, user and destination) for longer than the raw events. Summaries answer "has this domain ever been seen here?" long after the raw proxy logs are gone, but they cannot reproduce an individual event as evidence.

> [!WARNING]
> Field dropping and summarisation are irreversible. Decide them with the detection engineers and investigators, and keep the **raw original event** for at least the period over which you may need to re-parse data with a corrected parser or produce it as evidence.

## Cost: what you pay for and how to estimate it

SIEM cost is driven by four quantities: how much data arrives per day (**ingest volume**), how long it is kept on each tier, how many copies are kept (replicas and backups), and how much compute is needed to search it. Commercial products are frequently licensed by ingest volume, which makes the decision *whether to collect* a source the first cost lever; storage tiering is the second.

A useful first estimate needs only an average event size and a daily event count. The course dataset gives a measured example of event size: `banyan_events.jsonl` is 65,070 bytes for 340 events, about 191 bytes per event, and compresses with gzip at maximum level to 3,886 bytes, a ratio of roughly 16.7 to 1. Real events are larger (a full Windows Security event with all its fields is several times bigger than the kit's normalised records) and real compression ratios vary with content, so treat these as an illustration of the method, not as planning figures.

For an illustrative estate producing 20 million events a day at an average of 500 bytes, raw daily volume is

$$20{,}000{,}000 \times 500\ \text{bytes} = 10\ \text{GB per day}$$

and 180 days of raw data is about 1.8 TB before compression, replicas and index overhead. If hot storage holds 14 days with one replica, the hot tier needs roughly $14 \times 10 \times 2 = 280$ GB plus index overhead, while the remaining 166 days can sit on cheaper tiers with a single copy. The arithmetic shows why tiering matters: the expensive tier holds less than a tenth of the data, yet it serves almost all searches.

There are hidden costs too. Rehydrating archive data takes time and sometimes money, and a SOC that has never practised a restore discovers its problems during an incident. Deleting early has a cost that never appears on an invoice: the investigation that cannot be finished.

## Legal and regulatory requirements

Retention periods are not chosen in a vacuum. Several kinds of obligation apply, and the strictest applicable one sets the floor for each class of data.

**CERT-In Directions (India).** The Directions issued by CERT-In on 28 April 2022 under section 70B(6) of the Information Technology Act 2000 require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations to enable logs of all their ICT systems and maintain them securely for a **rolling period of 180 days**, within Indian jurisdiction, and to provide them to CERT-In when reporting an incident or when CERT-In directs. The same Directions require specified cyber incidents to be reported to CERT-In within six hours of being noticed, and require system clocks to be synchronised to the NTP servers of the National Informatics Centre or the National Physical Laboratory (or to sources traceable to them). For a SOC, this means that 180 days is a **minimum** for the covered logs, that the place of storage matters, and that the logs must be retrievable quickly enough to support a six-hour reporting clock and a later request.

**Industry standards and contracts.** Card-payment environments under PCI DSS must keep audit log history for at least twelve months, with at least the most recent three months immediately available for analysis. Sector regulators, customer contracts and cyber-insurance policies may add their own requirements. Collect these in one register owned by compliance, so the SOC is not guessing.

**Privacy law: the ceiling.** Telemetry contains personal data: user names, IP addresses, browsing history, file names. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 expects personal data to be kept only as long as its purpose requires and then erased, unless retention is required by law. Security monitoring is a legitimate purpose, but it does not justify keeping everything forever. The practical result is a retention policy with both a floor (the legal minimum and investigative need) and a ceiling (deletion when no purpose remains), and with access to older data restricted to those who need it.

**Legal hold.** When litigation, a regulator's inquiry or a law-enforcement request is expected, the relevant data must be preserved beyond its normal period. Legal hold suspends deletion for the specific data, not for the whole SIEM, and must be applied before the lifecycle policy reaches it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Regulatory wording changes and is interpreted by lawyers, not analysts. Use this lesson to understand the shape of the obligations; take the exact scope that applies to your organisation from your legal and compliance team.

Bringing purposes, obligations and cost together into one decision is the core of retention design.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows how a retention policy is derived: several independent requirements feed one decision per data class, and that decision is then expressed as tier ages and a deletion point. It matters because a retention period set from a single input (usually the budget) almost always fails one of the others.

**Components.** On the input side are five nodes. **Detection lookback** ("baselines, first-seen rules") is blue because it is an ordinary operational need. **Investigation and dwell time** ("reach back past initial access") is orange because it is the requirement most often under-estimated. **Legal and regulatory** ("CERT-In 180 days, PCI DSS, contracts") is violet, the colour of policy. **Privacy ceiling** ("DPDP: no longer than needed") is green: it protects the people whose data is held. **Cost and capacity** ("ingest, storage, restore") is slate, the neutral infrastructure constraint. All five point to **Retention policy per data class** (dark), the decision itself. That node points to **Tier ages** ("hot / warm / cold / archive") and to **Deletion point** ("unless legal hold"), and a dashed arrow from **Legal hold** (red) points to the deletion point.

**Relationships and arrows.** The solid arrows into the policy node mean "constrains": the first three push the period longer, the privacy ceiling and cost push it shorter. The arrows out of the policy are its implementation. The dashed arrow from Legal hold is an override, not a routine input: it suspends deletion for specific data.

**Execution flow.** For each data class (for example VPN logs, proxy logs, endpoint process events), you collect the five requirements, set the total period between the floor (the longest of lookback, dwell-time need and legal minimum) and the ceiling (privacy and cost), then split that period into tier ages and record the deletion point.

**Decision logic.** If the floor exceeds the ceiling, the conflict must be resolved explicitly: reduce cost by summarising or tiering, or document why privacy permits longer retention for this purpose. A legal hold always wins over the deletion point.

**Technical interpretation.** The output is a lifecycle policy in the SIEM, a written retention schedule, and the access controls for each tier.

**Practical example.** For Banyan's VPN logs: lookback needs 30 days for "new country for user" rules, investigations need at least 90, CERT-In requires 180, and privacy and cost allow a year. The policy becomes 30 days hot, then cold to day 180, then archive to one year, then deletion.

**Security implications.** A policy that is not written down is not enforced, and an undocumented deletion looks like evidence destruction. Retained logs must be protected against tampering at every tier, because an attacker who can delete history can also delete the record of their own access.

**Key learning.** The retention period is the longest of the needs that push it up, bounded by the limits that push it down, set per data class and written down.

## Forensic value: what is lost when data is deleted

Incidents are noisy at the end and quiet at the beginning. Data theft, ransomware encryption and log clearing generate loud, easily detected events; the phishing click, the first process launch and the first beacon are small and ordinary-looking. Detection often fires on the loud stages, and the analyst then has to work backwards to the quiet ones. If retention has already removed the quiet stages, the SOC can see the damage but not the cause.

Three properties of retained data decide its forensic value:

- **Coverage of the timeline.** The data must reach back past initial access. Whatever the median dwell time in published reports, your design question is: how old could the foothold be for the incidents we most fear, and do we still hold data from then?
- **Fidelity.** The raw original event, with all its fields, is what proves exactly what happened and what can be re-parsed if a parser was wrong. A summary or a normalised subset can show *that* something happened, not always *what*. This is why the course's teaching SIEM, `minisiem.py`, keeps every original JSON line in its `raw` column alongside the normalised fields.
- **Integrity.** Evidence that may support a regulatory report, disciplinary action or prosecution must be shown to be unaltered. Retention storage with restricted deletion, audit of administrative actions, and hashes recorded at export time (Module 1 Lesson 3 introduced `evidence_export.py` for this) make that possible.

## Worked example: a retention purge on the Banyan dataset

The course lab kit lets you see the forensic cost of short retention directly. The Banyan dataset covers one fictional day, 03:07Z to 13:33Z, with a scripted intrusion on workstation WS-104: a macro-enabled document download at 06:12Z, Word launching PowerShell at 06:14Z, a beacon every five minutes from 06:17Z, a VPN password spray at 06:42Z with one success at 06:46Z, a new local administrator at 07:02Z, a 48 MB upload at 08:12Z and the Security log cleared at 08:17Z. The six correlation rules in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py` each detect one stage.

To model a retention purge, imagine that the retention boundary falls at 07:00Z, so everything older is deleted, exactly as a lifecycle policy deletes an index that has passed its age. Work in your own copy of the kit (never inside the course library folder). Save the following as `retention_purge.py` in your working copy; it copies the database first so the original stays intact:

```python
"""Simulate a retention purge on a COPY of minisiem.db (Course 08, M3 L7)."""
import shutil
import sqlite3
import sys

cutoff = sys.argv[1]                      # e.g. 2026-11-03T07:00:00Z
shutil.copy("minisiem.db", "purged.db")   # never purge the working database
con = sqlite3.connect("purged.db")
gone = con.execute("DELETE FROM events WHERE ts < ?", (cutoff,)).rowcount
con.commit()
left = con.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM events").fetchone()[0]
print(f"cutoff {cutoff}: purged {gone} events, {left} remain in purged.db")
```

The query is parameterised (`?`), and the comparison works because every timestamp in the dataset uses the same ISO 8601 UTC format, so string order equals time order. Save a second helper, `compare_rules.py`, which imports the kit's rule set and prints the number of hits each rule gets against any database you name:

```python
"""Run every minisiem rule against two databases and compare hit counts."""
import sqlite3
import sys

from minisiem import RULES

for db in sys.argv[1:]:
    con = sqlite3.connect(db)
    counts = [f"{name.split()[0]}={len(con.execute(sql).fetchall())}" for name, sql in RULES.items()]
    print(f"{db:12} " + " ".join(counts))
```

Run them after loading the dataset:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python retention_purge.py 2026-11-03T07:00:00Z
cutoff 2026-11-03T07:00:00Z: purged 174 events, 166 remain in purged.db
$ python compare_rules.py minisiem.db purged.db
minisiem.db  R1=1 R2=1 R3=2 R4=1 R5=1 R6=1
purged.db    R1=0 R2=0 R3=2 R4=1 R5=1 R6=0
```

**Interpreting the result.** The purge removed 174 of the 340 events. Against the full database every rule fires. Against the purged copy, R3 (new local admin), R4 (large upload) and R5 (log cleared) still fire, because those stages happened after 07:00Z. But R1 (Office spawning a script interpreter) and R2 (VPN spray followed by success) have nothing left to match: the initial execution and the account compromise are gone. R6 (beaconing) also drops to zero even though beacons continued after 07:00Z: only 9 of the 18 requests remain, below the rule's threshold of 10. That last point matters: shortening retention does not only delete old events, it can silently break rules whose **lookback** spans the boundary.

An analyst working from the purged copy would see an account creating an administrator, uploading 48 MB and clearing a log, and would know something bad happened on WS-104. They could not show how the attacker got in, could not link the incident to the phishing domain, and could not see that a second account (gopal) was compromised over VPN from 203.0.113.66. Containment based on this partial picture would probably leave the VPN account open.

Delete `purged.db` when you have finished; your `minisiem.db` is untouched.

## Designing a retention schedule for Banyan

Applying the derivation from Figure 2 to Banyan's four sources gives a schedule such as the one below. The periods are a reasoned design for this fictional organisation, assuming it is covered by the CERT-In Directions and not subject to PCI DSS; your organisation's figures will differ.

| Data class | Hot (searchable, fast) | Total searchable | Archive until | Main driver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| VPN and identity logs | 30 days | 180 days | 1 year | Account-compromise scoping; CERT-In |
| Windows Security and Sysmon | 14 days | 180 days | 1 year | Root cause on endpoints; CERT-In |
| Web proxy | 14 days | 180 days | 180 days | Volume and privacy; CERT-In floor |
| Daily summaries (domains, hosts, logons) | 1 year | 1 year | 2 years | Long first-seen lookback, low privacy impact |
| Case records and exported evidence | n/a | Case lifetime | As legal requires | Evidence; legal hold |

Notice three design choices. Proxy logs, the largest and most privacy-sensitive source, stop at the legal floor; their long-term value is carried by summaries. Identity logs get a longer hot period because "first logon from this country" rules need a month of baseline. And exported evidence follows the case, not the SIEM schedule, so a case opened on day 170 does not lose its evidence on day 180.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"Retention means the hot period."** Licences and dashboards often advertise the hot period. What matters for investigation is the total searchable period, and what matters legally is the total period the data can be produced.
- **Setting retention from the budget alone.** The cost is visible, the lost investigation is not. Always state the forensic consequence of a proposed cut.
- **Forgetting that lookback rules break.** As the worked example showed, a rule can stop firing because its window now crosses the retention boundary.
- **Never testing a restore.** Archived data that cannot be rehydrated within the time an incident allows is not retained in any useful sense.
- **Keeping only normalised fields.** A parser bug discovered later cannot be corrected without the raw events.
- **Treating the legal minimum as a maximum, or ignoring privacy.** 180 days is a floor for covered logs; keeping all personal data indefinitely is not a safe default either.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Retained logs are a target. An attacker who has reached administrative access will often try to remove evidence; the Banyan storyline ends with Windows Event ID 1102, "the audit log was cleared", and the kit's rule R5 detects it. Clearing a local log does not help the attacker if the events were already forwarded, which is one reason central collection matters. The same reasoning applies one level up: the SIEM and its archive must be harder to tamper with than the systems they watch.

- **Separate privileges.** SIEM administrators who manage lifecycle policies should not be the same accounts used for day-to-day IT administration, and deletion should require a change-controlled policy, not a console button.
- **Use immutable storage for the archive.** WORM or object-lock settings prevent deletion before the retention date, even by an administrator.
- **Audit and alert on retention changes.** A shortened lifecycle policy, a deleted index or a disabled snapshot job should raise an alert to the SOC lead, just as a cleared Windows log does.
- **Protect older tiers equally.** Access control, encryption and audit must follow data down the tiers.
- **Minimise access to old personal data.** Restrict searches of archived data to named roles and log every restore.

## Best practices

1. Write a retention schedule per data class, with the purpose, the legal basis, the tier ages and the owner of each line.
2. Set the hot period from detection lookback, the searchable period from realistic dwell time, and the total from the strictest legal requirement, then check it against the privacy ceiling.
3. Keep raw events for the searchable period; use summaries to extend lookback cheaply.
4. Make the archive immutable and test a restore at least once a quarter, timing it.
5. Apply legal holds through a documented process, and export case evidence with hashes so it outlives SIEM retention.

## Key takeaways

- Telemetry is retained for detection lookback, investigation beyond the dwell time, legal and regulatory obligations, and operational audit; each sets a different minimum.
- Tiering moves data from hot through warm and cold to archive and deletion, trading search speed for storage cost; deletion is irreversible unless a legal hold applies.
- The CERT-In Directions of April 2022 require covered entities to keep ICT logs for a rolling 180 days within Indian jurisdiction; that is a floor, while the DPDP Act 2023 pushes towards deleting personal data when its purpose ends.
- Short retention removes the quiet early stages of an intrusion first, and can silently break rules whose lookback crosses the boundary: in the kit, a 07:00Z purge kept the log-clearing alert but erased the phishing execution, the VPN compromise and the beacon detection.
- Protect retained data as evidence: raw events, immutable archives, restricted deletion, audited policy changes and tested restores.
