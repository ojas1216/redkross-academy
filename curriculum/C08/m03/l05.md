## Introduction and conceptual foundation

By the time an event reaches a dashboard it has been collected, parsed, normalised and stored, and some of it has been matched by correlation rules. The dashboard is the part of the SIEM that people actually look at. That makes it powerful and dangerous at once. A good dashboard puts the answer to a recurring operational question in front of the person who must act on it, at the moment they need it. A bad one looks busy, reassures without justification, and hides the one fact that mattered behind a wall of colourful counts.

The central idea of this lesson is simple: **a dashboard is a set of saved questions, not a picture of the data**. Every panel should exist because somebody repeatedly needs to know something, and every panel should make it obvious what to do when the answer changes. "How many events did we ingest today?" is rarely such a question; nobody acts differently because the count is 4.1 million instead of 3.9 million. "Has any critical log source stopped sending in the last hour?" is such a question, because a yes means that rules for that source are silently blind and someone must fix the collector now.

There is a second idea that new analysts often learn the hard way: **dashboards inform; they do not detect**. A chart of event volume shows activity, not threat. In the Banyan Microfinance dataset used throughout this course, the hour in which the intrusion began is not the busiest hour of the day and would not stand out on a volume chart at all. The intrusion was found by correlation rules (Lesson 4) and would be confirmed by search (Lesson 6). Dashboards have a different job: keeping the SOC aware of its workload, its blind spots and the overall shape of activity, so that the rules and the analysts can do theirs.

This lesson covers what SOC dashboards are for, which families of dashboard a SOC normally runs and who reads each, how to design a panel starting from a question, what to show and what to leave out, and how to build realistic panels from the course lab's `minisiem.py` with real outputs from the Banyan dataset. It ends with the security side of dashboards, which is often forgotten: dashboards expose data, consume search capacity, and can mislead in ways that an attacker may benefit from.

> [!NOTE]
> Every product draws dashboards differently: Splunk, Microsoft Sentinel (workbooks), Elastic/Kibana, Wazuh and Grafana all have their own editors. The design principles in this lesson are product-neutral. The panels are built here as SQL queries in `minisiem.py` so that you can see exactly what each one computes.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Dashboard | A collection of panels, each driven by a saved search, displayed together for one audience and purpose. |
| Panel (widget, tile, visualisation) | One element of a dashboard: a number, chart, table or list produced by one query. |
| Saved search | A stored query with a fixed time range or schedule; panels, reports and correlation rules are all built on them. |
| Time range | The window of events a panel covers, such as "last 24 hours"; relative ranges move with the clock, absolute ones do not. |
| Refresh interval | How often the panel re-runs its query; each refresh costs search capacity. |
| Baseline | The normal value or range of a measure, learned from history, against which the current value is judged. |
| Threshold | A value at which a panel changes state (for example turns red) or a meta-alert fires. |
| Drilldown | A click on a panel element that opens the underlying events or a more detailed search filtered on that element. |
| Top-N | A ranked list of the N largest (or smallest) values of a field, such as the ten hosts with most outbound bytes. |
| Fill rate | The percentage of events in which a field is populated; a falling fill rate signals a parsing problem. |
| Vanity metric | A number that looks impressive but does not change any decision, such as total events ingested. |
| Situational awareness | A shared, current understanding of workload, coverage and notable activity across the SOC. |

## What SOC dashboards are for

A SOC runs dashboards for three jobs. It helps to keep them separate, because a panel that tries to do all three usually does none of them well.

### Job 1: managing the work

The alert queue is the SOC's production line, and the people running it need to see its state at a glance. Typical questions are: how many alerts are open at each severity, how old is the oldest unacknowledged high-severity alert, which alerts have breached or are about to breach their service level (Module 1, Lesson 6), and how many cases is each analyst holding. These panels are read every few minutes by the shift lead and every analyst. Their answers drive immediate actions: take the next alert, ask for help, escalate a breach.

### Job 2: assuring that the SOC can see

Monitoring is only as good as its data. Module 2 showed that a source which stops sending produces no alerts, and that an empty queue for that source looks exactly like a quiet night. Health dashboards answer: is every important source still sending, at about its usual rate, with current timestamps; are parsers failing; is indexing lagging; did any scheduled correlation search fail or time out; is storage or licence headroom running out. These panels are read at the start of every shift and whenever something looks oddly quiet. Their answers drive engineering actions: restart a collector, fix a parser, raise a change.

### Job 3: understanding activity and risk

The third family shows the shape of activity: authentication failures over time, outbound data per host, new or rare destinations, detections by ATT&CK tactic, and trends over weeks and months for managers. These panels support judgement rather than immediate action. They help an analyst ask "is this normal for us?", help detection engineers see which rules are noisy (Module 7), and help managers decide where to invest. They are the panels most likely to be misread, because activity charts invite the viewer to look for a threat that the chart cannot show.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> None of these jobs is "detect attacks". Detection is done by correlation rules, which run continuously and raise alerts, and by analysts and hunters running searches. A dashboard that someone must stare at in order to notice an attack is a detection that has not been written yet.

### Dashboard families and their audiences

Combining the jobs with the people who read them gives the usual set of SOC dashboards. Small SOCs may merge some of these; large ones may split them further by platform or customer.

| Dashboard | Audience | Typical panels | Action it drives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alert queue | Analysts, shift lead | Open alerts by severity and age; SLA breaches; unassigned high alerts | Pick, escalate, rebalance work |
| Log-source health | Shift lead, SIEM engineers | Last event per source; events per hour versus baseline; parse errors; field fill rate | Fix collectors and parsers |
| Detection performance | Detection engineers | Alerts per rule; true and false-positive rates per rule; rules that never fire | Tune, retire or test rules |
| Investigation (entity) | Analysts during a case | Everything about one host, user or IP over a chosen time range | Scope an incident |
| Activity and exposure | Analysts, hunters | Authentication failures over time; outbound bytes per host; rare destinations | Open a hunt or investigation |
| Management and risk | SOC manager, CISO | Trends in MTTA/MTTR, incidents by category, coverage, backlog | Staffing, investment, reporting |

The investigation dashboard is a special case. It is not watched; it is opened. An analyst types a host name or a user into a field at the top, and every panel re-runs filtered on that entity. In effect it is a bundle of the searches you will learn to write in Lesson 6, prepared in advance so that the first ten minutes of every investigation look the same.

The management dashboard is another special case, because its readers are not analysts. It should show trends and outcomes (Module 1, Lesson 7) in terms a non-specialist can act on, and it should never be built from the same raw panels the analysts use. Management asking for "one dashboard showing whether we are under attack" is a common request; the honest answer is that no dashboard can prove the absence of an attack, and that what can be offered is a small set of measures with owners, plus regular narrative briefings.

## Designing a panel from a question

Most bad dashboards are built from the data outwards: someone opens the dashboard editor, sees the available fields, and adds charts of whatever looks interesting. Good dashboards are built from the question inwards. The procedure below is the one this course recommends; it is short enough to apply to every panel, and it forces the decisions that are otherwise made by accident.

Start with the **question**, written as a sentence that has a yes/no or a number as its answer. "Is any critical log source silent for more than 30 minutes?" is a question. "Log sources" is not. Next, name the **audience and the action**: who will look at this panel, and what they will do differently depending on the answer. If you cannot name an action, the panel is a candidate for removal before it is built. Then define the **data and query**: which sources and normalised fields answer the question (Lesson 3 is what makes this possible across sources), over what time range, and how often the panel refreshes.

With the query in hand, decide the **baseline and threshold**. A number on its own is meaningless; 47 events in an hour is high for a VPN concentrator at night and low for a proxy at noon. The baseline may be a fixed expectation ("every source sends at least once per 15 minutes"), the same hour last week, or a rolling average. The threshold is the point at which the panel changes state, and the point at which a meta-alert should fire so that nobody has to be watching. Only then choose the **visual form**: a single number with a coloured state for yes/no questions, a line over time for trends, a sorted table for top-N lists, a bar chart for comparing a few categories. Finally, add **context and drilldown** (a click opens the underlying events, and the panel title or description links to the runbook that says what to do) and give the panel an **owner and a review date**. Panels that nobody has acted on for a quarter are retired.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram is the design procedure for a single dashboard panel, from the question it answers to its periodic review. It matters because each step is a decision that, if skipped, is made by default, and the defaults are what produce vanity charts and unread dashboards.

**Components.** Seven rounded boxes run from top to bottom. The first, **Question**, is in orange because it is the focus: its sub-label gives the worked example, "is a critical source silent > 30 min?". **Audience and action** asks who reads the panel and what they do. **Data and query** names the sources, fields and time range. **Baseline and threshold** defines normal and the point of change. **Visual form** chooses a number, line, table or top-N list. **Context and drilldown** links the panel to the underlying events and the runbook. The last box, **Owner and review**, in the dark tone, is the outcome that keeps the dashboard honest over time. A dashed feedback arrow runs from the last box back to the first, labelled with the review rule.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each downward arrow means "the next decision depends on this one". The visual form is chosen after the threshold because the threshold decides whether the panel is a state (a number that turns red) or a trend (a line with a band). The feedback arrow means that review can change the question itself: a panel may be rewritten, merged or deleted.

**Execution flow.** The steps are taken in order when a panel is created, and the loop is taken at each scheduled review, typically quarterly, or whenever an incident review shows that a panel misled someone.

**Decision logic.** The critical branch is hidden in the second box. If no audience or action can be named, the procedure stops and the panel is not built. A second branch sits in the fourth box: if a threshold can be defined precisely, the condition should also become a meta-alert or a correlation rule, so that the panel becomes a convenience rather than the only safeguard.

**Technical interpretation.** Underneath, every panel is a saved search with a time range, a refresh interval and a rendering. The procedure makes each of those parameters deliberate. It also documents the panel: anyone can read the question, the owner and the threshold and judge whether the panel still makes sense.

**Practical example.** At Banyan, the question "is a critical source silent?" is answered by the per-source `last_seen` query used at the start of every shift (Module 1, Lesson 4). The audience is the shift lead; the action is to open a ticket with the SIEM engineer. The baseline is "Sysmon from every workstation at least every 15 minutes during office hours", and the visual form is a table of sources with a red state when `last_seen` is older than the threshold. In the lab dataset, Sysmon's last event is at 06:14 while the proxy keeps sending until 12:32, which is exactly the gap this panel exists to show.

**Security implications.** A panel without a threshold or an alert behind it relies on someone happening to look. Attackers who stop or tamper with logging benefit directly from health panels that nobody owns.

**Key learning.** Build every panel from a question with an audience, an action and a threshold; if the threshold is precise, make it an alert too.

## What to show, and what not to show

The procedure removes most bad panels before they are built, but some patterns are tempting enough to deserve explicit treatment.

### Show

**Rates and ratios against a baseline.** "Authentication failures per hour, with the normal band for this hour shaded" tells the viewer whether now is unusual. A raw count tells them only that it is non-zero.

**Age, not just count.** Five open high-severity alerts is fine if they were raised ten minutes ago and alarming if the oldest is six hours old. Queue panels should show the age of the oldest item in each severity, and SLA panels should show time remaining.

**Top-N lists of entities.** The ten hosts with most outbound bytes, the ten users with most failed logons, the ten rarest destinations. Lists of named entities lead directly to a drilldown and an investigation, which is what an analyst needs.

**Coverage and health.** Last event per source, events per hour per source, parse-error counts and the fill rate of fields that rules depend on. Module 2 showed that a vendor format change can leave events arriving while `user.name` stops being extracted; volume stays normal and only a fill-rate panel reveals the break.

**Change.** New values that have never been seen before (a new admin account, a new country for a VPN user, a new parent process for an Office application) are more informative than totals.

### Do not show (or show only with great care)

**Vanity totals.** "Events ingested today", "threats blocked this month" and similar numbers look impressive in a management meeting but change no decision. If a number must appear for capacity planning, put it on the engineering dashboard, not the analyst one.

**Attack maps.** Animated world maps of "attacks" in real time are attractive and nearly useless. Source geography of internet noise says little about risk, geolocation of addresses is approximate, and the most dangerous activity (a valid account logging in through the VPN) does not appear as an "attack" at all.

**Single "threat level" gauges.** A gauge that summarises the SOC's view as green, amber or red invites false reassurance. It hides how it is calculated, and its green state is easily read as "we are safe".

**Cumulative and stacked charts that hide the small series.** A stacked area chart of events by source will show the proxy as a thick band and the VPN as an invisible line, yet the VPN carried the password spray. Pie charts with more than four or five slices have the same problem.

**Unnecessary personal data.** A panel that lists every user's browsing destinations on a wall screen exposes personal data to everyone in the room. Under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and ordinary good practice, show what the purpose requires (for example a host name and a count) and keep the detail behind a drilldown that requires access and is logged.

> [!TIP]
> When reviewing a dashboard, ask of each panel: "If this number doubled, or dropped to zero, what would I do?" If the honest answer is "nothing", the panel is decoration.

## Worked example: building Banyan panels with minisiem

The course lab's `LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py` loads the synthetic Banyan Microfinance day (`banyan_events.jsonl`, 340 events from four sources: Windows Security, Sysmon-style endpoint, proxy and VPN) into a SQLite table called `events`. Every panel in this section is one `search` query; a real SIEM would run the same logic on a schedule and draw the result. Work in your own copy of the kit, never in the original folder:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
```

`load` (re)creates the `events` table with normalised columns (`ts`, `source`, `event_id`, `host`, `user`, `src_ip`, `url`, `outcome`, `image`, `parent_image`, `bytes_out`, `target_user`) plus the raw JSON. `search` takes one SQL statement in double quotes and prints the result with ` | ` between columns. All timestamps are UTC; Banyan's office is in India, so add 5 hours 30 minutes for IST (03:00 UTC is 08:30 IST).

### Panel 1: the volume chart everybody builds first

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT substr(ts,12,2) AS hour_utc, COUNT(*) AS n FROM events GROUP BY hour_utc ORDER BY hour_utc"
hour_utc | n
03 | 28
04 | 65
05 | 34
06 | 47
07 | 38
08 | 33
09 | 26
10 | 25
11 | 32
12 | 11
13 | 1
```

`substr(ts,12,2)` takes characters 12 and 13 of the ISO timestamp (`2026-11-03T06:14:00Z` gives `06`), which is a simple hourly bucket. `GROUP BY` counts the events per bucket and `ORDER BY` sorts them by hour. Plotted as a bar chart, this is the classic "events over time" panel.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The chart shows how many events of all sources the Banyan SIEM received in each UTC hour of the lab day, exactly as returned by the query above. It is included to demonstrate a limit, not a technique: the hour in which the intrusion started does not stand out.

**Components.** There are eleven horizontal bars, one per hour bucket from **03 UTC** to **13 UTC**, each labelled with its count. Ten bars are in the neutral slate tone. The **06 UTC** bar, with 47 events, is orange because it is the hour of interest: it contains the phishing download at 06:12, PowerShell started by Word at 06:14, the first beacons to `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, the eight-user VPN password spray at 06:42 and the successful VPN logon as `gopal` at 06:46. The note under the chart states the takeaway.

**Relationships.** The bars are independent counts; nothing in the chart links one event to another. That is the core problem: correlation needs relationships (the same host, the same source address, an order in time) and a volume chart throws them away.

**Execution flow.** Read the chart as a day. Activity rises as staff log on (03 UTC, 08:30 IST), peaks at **04 UTC** with 65 events, when every workstation is starting applications and browsing, drops at 05, rises to 47 at 06, then declines through the afternoon to a single event at 13 UTC (a late VPN logon).

**Decision logic.** If an analyst used this panel to decide "is anything wrong?", the busiest hour, 04, would attract attention first, and it is entirely routine (it includes two genuine typo logon failures). The 06 bar is below the peak and well inside the day's range, so a threshold on total volume would not fire for it.

**Technical interpretation.** The intrusion adds only a few dozen events to a busy hour, and they come from several sources with very different normal volumes. Proxy events dominate the total (284 of 340), so a change in the small VPN source is swamped. Splitting by source helps, but still does not show what matters.

**Practical example.** A manager looking at this chart on a wall screen at 12:00 IST (06:30 UTC) would see a normal morning. By that time the finance workstation WS-104 was already beaconing out. The alerts that revealed it came from correlation rules R1, R2 and R6 (Lesson 4), not from any chart.

**Security implications.** Volume panels give false reassurance if they are treated as detection. Their legitimate use is capacity planning and gross health: a bar of zero for a working hour would mean the pipeline is broken.

**Key learning.** Volume shows activity, not threat; build panels that answer specific questions, and leave detection to rules.


Grouping by two columns gives one row per hour and source. Two things appear that the total hid. VPN activity exists in only two hours, and nine of its ten events fall in 06 UTC, which is unusual for a source that normally sees one logon at a time. And Sysmon disappears after 06 UTC: its last event is the PowerShell process at 06:14. Whether that is a collector failure, a host going offline, or simply how this synthetic dataset was generated, a health panel must raise the question rather than leave it to chance. Notice that the absence is visible only as a missing row; a chart that draws "no row" as nothing at all, rather than as an explicit zero, makes silence easy to miss.

### Panel 3: authentication failures per hour

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT substr(ts,12,2) AS hour_utc, COUNT(*) AS failures FROM events WHERE outcome='failure' GROUP BY hour_utc ORDER BY hour_utc"
hour_utc | failures
04 | 2
06 | 8
```

This panel answers a narrower question: "are failures unusual right now?" The two failures at 04 UTC are Windows logon failures by `ben` and `hema` (typos); the eight at 06 UTC are VPN failures for eight different users from one address within 49 seconds. A count per hour shows that 06 is unusual, but not why. The more useful panel for the same question is a top-N table: source address, number of distinct users failed, and whether a success followed. That is precisely the logic of correlation rule R2, which is the point: once a panel's question can be stated that precisely, it should be a rule.

### Panel 4: outbound data per host

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT host, COUNT(*) AS requests, SUM(bytes_out) AS total_out, MAX(bytes_out) AS max_out FROM events WHERE source='proxy' GROUP BY host ORDER BY total_out DESC"
host | requests | total_out | max_out
WS-104 | 49 | 48263095 | 48200000
WS-103 | 37 | 82933 | 3679
WS-106 | 32 | 78117 | 3880
WS-101 | 37 | 75339 | 3732
WS-105 | 33 | 71992 | 3825
WS-108 | 40 | 66685 | 3549
WS-102 | 28 | 64825 | 3991
WS-107 | 28 | 57130 | 3999
```

This is a top-N panel of entities, sorted by total bytes sent through the proxy. WS-104 sent about 48 MB; every other host sent under 100 KB. Showing `max_out` beside `total_out` matters: it reveals that almost all of WS-104's total is a single request of 48,200,000 bytes (the upload to `files-share-drop.example` at 08:12 UTC), rather than many medium ones. An analyst reading this panel has an entity to drill into. Compare that with a chart of total outbound bytes per hour, which would show one huge bar at 08 UTC and tell the viewer nothing about which host or destination caused it.

## Thresholds, baselines and time

**Static thresholds** ("more than 10 failures per hour") are easy to explain and easy to break: they are too sensitive at peak times and too lax at night. **Relative baselines** compare now with the same hour on previous days or with a rolling average and flag deviations. They adapt to the organisation's rhythm, but they learn whatever was happening during the baseline period, including an attacker who was already present. A practical compromise is a static floor for health ("at least one event per 15 minutes") and baselines for activity, reviewed by a person.

**Time ranges** must be chosen to fit the question. A queue panel covers "now"; an activity panel usually covers 24 hours with hourly buckets; a management trend covers weeks. Every panel should show its time range and time zone in its title. Mixing UTC panels with IST panels on one dashboard is a classic cause of confusion during incidents: 06:46 UTC is 12:16 IST, and an analyst who reads one as the other will place events in the wrong order. This course keeps everything in UTC and converts only for people outside the SOC.

**Refresh intervals** cost search capacity. A dashboard of twenty panels refreshing every 30 seconds on a wall screen, plus the same dashboard open in ten browsers, can consume more search capacity than the correlation rules. Refresh only as fast as the decision requires; health and queue panels may need a minute or two, trend panels an hour.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**Treating a dashboard as detection.** If an outcome matters enough to watch, write a rule for it. The Banyan intrusion hour is not visible in total volume.

**Showing counts without baselines or age.** "12 open alerts" means nothing without severity and age.

**Hiding zero.** Charts that drop empty buckets make a silent source look like a short gap, or invisible. Health panels must show explicit zeros and last-seen times.

**Trusting a panel whose inputs broke.** A dashboard built on `user` shows nothing wrong when the parser stops extracting `user`; it shows fewer users. Health of the inputs is part of every dashboard's meaning.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Dashboards are part of the attack surface and of the SOC's own risk.

**Data exposure.** A dashboard is a pre-built query over sensitive data. Role-based access must apply to dashboards as it does to search; a dashboard shared with the help desk must not expose security logs they could not search directly. Wall screens in shared spaces should show aggregates, not user names or URLs.

**Misleading by design or by accident.** A dashboard that shows green while a source is dead is worse than no dashboard, because it suppresses the questions people would otherwise ask. Attackers who clear logs (Banyan's event 1102 at 08:17 UTC) or stop agents benefit from health panels that are unowned or unalerted.

**Integrity of dashboards.** Changes to dashboards, saved searches and thresholds should be logged and reviewed like rule changes. Silently raising a threshold or deleting a panel can hide activity as effectively as deleting logs.

**Availability.** Heavy dashboards compete with correlation searches for capacity. A dashboard that times out returns nothing, and "nothing" can look like "no events". Monitor search failures and timeouts, and make panels show errors visibly.

**Reporting clocks.** CERT-In's directions of April 2022 require reportable incidents to be reported within six hours of being noticed. A queue panel that shows, for each confirmed incident, the time since it was noticed helps the shift lead keep that obligation in view; it does not replace the incident process (Module 8).

## Best practices

- Write the question, audience, action, threshold and owner in each panel's description.
- Group dashboards by job: queue, health, detection performance, investigation, activity, management.
- Prefer top-N tables of entities with drilldowns over decorative charts.
- Show age and SLA time remaining on queue panels.
- Show last-seen time, expected interval, fill rate and parse errors per source; back every health threshold with an alert.
- Keep one time zone (UTC) and state the time range in every panel title.
- Limit refresh rates to what decisions need; watch search load from dashboards.
- Apply access control and minimise personal data on shared screens.
- Version and review dashboard changes; retire panels nobody has acted on.

## Key takeaways

- A dashboard is a set of saved questions. Each panel needs a question, an audience, an action, a threshold and an owner.
- SOC dashboards do three jobs: manage the work, assure that the SOC can see, and help people understand activity. None of these jobs is detection.
- In the Banyan data the intrusion hour (06 UTC, 47 events) is below the day's peak (04 UTC, 65 events); volume charts show activity, not threat.
- Show rates against baselines, ages, top-N entities, health and change; avoid vanity totals, attack maps, threat gauges and charts that hide small series.
- Health panels need explicit zeros and last-seen times, and every precise health threshold should also be an alert.
- Dashboards expose data, consume search capacity and can mislead; control access, log changes and keep one time zone.
