## Introduction and conceptual foundation

An alert is a claim. When the rule `R1 office_spawns_script` fires, it claims that Microsoft Word started a script interpreter on a host. When `R2 vpn_spray_then_success` fires, it claims that one external address failed to log in as at least five different users and then succeeded as one of them. A claim can be wrong in three different ways: the events it relies on may not say what the alert summary suggests, the rule's logic may match something it was never meant to match, or the activity may be exactly what the rule describes and still be legitimate. **Validation** is the disciplined work of testing the claim against the evidence before anyone acts on it.

Validation follows enrichment (Lesson 3) and precedes the disposition and false-positive handling of Lesson 5 and the escalation decision of Lesson 6. Enrichment told you who and what the entities are and how much they matter. Validation asks a sharper question, the one the course library phrases as: *if this alert were real, what else would be true, and is it there?* An analyst who validates well escalates real incidents with the evidence already attached, and closes false alarms with a reason another analyst can check. An analyst who skips validation either escalates noise, which wastes the incident responders' time and erodes trust in the SOC, or closes real attacks because the summary looked familiar.

Validation is not a full investigation. It is time-boxed: many SOCs aim for a first decision within roughly 15 to 30 minutes, although the figure varies by organisation. Its output is a conclusion of one of three kinds, each with its evidence: **confirmed** (the activity happened and is malicious or unauthorised), **refuted** (it did not happen as claimed, or it is legitimate), or **undetermined** (the evidence available in the time box is not enough, and you record what is missing). All three are legitimate results; pretending to certainty you do not have is not.

> [!NOTE]
> Module 4 used the Banyan WS-104 events to teach how to *read* Windows, proxy and Sysmon logs. This lesson uses the same dataset for a different purpose: to test specific alerts. The emphasis is on method, so that you can apply it to any rule in any SIEM.

All queries in this lesson were run with the course lab kit's teaching SIEM, `LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py`, in a temporary copy of the kit, against its synthetic dataset `banyan_events.jsonl`. Every output shown is exactly what was printed.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Validation | Testing whether an alert reflects real activity of the kind the rule describes, using the underlying evidence. |
| Claim | What the alert asserts happened; the hypothesis validation tests. |
| Raw event | The original record as received from the source, before the SIEM renamed, dropped or summarised fields. |
| Normalised view | The SIEM's common-schema columns; convenient, but may omit fields the source sent. |
| Rule reproduction | Re-running the rule's own logic by hand to see exactly which events it matched and why. |
| Corroboration | Independent evidence, ideally from a different source, that is consistent with the claim. |
| Refutation | Evidence that the claim is false or that the activity is legitimate. |
| Pivot | Moving from one entity (host, user, address, time) to all other events that share it. |
| Time window | The period around the alert searched for related events, for example 15 minutes before and after. |
| Undetermined | A conclusion that the available evidence neither confirms nor refutes the claim. |

## The three questions of validation

Every validation, whatever the rule, answers three questions in order.

**1. Did the recorded events happen as the alert summary implies?** Read the events themselves, not the alert title. Check the host, the user, the time and its time zone, and the exact field values. A rule titled "Office spawns script" that fired on a process whose parent was a different Office application, or on an event from a test host, is already a different story.

**2. Does the rule's logic mean what its name says?** Reproduce the rule. Look at its conditions, thresholds, joins and time windows, and check which events it actually matched. Rules are written by people and tested against a handful of examples; edge cases are where they fail. A correlation rule with no time limit, a threshold that counts the user's own attempt, a pattern that matches a substring in the wrong field: each of these produces alerts that are logically correct and practically misleading.

**3. Is the activity malicious or unauthorised?** Look for corroboration across sources and for refutation. If Word really started a hidden PowerShell, what else would be true if it were an attack? A document arriving shortly before, network connections shortly after, perhaps account or persistence changes later. If it were legitimate, what would be true instead? A known macro, a signed script, a change ticket, an application owner who can explain it.

The first two questions catch false positives: alerts where the rule matched something it should not have. The third separates true positives from benign true positives: alerts where the rule worked exactly as designed and the activity was authorised. Lesson 5 turns these outcomes into formal dispositions.

## The validation workflow

The three questions become a short, repeatable workflow. It starts by writing down the claim precisely, because a vague claim cannot be tested. It then reproduces the rule, reads the raw event, pivots across sources around the alert's entities and time, deliberately looks for refuting evidence, and ends with a conclusion and the evidence for it. When a pivot reveals a new entity, such as a new domain or a second host, the workflow starts again for that entity.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the validation workflow as six steps run from top to bottom, with a side note that restarts it. It matters because validation done in a different order tends to fail in predictable ways: starting with the pivot before reading the raw event, for example, builds a story on a field that may have been mis-parsed.

**Components.** The first box, slate, is **State the claim**, with the `R1` example "Word started PowerShell on WS-104". The second, violet, is **Reproduce the rule logic**: re-run the rule query and check its fields, thresholds and joins. The third, blue, is **Read the raw event**, for the fields the normalised view dropped. The fourth, orange, is **Pivot across sources**: the same host, user or address within a time window. The fifth, green, is **Seek refuting evidence**: an approved script, a change ticket, the owner's explanation. The last, dark, is **Conclude**: confirmed, refuted or undetermined, each with its evidence. To the right of the last box, a short side note reads *new entity found? it becomes a new claim: restart at step 1*.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each downward arrow means "only once the previous step is done". The side note means that a conclusion can produce a new claim: the pivot for `R1` reveals `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, which becomes its own claim to validate.

**Execution flow.** Claim, reproduce, raw, pivot, refute, conclude, and restart when the evidence names something new. Steps 2 and 3 are about the alert itself; steps 4 and 5 look outside it.

**Decision logic.** The workflow has an exit at every step. If step 2 shows the rule matched the wrong thing, the alert is a false positive and the later steps are unnecessary. If step 3 shows the event came from a test host or an excluded process, the same applies. If step 5 finds a valid change ticket, the conclusion is benign. Only alerts that survive every test reach "confirmed".

**Technical interpretation.** In `minisiem.py` the rules are SQL statements in the `RULES` dictionary, so step 2 is literally re-running that SQL, or a variant of it, with `minisiem.py search`. The raw event is kept in the `raw` column next to the normalised columns, so step 3 is a query that selects `raw`.

**Practical example.** For `R1` on `WS-104`: the claim is stated; the rule query returns one event at `06:14Z`; the raw event shows a hidden, encoded PowerShell command line that the normalised columns do not hold; the pivot finds the `.docm` download two minutes earlier and the first request to a rare domain three minutes later; no change ticket or approved macro explains it; the conclusion is confirmed.

**Security implications.** Skipping step 5 is the commonest validation failure: analysts look only for evidence that confirms. Deliberately seeking refutation protects against both wrongful escalation and confirmation bias.

**Key learning.** Validate the claim, not the title: reproduce the rule, read the raw event, corroborate across sources, try to refute, then conclude with evidence.

## Worked validation 1: R1 on WS-104

Start from a fresh copy of the lab kit and load the dataset:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python minisiem.py rules
R1 office_spawns_script: 1 hit(s)
    ('2026-11-03T06:14:00Z', 'WS-104', 'divya', 'C:\\Windows\\System32\\WindowsPowerShell\\v1.0\\powershell.exe')
R2 vpn_spray_then_success: 1 hit(s)
    ('203.0.113.66', 8, 'gopal', '2026-11-03T06:46:00Z')
R3 new_local_admin: 2 hit(s)
    ('2026-11-03T07:02:00Z', 'WS-104', 'divya', 'svc_update')
    ('2026-11-03T07:03:00Z', 'WS-104', 'divya', 'svc_update')
R4 large_upload: 1 hit(s)
    ('2026-11-03T08:12:00Z', 'WS-104', 'divya', 'https://files-share-drop.example/upload', 48200000)
R5 log_cleared: 1 hit(s)
    ('2026-11-03T08:17:00Z', 'WS-104', 'divya')
R6 beacon_regular_small: 1 hit(s)
    ('WS-104', 'https://cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping', 18, 220, 220)
```

`load` rebuilds the database from the dataset; `rules` runs every rule in the `RULES` dictionary and prints the hit count and up to three hits for each.

### Step 1: state the claim

`R1` claims: on `WS-104`, under the account `divya`, at `06:14Z` (11:44 IST), a process whose parent image contains `winword.exe` started PowerShell or cmd.

### Step 2: reproduce the rule and read the raw event

The rule's SQL in `minisiem.py` selects Sysmon process-creation events (`event_id=1`) whose lower-cased `parent_image` contains `winword.exe` and whose `image` contains `powershell.exe` or `cmd.exe`. Re-running its core condition and asking for the `raw` column shows exactly which record it matched:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT raw FROM events WHERE event_id=1 AND host='WS-104' AND lower(parent_image) LIKE '%winword%'"
raw
{"@timestamp": "2026-11-03T06:14:00Z", "command_line": "powershell.exe -NoP -W Hidden -EncodedCommand <redacted-for-course>", "event_id": 1, "host": "WS-104", "image": "C:\\Windows\\System32\\WindowsPowerShell\\v1.0\\powershell.exe", "parent_image": "C:\\Program Files\\Microsoft Office\\root\\Office16\\WINWORD.EXE", "source": "sysmon", "user": "divya"}
```

Three things are now verified. The parent really is `WINWORD.EXE` from the Office installation path, not a different program whose name merely contains the string. The child really is Windows PowerShell. And the raw event holds a field that the normalised view does not: `command_line`. `minisiem.py` keeps only twelve common fields as columns, and `command_line` is not one of them, so an analyst who looked only at the columns would never see it. The command line asks PowerShell to skip the user profile (`-NoP`), run with a hidden window (`-W Hidden`) and execute a Base64-encoded script (`-EncodedCommand`; the course redacts the payload). Legitimate automation sometimes uses each of these options, but a Word document launching a hidden, encoded script is a well-known malicious pattern, which is why the kit's Sigma rule `rk_office_spawns_shell.yml` maps it to ATT&CK T1204.002 (user execution of a malicious file) and T1059.001 (PowerShell).

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Always read the raw event behind an alert. The normalised view is built for searching across sources, and it drops whatever its schema does not include. In this dataset the most important field of the `R1` event, and the `country` field of every VPN event, exist only in `raw`.

### Step 3: pivot across sources

If the alert were a real malicious document, a document should have arrived shortly before and the host should have done something new on the network shortly after. Pivot on the host and a narrow window around the alert:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, source, event_id, url, image FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND ts BETWEEN '2026-11-03T06:07' AND '2026-11-03T06:20' ORDER BY ts"
ts | source | event_id | url | image
2026-11-03T06:12:00Z | proxy | None | https://banyan-payroll-update.example/invoice.docm | None
2026-11-03T06:14:00Z | sysmon | 1 | None | C:\Windows\System32\WindowsPowerShell\v1.0\powershell.exe
2026-11-03T06:17:00Z | proxy | None | https://cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping | None
```

Two minutes before the PowerShell start, the proxy recorded the download of a macro-enabled document, `invoice.docm`, from a look-alike of the company's own name. Three minutes after it, the host made its first request to a domain no other host contacts. These are **independent sources**: the proxy knows nothing about Sysmon, and Sysmon nothing about the proxy. When two sources that cannot influence each other tell a consistent story, the story is much stronger than either alone.

The new domain is a new entity, so the workflow restarts for it, as the side note in the figure says. Validate the `R6` beacon claim with one aggregate query:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT COUNT(*) AS hits, MIN(ts) AS first, MAX(ts) AS last, MIN(bytes_out) AS min_b, MAX(bytes_out) AS max_b FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND url LIKE '%cdn-telemetry-sync.example%'"
hits | first | last | min_b | max_b
18 | 2026-11-03T06:17:00Z | 2026-11-03T07:42:02Z | 220 | 220
```

Eighteen requests between `06:17:00Z` and `07:42:02Z` is one every five minutes, each exactly 220 bytes. Listing the individual timestamps shows the spacing never varies by more than a few seconds (`06:17:00`, `06:22:01`, `06:27:03`, `06:32:03` and so on). That fixed interval and fixed size is what an automated check-in looks like; a human browsing does not produce it. Enrichment in Lesson 3 added that the domain is a high-confidence ISAC indicator and is seen by one host only.

### Step 4: seek refuting evidence

What would make this benign? A finance macro approved by the application owner, a signed script, a change ticket, or an IT automation that uses this pattern. The Sigma rule itself lists "legitimate macros that launch scripts" as its known false positive. The checks are: the organisation's list of approved macros and add-ins, the change calendar, and, where policy allows, the user or the application owner through a known channel. In the lab none of these exists, and the document came from an outside look-alike domain rather than an internal share.

### Step 5: conclude

**Confirmed**, with evidence: the raw Sysmon event (hidden, encoded PowerShell from Word), the preceding `.docm` download from a look-alike domain, and the regular 220-byte requests to a rare, intelligence-listed domain beginning three minutes later. Each item is cited by source and timestamp, so the next analyst can re-run every query.

## Worked validation 2: R2, reproducing correlation logic

`R2` is a correlation rule, and correlation rules hide more logic than single-event rules. Its claim, from the hit `('203.0.113.66', 8, 'gopal', '2026-11-03T06:46:00Z')`, is: the address `203.0.113.66` failed VPN logins for eight distinct users and then succeeded as `gopal` at `06:46Z`.

### Reproduce the rule

The rule joins each successful VPN login (`s`) to every failed VPN login (`f`) from the same address at or before the success, counts distinct failed users, and keeps groups with at least five. Reproduce the join without the grouping to see exactly which failures were counted:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT f.user, f.ts FROM events s JOIN events f ON f.src_ip = s.src_ip WHERE s.source='vpn' AND s.outcome='success' AND s.src_ip='203.0.113.66' AND f.source='vpn' AND f.outcome='failure' AND f.ts <= s.ts ORDER BY f.ts"
user | ts
asha | 2026-11-03T06:42:00Z
ben | 2026-11-03T06:42:07Z
chen | 2026-11-03T06:42:14Z
divya | 2026-11-03T06:42:21Z
eshan | 2026-11-03T06:42:28Z
farah | 2026-11-03T06:42:35Z
gopal | 2026-11-03T06:42:42Z
hema | 2026-11-03T06:42:49Z
```

Reading the rule this way exposes two properties of its logic. First, the count of eight **includes `gopal` himself**: his own failure at `06:42:42Z` is one of the eight. That is reasonable for spraying, but the analyst should know it. Second, the rule has **no time window**: `f.ts <= s.ts` accepts a failure from any earlier time, so a scattering of forgotten-password failures over weeks could, in a longer dataset, add up to five and fire the rule. Here the evidence removes that doubt: all eight failures fall within 49 seconds, exactly seven seconds apart, in alphabetical order of username. That regularity is the signature of automation, not of eight colleagues mistyping.

It is also worth checking the rule's negative case. A variant with a `LEFT JOIN` lists every VPN success with its failure count:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT s.src_ip, s.user, s.ts, COUNT(DISTINCT f.user) AS users_failed FROM events s LEFT JOIN events f ON f.src_ip = s.src_ip AND f.source='vpn' AND f.outcome='failure' AND f.ts <= s.ts WHERE s.source='vpn' AND s.outcome='success' GROUP BY s.src_ip, s.user, s.ts"
src_ip | user | ts | users_failed
198.51.100.104 | gopal | 2026-11-03T13:33:00Z | 0
203.0.113.66 | gopal | 2026-11-03T06:46:00Z | 8
```

The routine evening session has zero preceding failures and correctly did not fire. The rule separates the two cases cleanly on this data.

### Read the raw events and pivot on the user

The raw VPN events hold the synthetic `country` field, which the normalised columns lack:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, user, outcome, json_extract(raw, '$.country') AS country FROM events WHERE src_ip='203.0.113.66' ORDER BY ts"
ts | user | outcome | country
2026-11-03T06:42:00Z | asha | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:07Z | ben | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:14Z | chen | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:21Z | divya | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:28Z | eshan | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:35Z | farah | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:42Z | gopal | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:42:49Z | hema | failure | ??
2026-11-03T06:46:00Z | gopal | success | ??
```

`json_extract(raw, '$.country')` is SQLite's built-in JSON function; it reads one key from the stored raw event. Every event from this address has an unknown location, unlike `gopal`'s routine session (`IN`). Now pivot on the user around the success:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT ts, source, user, url FROM events WHERE user='gopal' AND ts BETWEEN '2026-11-03T05:30' AND '2026-11-03T07:30' ORDER BY ts"
ts | source | user | url
2026-11-03T05:42:00Z | proxy | gopal | https://news.example.com/
2026-11-03T05:54:00Z | proxy | gopal | https://crm.banyan.example/
2026-11-03T06:42:42Z | vpn | gopal | None
2026-11-03T06:46:00Z | vpn | gopal | None
2026-11-03T07:22:00Z | proxy | gopal | https://intranet.banyan.example/
```

`gopal` was browsing through the office proxy from his workstation `WS-107` (internal address `10.40.0.26`) before and after the VPN session. An employee working at an office desk does not normally need a VPN session from an external address of unknown location at 12:16 IST. This is strong corroboration that the VPN success used `gopal`'s credentials without `gopal`. It is not proof: he might have connected from a personal device for a reason. That is precisely the refutation question to put to him through a verified channel, and the credentials should be treated as at risk until it is answered. Module 2 also showed a firewall record, from the kit's `normalise_demo.py` sample, of the same address being dropped on TCP 3389 towards an internal host at `06:46:21Z`; if your firewall logs are in the SIEM, that is one more independent source to check.

**Conclusion: confirmed** password spraying with a successful login as `gopal` from `203.0.113.66`; whether this is linked to the WS-104 phishing is a **hypothesis**, recorded as such.

The axis beneath gives the time range.

**Relationships.** There are no arrows: the relationships are positions in time. The first three lanes share one host, `WS-104`; the fourth shares nothing with them except the time window.

**Execution flow.** Read left to right: download, script start and first beacon within five minutes; spraying and the VPN success in the middle of the beacon series; account changes while beaconing continues; the upload and the log clearing at the end.

**Decision logic.** Corroboration is strongest where lanes from different sources cluster on the same host within minutes, as at 06:12 to 06:17. Clusters across different hosts, such as the VPN lane, are consistent in time but do not share an entity, so they support a hypothesis, not a link.

**Technical interpretation.** Each tick is one row returned by a `minisiem.py search`. The regularity of the proxy beacons, set against the irregular single events in the other lanes, is visible at a glance; it is what `R6` tries to capture with its count and byte-range test.

**Practical example.** An analyst validating `R4` (large upload) alone might accept a plausible business explanation. Seen on this timeline, the upload follows a malicious document, a beacon and a new administrator account on the same host, and precedes the clearing of the log that would have recorded more.

**Security implications.** Attackers can forge or suppress one source; consistent records across several are much harder to fake. Conversely, a gap in one lane may mean missing telemetry, not missing activity.

**Key learning.** Confirm with independent sources; treat time-aligned events without a shared entity as a hypothesis to test.

## Validation pitfalls in the evidence itself

Validation can fail because the evidence-gathering is flawed. Four traps recur.

**Filters that silently drop evidence.** Modules 2 and 3 showed that in SQL a comparison with a missing (NULL) value is neither true nor false. An analyst looking for all non-beacon activity on `WS-104` with `NOT (url LIKE '%ping%')` gets a different answer from one who allows for missing URLs:

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT COUNT(*) FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND NOT (url LIKE '%ping%')"
COUNT(*)
31
$ python minisiem.py search "SELECT COUNT(*) FROM events WHERE host='WS-104' AND (url IS NULL OR url NOT LIKE '%ping%')"
COUNT(*)
40
```

The nine missing events are the Windows and Sysmon records, which have no URL, including the `06:14Z` PowerShell start itself. A validation that concludes "nothing else happened on the host" from the first query is wrong. Before you write "no further activity", make sure your filter could not have excluded it.

**Time zones and clocks.** The dataset is in UTC; Banyan's staff work in IST, five and a half hours ahead. A 06:14 event is mid-morning in the office, not dawn. When sources disagree on time zone, or a host's clock drifts, events that belong together appear minutes or hours apart, and an analyst can wrongly reject corroboration. Normalise to UTC and note any source whose clock you could not verify.

**Counting one action twice.** `R3 new_local_admin` returns two hits, `4720` at `07:02Z` and `4732` at `07:03Z`. Reading the raw events shows one story: the account `svc_update` was created, then added to the `Administrators` group a minute later. Validate it as one action, not two incidents.

**Rule logic that is broader than its name.** `R6 beacon_regular_small` checks only that a host made ten or more requests to one URL with less than 50 bytes of variation in size; it never looks at the intervals. A dashboard that refreshes many times a day could satisfy it. The validation step that makes it meaningful is the one done above: checking that the timestamps are evenly spaced.

## Validation with people

Some alerts can only be refuted by a person: the user who ran a script, the administrator who created an account, the application owner who knows that a macro is approved. Asking is a legitimate and often decisive validation step, provided it is done safely:

- **Use a verified channel.** Contact the person through a known phone number or the service desk, never by replying to the suspicious email or message, and never through the account you suspect is compromised. If `gopal`'s credentials are in an attacker's hands, a chat message to `gopal` may be answered by the attacker.
- **Ask neutral, specific questions.** "Did you connect to the VPN at about 12:16 today, and from which device?" is better than "Did you get hacked?"
- **Consider insider risk.** If the activity could be deliberate misuse by the person you would ask, do not alert them; escalate according to the playbook so that HR and legal can be involved.
- **Record the answer as evidence**, with who said what and when, and corroborate it where you can. A user's statement is evidence, not proof.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Validating the title, not the event.** The alert name is a summary written by a rule author; the raw event is the evidence.
- **Looking only for confirming evidence.** Always ask what would be true if the alert were benign, and check for it.
- **Treating time alignment as a link.** The VPN spray and the WS-104 activity overlap in time but share no entity. That is a hypothesis.
- **Trusting the normalised view.** In this dataset the decisive `command_line` and `country` fields exist only in `raw`.
- **Concluding "nothing happened" from an empty result.** Check that your query, your time window and your telemetry could have shown it.
- **Spending the whole shift on one alert.** Validation is time-boxed. When the box runs out, record "undetermined", list what is missing and escalate or hand over.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Validation is where the SOC's credibility is made. Confirmed alerts escalated with their evidence get fast action from incident responders; noisy escalations teach them to ignore the SOC. Refuted alerts closed with a reason feed detection engineering (Module 7): if `R2` ever fires on scattered failures because it has no time window, the validation notes are the evidence for adding one.

Validation also has security consequences of its own. The analyst should work from the SIEM's copy of the evidence and not log in to the affected host to "have a look", which changes its state and may alert the attacker; host-level collection belongs to incident response and evidence preservation (Module 8). Queries that pivot widely across user activity touch personal data, so they should be limited to what the alert justifies and recorded in the case. And an attacker who knows how the SOC validates may plant evidence that refutes an alert, such as a plausible-looking change ticket, so refuting evidence should be checked at its source as carefully as confirming evidence.

## Best practices

1. Write the claim down in one sentence before querying anything.
2. Reproduce the rule's logic and read the raw event behind every hit.
3. Corroborate with at least one independent source, and record each item by source and timestamp.
4. Deliberately look for refuting evidence: approved activity, change tickets, owners' explanations.
5. Check your own queries for silent exclusions, time-zone errors and double counting.
6. Contact people only through verified channels, and consider insider risk first.
7. Conclude confirmed, refuted or undetermined, with the evidence, inside the time box.
8. Feed rule weaknesses found during validation back to detection engineering.

## Key takeaways

- Validation tests an alert's claim against the evidence: did the events happen as stated, does the rule's logic mean what its name says, and is the activity malicious or authorised?
- The workflow is: state the claim, reproduce the rule, read the raw event, pivot across sources, seek refutation, conclude, and loop when new entities appear.
- The raw event often holds what the normalised view drops; in the Banyan data, the decisive PowerShell command line and the VPN country exist only in `raw`.
- Corroboration from independent sources turns "suspicious" into "confirmed"; time alignment without a shared entity remains a hypothesis.
- Reproducing correlation logic exposes hidden assumptions, such as `R2` counting the user's own failure and having no time window.
- Confirmed, refuted and undetermined are all valid outcomes when recorded with evidence inside the time box.
