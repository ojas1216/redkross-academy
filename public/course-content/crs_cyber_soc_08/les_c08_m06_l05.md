## Introduction and conceptual foundation

A threat intelligence feed is a stream of indicators, and sometimes of richer objects such as reports and relationships, that a producer publishes on a schedule so that consumers can load them into their tools. To a SOC, a feed looks like a simple promise: subscribe, import, and your SIEM will start telling you when known-bad infrastructure appears in your logs. In practice the promise is only kept by teams that manage their feeds as carefully as they manage their detection rules. A feed that is imported blindly does three kinds of damage. It floods the queue with matches on addresses that were bad two years ago and now belong to someone else. It hides the few indicators that matter among thousands that do not. And, when it drives automatic blocking, it can take down a business service because a content delivery network address was once listed next to a phishing page.

Lessons 1 to 4 covered indicator types, confidence, enrichment and MISP. This lesson is about the inbound side of the platform: **where feeds come from, what formats they arrive in, how to judge whether a feed is worth having, and the four routine operations that keep a feed useful: normalisation and deduplication, allowlisting, scoring, and ageing out.**

The organising idea is the one the intelligence cycle starts with: *requirements first*. A SOC should be able to say, for every feed it consumes, which question that feed helps answer. "Which infrastructure is being used against finance-sector users in India this quarter?" is a requirement; "more indicators are better" is not. A feed with no requirement behind it is noise with a licence fee.

> [!NOTE]
> The feed in this course's lab kit (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/ioc_match.py`) is a small fictional list whose fields mirror STIX ideas (type, value, confidence, valid_until, source, context, TLP). It is a teaching structure, not STIX JSON, and its five entries deliberately come from the four kinds of source this lesson describes.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Feed | A regularly updated, machine-readable publication of indicators or intelligence objects from one producer. |
| Open feed | A feed published free of charge, often by researchers or community projects, usually with little context per indicator. |
| Commercial feed | A paid feed from a security vendor, typically with curation, context, confidence scores and a support contract. |
| Community / ISAC feed | Intelligence shared among members of a trust community such as a sector Information Sharing and Analysis Center, usually under TLP. |
| Internal intelligence | Indicators produced by your own SOC from its own incidents and investigations. |
| STIX 2.1 | Structured Threat Information Expression, an OASIS standard JSON format for intelligence objects such as indicators, malware and relationships. |
| TAXII | Trusted Automated Exchange of Intelligence Information, the companion protocol for transporting STIX content over HTTPS. |
| Normalisation | Converting indicator values to one canonical form so that the same indicator from two feeds compares as equal. |
| Deduplication | Merging records that describe the same indicator into one, while keeping every source and sighting. |
| Allowlist | A curated list of values (own domains, critical services, shared infrastructure) that must never raise alerts or be blocked on indicator evidence alone. |
| Ageing out | Lowering the weight of, and finally retiring, an indicator as it gets older or passes its `valid_until` time. |
| Hit rate | The share of a feed's indicators that ever match your telemetry; a measure of relevance, not of quality on its own. |

## Where feeds come from

Every feed is shaped by who produces it and why. Knowing the producer's incentives tells you what to expect from the content.

### Open feeds

Open feeds are published free by researchers, non-profit projects and community volunteers. Typical content is a list of addresses, domains, URLs or file hashes associated with a category of activity: scanning, botnet command and control, phishing pages, malware distribution. Their strengths are cost, breadth and speed; their weaknesses follow from how they are produced. Many open lists are generated automatically from honeypots or sandboxes, so an entry means "this address did something we classify as bad, at some point" and little more. Context is thin, confidence is rarely stated, and entries are not always removed when the address is reassigned. An open-feed entry should normally be treated as a lead for enrichment, not a reason to block.

### Commercial feeds

Commercial feeds are sold by security vendors, either on their own or bundled with a product such as an EDR, firewall or threat intelligence platform. You are paying for curation: analysts who vet indicators, attach context (malware family, campaign, first and last seen), assign confidence and expire entries. Quality varies by vendor, and the contract matters as much as the data: licensing may forbid sharing the indicators onward. Commercial feeds also overlap heavily with open sources; part of evaluating one is measuring how much of it you already have.

### Community and ISAC feeds

Information Sharing and Analysis Centers and similar trust communities exist so that organisations facing the same threats can warn each other. Their content is often the most relevant you will receive, because it was seen against organisations like yours, and it usually carries context written by an analyst who handled the incident. It also carries obligations: community intelligence is normally shared under the Traffic Light Protocol, and breaking a TLP:AMBER restriction can cost you your membership as well as tip off the adversary. In India, CERT-In advisories play a related role.

### Internal intelligence

The best source of indicators for your organisation is often your own SOC. Every confirmed incident produces infrastructure, file hashes and behaviours that were proven to be used against you. Internal intelligence has the highest relevance and, if the investigation was sound, high confidence. Its weakness is coverage: it only contains what you have already seen. Record it in the same platform and fields as external feeds, so it is matched, aged and shared the same way.

## Formats: from CSV lists to STIX and TAXII

Feeds arrive in a few recurring formats. The format decides how much context survives the journey from producer to your SIEM.

### Plain lists and CSV

The simplest feeds are text files with one value per line, or CSV files with a few columns such as value, type, first seen and a category. They are trivial to parse and to load into a lookup table, which is why so many open feeds use them. Their limitation is that each consumer must guess the meaning of the columns, there is no standard way to express confidence, validity or relationships, and a value with no type column is ambiguous (is `example.com` a domain or part of a URL?).

### STIX 2.1

STIX 2.1 (Structured Threat Information Expression), an OASIS Standard since 10 June 2021, defines a set of JSON objects for exchanging intelligence. Domain objects include `indicator`, `malware`, `threat-actor`, `intrusion-set`, `campaign`, `attack-pattern`, `infrastructure`, `vulnerability` and `report`; relationship objects connect them, for example "indicator *indicates* malware". For a SOC the most important object is the indicator, because it carries a detection pattern and the properties that a feed-management process needs. A minimal indicator for the ISAC domain in the lab feed might look like this (the identifier is a placeholder UUID):

```json
{
  "type": "indicator",
  "spec_version": "2.1",
  "id": "indicator--00000000-0000-4000-8000-000000000001",
  "created": "2026-11-01T09:00:00.000Z",
  "modified": "2026-11-01T09:00:00.000Z",
  "name": "Loader C2 domain (finance-sector phishing)",
  "pattern": "[domain-name:value = 'cdn-telemetry-sync.example']",
  "pattern_type": "stix",
  "valid_from": "2026-11-01T09:00:00Z",
  "valid_until": "2026-12-31T23:59:59Z",
  "confidence": 85
}
```

Read it field by field. `type`, `spec_version` and `id` identify the object; an id is the object type, two hyphens and a UUID, and it stays the same across updates so that a consumer can recognise a revised version of the same indicator. `created` and `modified` record when the producer created and last changed it. `pattern` is the detection logic, written in the STIX patterning language and announced by `pattern_type`; here it says "a domain-name object whose value equals this string". `valid_from` and `valid_until` bound the period in which the producer considers the indicator meaningful, which is exactly the information ageing out needs. `confidence` is an integer from 0 to 100. The lab feed's value, confidence and valid_until fields each have a STIX home here.

### TAXII

TAXII is the protocol for moving STIX content between systems over HTTPS. A TAXII server publishes a discovery endpoint, one or more API roots, and within them **collections**: named sets of objects that a client can be granted permission to read or write. A consumer polls a collection and asks for objects added after the time of its last request, so that it only downloads what changed. TAXII therefore answers "how do I get the feed, securely and incrementally?", while STIX answers "what does each item mean?". Many ISACs and commercial providers offer TAXII collections, and threat intelligence platforms such as MISP and OpenCTI can act as TAXII clients, servers, or both.

> [!TIP]
> When a provider offers both CSV and STIX/TAXII, prefer STIX/TAXII even if you only use the indicators today. The validity window, confidence and relationships it carries are exactly the fields your ageing and scoring logic will need later, and they are lost the moment the data is flattened into a list.

## The feed-management pipeline

Between a feed's arrival and an analyst seeing a match, every indicator should pass through the same sequence of operations. Platforms name the stages differently, but the stages are the same.

1. **Collect** from each source on its own schedule: TAXII polling, a downloaded CSV, an ISAC portal export, your own case notes.
2. **Normalise** values and field names into one internal schema.
3. **Deduplicate and merge** records that describe the same indicator.
4. **Filter against the allowlist**, so that your own infrastructure and known shared services never become alerts or blocks.
5. **Score** each indicator from its confidence, its source's reliability and its age.
6. **Age out** indicators past their validity, keeping them for history but removing them from action.
7. **Distribute** by score: high scores to detection and, with change control, to blocking; lower scores to enrichment and hunting only.
8. **Feed back** what happened: false matches to the producer, confirmed sightings to your community.

**Components.** The four sources are **Open feeds** ("free, thin context"), **Commercial feeds** ("curated, licensed"), **ISAC / community** ("relevant, under TLP") and **Internal cases** ("proven against us"), all in slate because they are external inputs to the process. They feed the processing chain inside the threat intelligence platform: **Normalise** ("canonical type + value"), **Deduplicate and merge** ("one record, all sources"), **Allowlist filter** ("own and shared infrastructure", in green because it protects the business), **Score** ("confidence × reliability × age", in orange as the key judgement) and **Age out** ("past valid_until: history only"), in violet because it is a policy decision rather than a data transformation. The two destinations close the chain: **Detect / block** in dark, for high-score current indicators, and **Enrich / hunt** in blue, for everything else that is still worth knowing.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each source arrow points into Normalise: all sources, whatever their format, enter one funnel. The chain then runs stage by stage, each arrow meaning "the cleaned output of this stage becomes the input of the next". After Age out the flow splits, with the arrows labelled "high score, current" and "low score or expired". The dashed arrow labelled "false matches, sightings" runs from Enrich / hunt back to ISAC / community; it is the feedback path to producers.

**Execution flow.** Indicators are normalised before they are compared, because deduplication on raw strings misses duplicates that differ only in case or formatting. The allowlist is applied before scoring so that a high-confidence entry for your own mail domain can never reach the blocking path. Ageing is applied last, at match time, because age changes every day even when the feed does not.

**Decision logic.** The visible branch is the split at the end: only current, high-scoring indicators go to detection and blocking; expired, low-confidence or context-free indicators go to enrichment and hunting, where a human reads them in context. The allowlist filter is an implicit branch: matching entries leave the action path entirely, but are logged for review.

**Technical interpretation.** In a platform such as MISP or OpenCTI these stages correspond to feed connectors, warning lists, decay or scoring models, and export rules that publish selected attributes to the SIEM. In the lab kit the stages are compressed: the feed is a hand-curated list, scoring is the stated confidence, and ageing is the comparison of `valid_until` with the fixed `AS_OF` date.

**Practical example.** The ISAC domain `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` (confidence 85, valid until 31 December 2026) passes every stage and is fit for detection and, after review, blocking. The open-feed address `198.51.100.104` (confidence 40, valid until 30 June 2026) is already past its validity on the lab date of 3 November 2026, so it may only be used for history and enrichment.

**Security implications.** The pipeline is also a control against poisoning: an adversary or a careless producer who inserts a popular domain into a feed is stopped by the allowlist, and an indicator that is never corroborated decays instead of living forever.

**Key learning.** Never connect a feed straight to a blocklist; every indicator must be normalised, deduplicated, allowlist-checked, scored and aged before it is allowed to act.

## Judging a feed: quality criteria

Before subscribing, and at every renewal, evaluate a feed against criteria you can measure, not against the size of its indicator count.

| Criterion | Question to ask | How to measure it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | Does it describe threats to organisations like ours? | Hit rate against your telemetry; overlap with your own incidents. |
| Timeliness | How long after activity does an indicator appear? | Compare first-seen times with your own sightings and with other feeds. |
| Accuracy | How often is a match a false positive? | Share of matches closed as false positives over a review period. |
| Context | Does each entry say what it was seen doing, and when? | Proportion of entries with category, first/last seen and confidence. |
| Uniqueness | Does it add indicators we do not already have? | Overlap analysis against existing feeds after normalisation. |
| Ingestibility | Can we load it reliably without manual work? | Format (STIX/TAXII, CSV), stability of the schema, parsing errors. |
| Terms | What may we do with the data? | Licence and TLP: can it be shared, sent to cloud tools, used in reports? |

Two of these deserve comment. **Hit rate** is often misread. A feed with a very high hit rate may simply contain popular shared infrastructure; a feed with zero hits may be excellent intelligence about threats that have not reached you yet. Hit rate tells you a feed is relevant to your traffic, and the false-positive share of those hits tells you whether the relevance is useful. **Uniqueness** matters because feeds copy each other. Know how much of a paid feed you already have before you renew it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Record every evaluation: date, feed, the measurements above and the decision. A feed review that is not written down cannot be repeated next year, and a SOC that cannot say why it consumes a feed cannot defend the cost or the false positives it produces.

## Normalisation and deduplication

The same indicator reaches you in many spellings. One feed lists `CDN-Telemetry-Sync.example.` with a trailing dot, another `cdn-telemetry-sync[.]example` in "defanged" form so that it cannot be clicked by accident, a third embeds it in a URL. Unless they are reduced to one canonical form, one real connection may raise three alerts that look like independent confirmations.

Normalisation rules are simple and should be applied in code, once, at ingestion:

- **Domains:** convert to lower case, remove a trailing dot, re-fang defanged notation (`[.]` back to `.`, `hxxp` back to `http`), and store internationalised names in one consistent form.
- **URLs:** split into scheme, host and path so that the host can also be matched as a domain; normalise the host as above.
- **IP addresses:** store in canonical text form; keep CIDR ranges as ranges rather than expanding them; record IPv4 and IPv6 under separate types.
- **Hashes:** convert to lower-case hexadecimal and record the algorithm as the type (`md5`, `sha1`, `sha256`); never compare a SHA-1 value with a SHA-256 value.
- **Field names:** map each producer's columns into your schema (type, value, confidence, first seen, valid until, source, context, TLP).

Deduplication then merges records whose canonical type and value are identical. The merge must **keep** information, not discard it: the merged record lists every source, keeps the earliest first-seen and the latest last-seen, keeps every context string, and carries the **most restrictive** TLP of the contributing records. What it must not do is add the confidences together. Two feeds reporting the same address may both have copied it from a third, so two entries are not two independent confirmations; Lesson 6 returns to this problem as circular reporting.

Matching needs the same discipline. The kit's matcher builds its domain search as the pattern `%://<domain>/%`, with the domain passed as a query parameter rather than pasted into the SQL. Requiring the scheme separator before the domain and a slash after it means that `evil.example` does not match `notevil.example`, or a URL that merely mentions the name in its path, as a naive substring search would. As the kit's own notes point out, the pattern also does not match subdomains; a production matcher would extract the hostname from each URL and compare it with the indicator and with its parent domains.

## Allowlisting

An allowlist (also called a warning list or exclusion list) is the set of values that must never raise an alert or be blocked on indicator evidence alone. It exists because feeds contain popular legitimate infrastructure far more often than newcomers expect: a malware sample contacts a well-known search engine to test connectivity, a phishing kit is hosted for a few hours on a large cloud provider's address, a sandbox records the public DNS resolver its malware used. Automatically generated feeds pick all of these up.

A good allowlist has layers:

- **Your own estate:** your public address ranges, your domains (in the lab, `banyan.example` and its subdomains such as `mail.banyan.example`, `intranet.banyan.example` and `crm.banyan.example`) and your private RFC 1918 ranges.
- **Critical business services:** the SaaS, payment, update and partner services the business depends on.
- **Widely shared infrastructure:** major cloud and CDN ranges, public DNS resolvers, well-known top domains. MISP ships curated warning lists of these.
- **Documented exceptions:** specific values excluded after an analyst confirmed a false positive, each with a reason, an owner and a review date.

The allowlist is a control with its own risk. Legitimate services can be abused, and attackers deliberately host content on trusted platforms because they expect to be allowlisted. So an allowlist entry should **remove a value from automatic action**, not from visibility: the match is still logged and can still be hunted. And, like the suppressions in Module 5, every exception needs an owner, a reason and an expiry that someone actually reviews.

> [!WARNING]
> Blocking straight from a feed without an allowlist is how SOCs cause outages. A single feed entry for a shared CDN address can cut off every site hosted behind it. Put blocking behind change control, apply it only to high-confidence, current, allowlist-checked indicators, and monitor what the block actually stops.

## Scoring and ageing out

Indicators lose value with time at different rates. An IP address can be reassigned to a new customer within days of an attacker abandoning it. A domain lasts longer, though it can expire and be re-registered by someone else. A file hash never matches a different file, but the attacker can produce a new hash at will, so an old hash simply stops matching anything. A feed-management process expresses this as an **age policy**: a default lifetime per indicator type, overridden by the producer's own `valid_until` where one is given, and a score that falls as the indicator ages without fresh sightings. There is no universal correct lifetime; the right values come from your own measurements of how long matches on each type stay useful, and should be written down as SOC policy.

Scoring combines what you know about the indicator into one number that decides where it goes. A simple, explainable model multiplies three factors: the producer's confidence, your assessment of the source's reliability (Lesson 2), and a recency factor that falls from 1 towards 0 as the indicator approaches and passes its validity limit.

Ageing out does **not** mean deleting. An expired indicator keeps value for retrospective work: if an incident is later found to have started months ago, you want to know whether its infrastructure appeared in your logs at the time. The rule is that expired indicators stay in the platform for history and hunting, and leave the paths that alert or block automatically. The kit implements exactly this rule: expired entries are still matched and reported, with a warning, but marked so that they cannot trigger action.

This is identical to the kit's saved `ioc_match_output.txt`. `minisiem.py load` reads `banyan_events.jsonl` into a SQLite table; `ioc_match.py` takes no options and evaluates each feed entry against the fixed date `AS_OF = date(2026, 11, 3)`, so the result is reproducible. Read it as a feed manager. The two ISAC entries and the internal look-alike domain are current and match, and their context (loader C2, a look-alike registered on 29 October, password spraying against VPNs) fits what the events show. The look-alike domain (valid until 30 November) and the ISAC C2 domain (31 December) remain current.

**Key learning.** Expiry removes an indicator from automatic action; it never rewrites what the indicator already showed.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"More feeds mean better coverage."** Overlapping feeds multiply duplicates and false positives faster than they add unique, relevant indicators. Measure uniqueness and relevance before adding a feed.
- **Deduplicating by adding confidence.** Two copies of one report are not two confirmations. Merge sources, do not sum scores.
- **Deleting expired indicators.** You lose the ability to look back. Retire them from action, keep them for history.
- **Reading "no match" as "clean".** The lab hash cannot match because the dataset holds no hashes. A feed is only as useful as the telemetry it is matched against.
- **Ignoring licence and TLP at ingestion.** Pushing a TLP:AMBER feed into a third-party cloud enrichment service or a public sandbox may breach its terms; record TLP on every indicator from the start.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Feeds are an input to your security tools, so they are also part of your attack surface. A compromised or careless producer can push indicators that cause outages (blocking shared or business infrastructure) or blind spots (an adversary who can influence an allowlist can hide). Defend the feed path like any other integration: authenticate TAXII and portal connections, monitor for sudden changes in feed size or in the share of entries hitting your allowlist, keep the allowlist under change control, and require a human decision before any feed-driven block reaches production. Count matches per feed, the share closed as false positives and the blocks rolled back; they are the evidence for the next feed review.

## Key takeaways

- Feeds differ by producer: open feeds are fast and broad but thin; commercial feeds sell curation and context; ISAC feeds are relevant but bound by TLP; internal intelligence is the most relevant but narrowest.
- CSV lists lose context; STIX 2.1 indicators carry pattern, validity and confidence, and TAXII transports them securely and incrementally.
- Judge a feed by relevance, timeliness, accuracy, context, uniqueness, ingestibility and terms, measured against your own telemetry.
- Normalise, deduplicate, allowlist, score and age every indicator before it may act; never wire a feed directly to a blocklist.
- Expiry removes an indicator from action, not from history: the lab's spraying address is expired on 15 November, yet its matches on 3 November remain evidence.
