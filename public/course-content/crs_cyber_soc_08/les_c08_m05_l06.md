## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Triage ends in one of three ways: the analyst closes the alert with a documented disposition, keeps working it, or escalates it. The first two are the analyst's own decisions. The third hands the decision to someone else, and it is the one that most often goes wrong, in both directions. Under-escalation leaves a real intrusion in the hands of a single analyst with limited time and authority while the attacker keeps moving. Over-escalation pages senior staff for noise until, like a noisy rule, the escalation path itself stops being taken seriously.

This lesson gives you criteria for that decision and a method for carrying it out. It covers:

- **When to escalate**: hard triggers that require immediate escalation, impact-based triggers that require escalation even without proof, and the situations where escalating is wrong.
- **How to escalate**: choosing the route (up a tier, across to a specialist team, or out of hours to on-call), using the case system and a paging channel, and getting an acknowledgement that transfers ownership.
- **What an escalation must carry**: a fixed structure that lets a busy reader decide in a minute, illustrated with the Banyan Microfinance lab case.
- **Indian reporting obligations**: why the CERT-In six-hour reporting requirement makes the time an incident was noticed an essential field, and why the analyst flags reportability rather than deciding it.

Lesson 5 dealt with alerts that turn out to be noise. This lesson deals with the ones that do not. Module 8 later covers incident handling itself (communication, severity matrices, containment); here the focus is the triage analyst's moment of decision and hand-off.

> [!NOTE]
> The examples use the work queue that `LAB_ASSETS/C08/triage.py` produces from the synthetic `banyan_events.jsonl` dataset, and event times queried with `minisiem.py` in a temporary copy of the kit. The escalation times (when the alert was noticed, paged and acknowledged) are illustrative; the dataset contains events, not SOC activity.

### Escalation is a transfer of ownership

Module 1, Lesson 3 defined escalation as handing an alert or case to a higher tier or to incident response, with ownership transferred and acknowledged. Each part of that definition matters. A message in a chat channel saying "can someone look at WS-104?" is not an escalation: nobody owns the problem and nobody has agreed to. An escalation names a receiver, travels through a channel the receiver is obliged to watch, carries the evidence, and ends when the receiver acknowledges that the case is now theirs. Until that acknowledgement arrives, the escalating analyst still owns the case and must chase it.

The reason for this formality is the asymmetry of cost that runs through the whole module. An unnecessary escalation costs a senior analyst ten minutes. A missed or lost escalation can cost the organisation its data, its availability or a regulatory deadline.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Escalation | Transfer of an alert or case, with ownership, to a person or team with more authority, skill or context, completed by their acknowledgement. |
| Hierarchical escalation | Up the chain: Tier 1 to Tier 2, Tier 2 to the incident response lead, IR lead to the CISO. Moves authority. |
| Functional escalation | Across to a specialist owner: the identity team for an account, the network team for a firewall change, HR or legal for an insider matter. Moves expertise. |
| Escalation criteria | The written conditions, in the SOC's playbooks, under which an analyst must or may escalate, and to whom. |
| Acknowledgement | The receiver's explicit confirmation that they own the case, recorded with a time. |
| Escalation SLA | The maximum time allowed between the trigger and the escalation, and between the escalation and acknowledgement, usually by severity (Module 1, Lesson 6). |
| Time noticed | The time the organisation first became aware of the event. For CERT-In reporting, this is when the six-hour clock starts. |
| Point of contact (POC) | The person an organisation designates to interact with CERT-In; reports go through this role, not from individual analysts. |
| De-escalation | Lowering the priority or returning a case once new evidence shows it is less serious. Legitimate and to be recorded, never silent. |

## When to escalate

Escalation criteria fall into three groups. Every SOC writes its own, and you must use your employer's playbooks; the groups below are the common structure behind them.

### Hard triggers: escalate now

Some findings are serious enough that the triage analyst escalates as soon as they are validated, without finishing the investigation. Waiting for certainty would only give the attacker time. Typical hard triggers are:

- **Evidence of malicious execution** on a host: an Office application spawning a hidden, encoded script interpreter; a known-bad hash executing; a confirmed command-and-control pattern.
- **Privileged access or persistence**: a new administrator account, a change to a privileged group, a new service or scheduled task, a successful logon to an administrator account from an unusual source.
- **Defence tampering**: security logs cleared, an endpoint agent stopped or uninstalled, audit policy changed. Attackers do this when they are about to do something they do not want recorded, or have just done it.
- **Data leaving**: a large or unusual upload to an unknown destination, especially from a system holding personal or financial data.
- **Active spread or impact**: the same behaviour on several hosts, mass file modification, ransomware indicators, a service going down.
- **Credential compromise**: a successful login following a password spray or from an impossible location, or MFA fatigue followed by approval.

### Impact-based triggers: escalate even without proof

Some alerts cannot be validated quickly, but would be serious if real. If the asset is a crown jewel (payments, a domain controller, a core banking or customer database), the identity is privileged, or the behaviour could spread, the analyst escalates to Tier 2 for investigation even when the disposition is still undetermined. Lesson 5 described "undetermined" as a valid disposition; for a high-impact entity it is also a reason to escalate rather than a reason to wait.

A time-box makes this concrete. Many SOCs give Tier 1 a fixed time per alert, for example fifteen or thirty minutes by severity. When the time-box expires and the alert is neither closed nor confirmed, the playbook decides: low-impact alerts may return to the queue with notes; high-impact ones are escalated.

### Beyond your remit: escalate sideways

Some escalations are about authority or sensitivity rather than severity:

- **Actions you are not authorised to take**: isolating a production server, disabling an executive's account, blocking a domain used by a business partner. You recommend; the owner of that authority decides.
- **Specialist knowledge**: suspected malware that needs reverse engineering, a cloud control-plane event the SOC does not understand, an operational technology system.
- **Sensitive subjects**: activity suggesting an insider, a senior executive or a privacy matter. These go to the route the playbook names (often the SOC manager, with HR or legal), on a need-to-know basis, and are not discussed in shared channels.

### When not to escalate

Escalation is not a substitute for triage. Do not escalate an alert you have not looked at, an alert you can close with evidence, or an alert purely because you are unsure how to use a tool. Ask a colleague instead; that is consultation, not escalation. Equally, do not escalate the same case twice to different people without telling either of them: two parallel owners are as bad as none.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> When in doubt between escalating and not escalating a potentially serious alert, escalate. A good SOC culture never punishes a reasonable escalation that turns out to be benign; it reviews the criteria instead. What it should never accept is a serious alert that sat in one analyst's queue because they were not sure.

### Applying the criteria to the lab queue

| 10.8 | R5 log_cleared, WS-104 | Security log cleared 08:17 | Defence tampering | Escalate now |
| 7.2 | R2 vpn_spray_then_success, gopal | 8 users failed from 203.0.113.66, then gopal succeeded 06:46 | Credential compromise | Escalate now, functional route to identity team as well |
| 7.2 | R1 office_spawns_script, WS-104 | PowerShell from Word 06:14, after invoice.docm download at 06:12 | Malicious execution | Escalate now |
| 5.4 (x2) | R3 new_local_admin, WS-104 | svc_update created 07:02, added to group 07:03 | Privileged persistence | Escalate now (same case) |

The striking result is that every alert meets a hard trigger, and five of the six alert types concern the same host and user. The correct escalation is therefore not six separate pages but one case for WS-104 and divya, linked to a second, possibly related case for the VPN login as gopal. Whether the spray and the phishing are connected is a hypothesis that the escalation should state as such. Note also that the lowest-priority alert, R6, is evidence of command and control: priority orders the queue, but it does not decide escalation. The decision is made on the criteria.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram condenses the three groups of criteria into a decision path an analyst can run in seconds at the end of triage, and attaches the reporting check that every escalation needs. It matters because a written, shared path is what makes two analysts on different shifts escalate the same alert the same way.

**Components.** At the top, the slate box **Triage finding** ("validated, enriched") is the input: an alert that has already been through Lessons 1 to 4. Three violet decision boxes hold the questions: **Hard trigger present?** (with the examples "execution, privilege, tampering, exfil"), **High impact if real?** ("crown jewel, privileged, spreading") and **Possibly reportable?** ("CERT-In incident types"). The outcomes are toned by urgency: the dark box **Escalate now** ("page on-call, minutes"), the orange **Escalate to Tier 2** ("case queue, within SLA"), the green **Close or keep triaging** ("documented disposition") and, at the bottom, the red **Notify IR lead + legal POC** ("record time noticed").

**Relationships and arrows.** From **Hard trigger present?**, "yes" leads to **Escalate now**, which sits on the left of the lower half, and "no" leads to the second question. From **High impact if real?**, the arrow labelled "yes or undetermined" leads to **Escalate to Tier 2**, while "no" leads to **Close or keep triaging**. Both escalation boxes connect to **Possibly reportable?**: a solid arrow from **Escalate now**, because every urgent escalation is checked, and a dashed arrow labelled "if facts change" from **Escalate to Tier 2**, because an investigation can turn a routine case into a reportable one at any time. The final "yes" arrow leads to the red notification box.

**Execution flow.** The hard-trigger question comes first so that the most urgent cases leave the decision path immediately. The impact question handles everything else. The reporting check comes after the escalation decision, not before it: an analyst should never delay paging on-call while working out whether a regulator must be told.

**Decision logic.** Note the label "yes or undetermined": uncertainty about a high-impact entity is treated as a reason to escalate. Only a "no" (low impact, or evidence that it is benign) leads to closure or further triage at Tier 1.

**Technical interpretation.** The three questions use different evidence. Hard triggers are read from the events (event IDs, process trees, bytes). Impact comes from enrichment (asset criticality, identity role). Reportability comes from comparing the facts with a list in the CERT-In Directions, which is a legal judgement the SOC supports but does not own.

**Practical example.** R5 on WS-104, the cleared Security log, meets a hard trigger: it goes straight to **Escalate now**. A cleared log on a payments approver's workstation, in the same case as an upload of 48.2 MB, makes **Possibly reportable?** a clear "yes", so the IR lead and legal POC are notified with the time the SOC noticed it.

**Security implications.** Without the first question, analysts wait for certainty and attackers gain hours. Without the last, a reportable incident can be handled technically well and still miss its legal deadline.

**Key learning.** Escalate on likelihood and impact, not certainty, and make every escalation carry the time noticed so that reportability can be decided in time.

## How to escalate

Knowing that a case must be escalated is half the job. The other half is making sure the right person receives it, understands it quickly and accepts it.

### Choosing the route

The playbook for the alert type normally names the receiver. Where it does not, choose by what the case needs:

| Need | Route | Example from the lab |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deeper investigation | Hierarchical, to Tier 2 | An undetermined R4 upload from a low-criticality host |
| Containment or incident declaration | Hierarchical, to the IR lead or on-call | The WS-104 case |
| Action on an identity | Functional, to the identity team | Resetting gopal's credentials and reviewing his VPN sessions |
| Network blocking | Functional, to network or proxy owners | Blocking files-share-drop.example and cdn-telemetry-sync.example |
| Legal or regulatory judgement | Via the IR lead to the CISO and legal POC | Whether the WS-104 case is reportable |

A single case can need several routes. The rule is one owner, many contributors: the IR lead owns the WS-104 case, and the identity and network teams receive functional requests from that owner, each tracked in the case.

### Channel, urgency and acknowledgement

**Use the case system as the record and a paging channel for urgency.** The case holds the evidence and the history. For urgent escalations, a page, a phone call or the on-call tool makes sure a human is interrupted; an email or a ticket alone is not enough at 02:00. The page should point to the case rather than repeat it.

**Get and record the acknowledgement.** Note who acknowledged, when, and what they said they will do. If no acknowledgement arrives within the escalation SLA, follow the playbook's next step: page the secondary on-call, then the SOC manager. Silence is not consent; it is a failed escalation.

**Use an out-of-band channel when the normal one may be compromised.** If the evidence suggests that email accounts or the collaboration platform are compromised, do not discuss the incident there. In the lab case, divya's mailbox may be readable by the attacker, so the escalation should not be sent to or copied to her, and the case should not be discussed in email threads she is on.

**Keep it need-to-know.** An escalation goes to those who must act. Copying wide distribution lists spreads sensitive details and, in insider cases, may warn the subject.

### What an escalation must carry

The receiver is usually busy, often woken up, and has never seen the alert. The escalation must let them understand the situation and decide in about a minute, then find the detail when they need it. A fixed structure achieves this: the reader always knows where to look, and the writer is forced to separate facts from guesses.

| Field | What it answers | Discipline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WHAT | What is happening, in one sentence | Name the entities and the suspected activity, without jargon. |
| SO WHAT | Why it matters now | Business impact and risk: data, systems, people affected. |
| EVIDENCE | The key facts | Times in UTC, sources, values. Point to the case for the rest. |
| CONFIDENCE | How sure you are | Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses, explicitly. |
| TIME NOTICED | When the organisation became aware | Needed for SLAs and for the CERT-In reporting clock. |
| DONE | Actions already taken | Including evidence preserved, and what has deliberately not been done. |
| NEED | The decision or help requested | Specific, with a deadline. |

The first six fields of this structure follow the pattern used for the course's incident escalations (WHAT, SO WHAT, EVIDENCE, CONFIDENCE, DONE, NEED); TIME NOTICED is added here as a separate line because it is easy to lose inside the evidence. Applied to the lab case, with illustrative SOC times:

```text
WHAT:        Likely compromise of finance workstation WS-104 (user divya)
             via a malicious document, with a new local account,
             possible data exfiltration and log tampering.
SO WHAT:     A payments approver's device is affected. 48,200,000 bytes
             were uploaded to an unknown file-sharing domain. The
             Security log was cleared, so local evidence is reduced.
EVIDENCE:    06:12Z invoice.docm from banyan-payroll-update.example
             06:14Z Word spawned PowerShell (R1)
             06:17Z-07:42Z 18 small requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example (R6)
             07:02Z-07:03Z local account svc_update created, added to group (R3)
             08:12Z upload 48,200,000 bytes to files-share-drop.example (R4)
             08:17Z Security log cleared (R5)
             Related: 06:46Z VPN login as gopal from 203.0.113.66 after
             failures for 8 users from that address (R2)
CONFIDENCE:  High that WS-104 is compromised. Link between the VPN spray
             and WS-104 is a hypothesis based on timing only.
TIME NOTICED: 08:25Z (first R5 alert opened by Tier 1)
DONE:        Case CASE-0001 opened; queries and raw events attached.
             No containment taken. Not discussed by email.
NEED:        IR lead to own the case and decide on isolating WS-104 and
             resetting divya's and gopal's credentials within 30 minutes.
             Legal POC to assess CERT-In reportability.
```

Read it as the receiver would. The first two lines say what is wrong and why it matters. The evidence block is a timeline, every line with a time, a source rule and a value that can be checked in the case. The confidence line keeps a reasonable hypothesis (the VPN link) from becoming a "fact" that later misleads the investigation. The DONE line records a deliberate non-action, no containment yet, which matters because the receiver must know that the host is still live. The NEED line asks for two decisions with a deadline and flags the reporting question to the right role.

> [!TIP]
> Write the escalation as if it will be read aloud on a bridge call by someone who has never seen the SIEM. If a sentence would only make sense to you, rewrite it.

### The hand-off in sequence

The order in which messages move matters as much as their content. The figure shows the hand-off for the WS-104 case.

*Figure 5.12: An escalation as a hand-off of ownership. Illustrative message order for escalating the WS-104 case, from the Tier 1 page to a CERT-In report decided by the designated point of contact.*

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The diagram shows an escalation as an exchange between roles over time, rather than a single message. It matters because most escalation failures are failures of sequence: a page with no case behind it, a case with no page, or a report decision requested so late that the deadline cannot be met.

**Components.** Four lanes run left to right. **Tier 1 analyst** (blue) is the person who triaged the alerts. **On-call IR lead** (orange) is the receiver who will own the case. **CISO / legal POC** (violet, "designated point of contact") holds the authority for regulatory decisions and the formal relationship with CERT-In. **CERT-In** (slate) is the external body, India's national agency for incident response.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows are requests or deliveries; dashed arrows are replies. The analyst's **Page: SEV1 WS-104 + case link** interrupts the IR lead and points to the evidence. The dashed **Ack 08:31Z: IR lead owns the case** is the moment ownership moves. Only then does the analyst send **Package: what, so what, evidence**, the structured escalation above (in practice the package is already in the case, and this step confirms it has been read). The IR lead sends **Possibly reportable, noticed 08:25Z** to the CISO / legal POC, who replies with the dashed **Decision: report; containment approved**. The POC then sends **Report within 6 h of noticing** to CERT-In. The last dashed arrow, **Tasks: preserve evidence, watch gopal**, returns work from the new owner to the analyst.

**Execution flow.** Page, acknowledgement, package, reporting question, decision, report, tasking. The reporting question travels early, right after the IR lead takes the case, because the six-hour window is short and the legal assessment takes time.

**Decision logic.** If the acknowledgement does not arrive within the SLA, the analyst does not proceed down the lanes; they escalate to the secondary on-call. If the POC decides the incident is not reportable, the arrow to CERT-In is not sent, and the decision with its reasoning is recorded in the case.

**Technical interpretation.** The lanes show that reporting authority and technical authority sit with different roles. The analyst never contacts CERT-In directly; the analyst's contribution is the time noticed and the facts that make the decision possible.

**Practical example.** With the illustrative times, the SOC noticed at 08:25Z, which is 13:55 IST. Six hours later is 14:25Z, or 19:55 IST. That deadline should be written in the case at once, in both time zones.

**Security implications.** The order protects both the investigation and the organisation: nothing is sent to an external party before the owner and the POC agree, and the regulatory question is never left until the technical work is over. The tasking arrow also keeps the original analyst, who knows the data best, engaged after hand-off.

**Key learning.** An escalation is complete when ownership is acknowledged, and a reportable incident is on time only if the time noticed travels with it from the first message.

## Indian reporting obligations: what the analyst must know

The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) is the national agency for cyber incident response under section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000. Its Directions of 28 April 2022, issued under section 70B(6), require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates and government organisations to report the cyber incident types listed in the Directions to CERT-In **within six hours of noticing them or being brought to notice of them**. The same Directions require these entities to designate a point of contact to interface with CERT-In, to keep ICT system logs for a rolling 180 days within India, and to synchronise their clocks with NTP servers specified by CERT-In or traceable to them.

The listed incident types are broad. They include, for example, unauthorised access to IT systems or data, malicious code attacks such as ransomware, identity theft, spoofing and phishing attacks, data breaches and data leaks. The WS-104 case, with likely unauthorised access through a phishing document and a large upload of unknown content, plainly needs assessment against that list.

For the triage analyst this has four practical consequences:

1. **Record the time noticed, early and precisely.** The six hours run from noticing, not from containment or from certainty. Treat the first point at which the SOC was aware of the incident as the likely start, write it in UTC and IST, and let legal decide the final interpretation.
2. **Flag, do not decide.** Whether an event is reportable, and what goes in the report, is decided by the designated POC with legal advice. The analyst's escalation flags "possibly reportable" and supplies facts.
3. **Escalate fast enough to leave time.** If an analyst spends four hours confirming a compromise before escalating, the organisation has two hours left for a legal decision and a report. Hard triggers exist partly for this reason.
4. **Keep the evidence trustworthy.** Accurate clocks and retained logs are what make the timeline in a report credible; a cleared Security log, as in R5, is exactly why central collection matters.

Other obligations may apply in parallel, for example sector regulators' requirements and personal data breach intimation under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. Their scope and timelines are for legal and the data protection function to assess; the SOC's contribution is the same: fast escalation, a clear time noticed and reliable facts about what data may be involved.

> [!CAUTION]
> Do not paraphrase or estimate legal obligations in a case note ("we have 72 hours", "this is not reportable"). Write facts and flags. Interpretations belong to the people accountable for them, and an analyst's guess recorded in a case can mislead the decision.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **Waiting for certainty.** Escalation criteria are about likelihood and impact. By the time everything is confirmed, the attacker may have achieved their objective and the reporting window may be gone.
- **Escalating by chat.** A message in a team channel without a case, a named receiver and an acknowledgement is a hope, not an escalation.
- **Escalating alerts instead of cases.** Six pages for six alerts on one host bury the story. Group related alerts into one case first, as the lab table shows.
- **Treating queue priority as the escalation decision.** R6 had the lowest priority in the lab queue but showed command and control. Priority orders the work; criteria decide escalation.
- **Mixing fact and hypothesis.** "The VPN spray led to the phishing" written as fact can send an investigation down the wrong path. Label hypotheses.
- **Forgetting what was not done.** The receiver needs to know that a host is still live or that evidence has not yet been preserved.
- **Letting the clock go unrecorded.** Without a time noticed, nobody can show that a report was on time.
- **Silent de-escalation.** Downgrading a case is legitimate, but the reason must be recorded and the owner informed.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Escalation paths are part of the security architecture. Attackers benefit from every delay, so they time activity for nights, weekends and holidays, when escalation is slowest. The defences are organisational:

- **24x7 coverage of hard triggers**, through on-call rotas or a managed service, with escalation contacts that are tested regularly (a page that reaches a former employee's phone is a failed control).
- **Written criteria in playbooks**, so that escalation does not depend on one analyst's confidence or seniority.
- **Escalation SLAs and their measurement**: time from trigger to escalation and from escalation to acknowledgement, reviewed in SOC metrics alongside detection and response times.
- **Out-of-band communication** prepared in advance, for incidents where email or chat may be compromised.
- **Post-incident review of escalations**: were the criteria met earlier than the escalation happened? Were any escalations unnecessary? Both answers improve the criteria.
- **Readiness for reporting**: a known POC, a contact route to that person out of hours, and a case template field for the time noticed.

## Best practices

1. Validate quickly, then escalate on hard triggers without waiting to finish the investigation.
2. Escalate high-impact undetermined cases rather than holding them.
3. Group related alerts into one case before escalating, and state links between cases as hypotheses.
4. Use the case system for the record and a paging channel for urgency; obtain and record acknowledgement.
5. Use the fixed structure: what, so what, evidence, confidence, time noticed, done, need.
6. Record the time noticed in UTC and IST and flag possible CERT-In reportability to the IR lead and POC.
7. Keep escalations need-to-know and out-of-band when normal channels may be compromised.
8. Record de-escalations and their reasons as carefully as escalations.

## Key takeaways

- Escalation is a transfer of ownership, complete only when the receiver acknowledges it.
- Escalate immediately on hard triggers (malicious execution, privilege or persistence, defence tampering, data leaving, spread, credential compromise) and on high-impact cases even when undetermined.
- Queue priority orders the work; escalation criteria decide escalation. In the lab, the lowest-priority alert still showed command and control.
- A good escalation carries what, so what, evidence, confidence, time noticed, done and need, with facts and hypotheses clearly separated.
- CERT-In's Directions require listed incidents to be reported within six hours of noticing them. The analyst records the time noticed and flags reportability; the designated point of contact, with legal, decides and reports.
