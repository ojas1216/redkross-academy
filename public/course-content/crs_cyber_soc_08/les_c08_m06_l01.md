## Introduction and conceptual foundation

On the morning of 3 November 2026 the Banyan SOC dataset records a short, ugly story on workstation `WS-104`. At 06:12 the payments approver `divya` downloads `invoice.docm` from `banyan-payroll-update.example`. Two minutes later `WINWORD.EXE` starts `powershell.exe`. From 06:17 the machine calls `https://cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping` every 300 seconds. At 07:02 a local administrator called `svc_update` appears, at 08:12 about 48 MB leave for `files-share-drop.example`, and at 08:17 the security log is cleared. Separately, at 06:42, the address `203.0.113.66` tries eight VPN accounts in under a minute and gets into `gopal`'s.

Every item in that story could be written down and handed to another organisation so that it can look for the same thing: a domain, an address, a file hash, a parent-child process pair, a timing pattern. Those written-down things are **indicators**. Threat intelligence, as a SOC uses it day to day, is largely the business of receiving, judging, matching and sharing indicators, and of knowing what kind of thing each one is. That last part is the subject of this lesson, and it matters more than it first appears. A domain name and a behavioural pattern are both "indicators", but they differ in how they are matched, what telemetry they need, how easily an attacker can change them, how long they stay useful and how much damage a wrong one can do.

Module 5 lesson 3 showed how an analyst uses intelligence matches *during triage*, reading `ioc_match.py`'s five result lines as evidence for or against an alert. This module steps back and studies intelligence itself. Here the question is not "what does this match mean for this alert?" but "what kind of indicator is this, how much is it worth, and for how long?" The answer starts with two classic ways of sorting indicators: by their **structure** (atomic, computed, behavioural) and by the **cost to the adversary** of replacing them (the Pyramid of Pain). From those two follows the practical idea of **shelf life**: every indicator type decays at its own rate, and an intelligence programme that ignores decay fills its SIEM with stale values that match innocent traffic.

> [!NOTE]
> All indicators in this lesson are fictional and use documentation ranges (`203.0.113.0/24`, `198.51.100.0/24`) and `.example` domains. The lab feed in `LAB_ASSETS/C08/ioc_match.py` is described by its author as "STIX-inspired fields, not a full STIX implementation". Treat it as a teaching model of a feed, not as a standard format.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Observable | A fact that can be seen in data: an address in a log, a hash of a file, a process start. It carries no judgement. |
| Indicator | An observable (or pattern of observables) together with the judgement that it suggests malicious activity, plus context: what it was seen doing, by whom, how confident, valid until when. |
| Indicator of compromise (IOC) | The common name for indicators used to find intrusions that have already happened. In SOC usage it is often used loosely for any indicator. |
| Atomic indicator | An indicator that cannot be broken into smaller meaningful parts and keeps its meaning on its own: an IP address, a domain name, an email address. |
| Computed indicator | An indicator derived by calculation from data: a file hash, a statistical property of traffic, a regular expression over a field. |
| Behavioural indicator | A combination of atomic and computed indicators with logic, describing what the adversary *does*: "Word starts PowerShell", "many accounts fail then one succeeds from the same address". |
| Pyramid of Pain | David Bianco's 2013 model ranking indicator types by how much pain denying them causes an adversary: hashes, IPs, domains, network/host artefacts, tools, TTPs. |
| TTP | Tactics, techniques and procedures: how an adversary operates, as catalogued for example in MITRE ATT&CK. |
| Shelf life | The period during which an indicator remains a reliable sign of the activity it was reported for. |
| Validity window | The explicit `valid_from` / `valid_until` dates a producer attaches to an indicator; the lab feed uses `valid_until`. |
| False positive (intel) | A match on an indicator in benign traffic, for example a shared hosting address now used by someone else. |

## Three structural classes: atomic, computed, behavioural

The atomic / computed / behavioural classification comes from Hutchins, Cloppert and Amin's 2011 Lockheed Martin paper on intelligence-driven defence (the same paper that introduced the intrusion kill chain). Its value is that it describes *how an indicator is matched*, which in turn decides what data a SOC must collect before the indicator is any use at all.

### Atomic indicators

An atomic indicator is a single value that means something on its own. In the lab feed, `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, `banyan-payroll-update.example`, `203.0.113.66` and `198.51.100.104` are all atomic. Matching them is an equality test against one field: `ioc_match.py` looks for the domain inside the `url` column (`url LIKE '%://domain/%'`) and for the address in `src_ip`.

Atomic indicators are cheap to share, cheap to match and easy to explain, which is why they make up the bulk of most feeds. Their weakness is that they identify *infrastructure*, and infrastructure is what attackers rent, borrow and throw away. An atomic indicator is also only as precise as the thing it names: an IP address on a shared host or a cloud load balancer may front hundreds of unrelated sites, so equality on the address does not mean equality of the actor.

### Computed indicators

A computed indicator is produced by running a function over data. The commonest is a cryptographic hash of a file. The feed's fifth entry is the SHA-256 of the malicious `invoice.docm` (truncated in the course to `9f2c...e1a0`). A hash identifies one exact sequence of bytes with practically no false positives: if the hash matches, it is the same file. Change one byte and the hash changes completely, which is both the strength (precision) and the weakness (brittleness) of this class.

Not every computed indicator is a hash. The kit's `hunt.py` computes one too: for each host and URL it measures the gaps between requests and reports their coefficient of variation (CV, the standard deviation divided by the mean). Its saved output lists `WS-104 https://cdn-telemetry-sync.example/api/v2/ping n=18 mean_gap=300s cv=0.004`. "Requests to one URL at near-perfectly regular intervals" is a computed indicator of beaconing: it does not depend on the domain's name at all, only on a number derived from the timestamps.

Computed indicators always need the input data. A hash cannot match if no sensor records file hashes, which is exactly why the feed's highest-confidence entry (confidence 90) returns `no match` in the lab: the Banyan dataset contains no file hashes. The indicator was never wrong; the SOC was blind to it.

### Behavioural indicators

A behavioural indicator combines atomic and computed pieces with logic to describe an action. The Module 4 rules are behavioural indicators written as SIEM queries:

- `R1 office_spawns_script`: a Sysmon process creation (`event_id=1`) whose parent image contains `winword.exe` and whose image is `powershell.exe` or `cmd.exe`.
- `R2 vpn_spray_then_success`: failures for five or more distinct users from one source address, followed by a success from that address.

Neither rule names an attacker's address, domain or file. `R1` would fire whatever the document was called and wherever it came from; `R2` would fire for any spraying source. Behavioural indicators are harder to write, need richer telemetry (process lineage, authentication outcomes) and produce more borderline matches that need an analyst's judgement. In exchange they survive the attacker's routine changes of infrastructure and tooling.

> [!TIP]
> When you receive an indicator, first ask "which field, in which log source, would this have to match?" If you cannot name the field and confirm the source is collected, the indicator cannot protect you, however good it is.

## The Pyramid of Pain

The structural classes say how an indicator is matched. David Bianco's Pyramid of Pain (2013) asks a different question: if a defender detects and denies this indicator, how much does it cost the adversary to work around it? The answer orders indicator types into six levels, cheapest to replace at the bottom.

*Figure 6.1: The Pyramid of Pain, mapped to the Banyan case. David Bianco's six indicator levels, from trivial to replace at the base to tough at the top, each with an example from the lab dataset.*

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure ranks six indicator types by the effort an adversary must spend to replace them once a defender detects and blocks them. It explains why an intelligence programme that collects only hashes and addresses keeps losing the same fight, and why behavioural detection is worth its cost.

**Components.** The pyramid is drawn as six stacked rounded bands, narrow at the top. From the base upwards: **Hash values** (slate, "trivial"), **IP addresses** (blue, "easy"), **Domain names** (violet, "simple"), **Network & host artefacts** (orange, "annoying"), **Tools** (red, "challenging") and **TTPs** (dark, "tough"). Each band's sub-label pairs Bianco's pain word with an example from the Banyan dataset: the `invoice.docm` hash, `203.0.113.66`, `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, the fixed `/api/v2/ping` path at a 300 s rhythm, the loader that beacons, and the phishing-then-spray-then-exfiltration way of operating.

**Relationships.** Reading upwards, each level costs the adversary more to change. A hash changes when one byte of the file changes. An address changes when the attacker moves to another rented server. A domain costs a registration and some setup. Artefacts such as a URL path, a user-agent or a beacon rhythm are baked into the tooling and need code or configuration changes. Replacing a tool means building or buying another. Changing TTPs means changing how the group works, which is the slowest and most expensive change of all.

**Execution flow.** The pyramid is not a procedure, but a SOC reads it in two directions. Intelligence usually *arrives* at the bottom (feeds are dominated by hashes, IPs and domains) and the analyst's job is to *climb*: from a reported domain, find the artefacts and behaviour around it, and write detections at the highest level the telemetry allows.

**Technical interpretation.** The bottom three levels are atomic or computed values matched by equality. The top three are behavioural: they match patterns in process, network and authentication data. This is why the pyramid and the structural classes agree: the higher you go, the more logic and telemetry the indicator needs and the fewer exact values it contains.

**Practical example.** In the lab, the vendor's hash at the base matched nothing because no hashes were collected. The beacon artefact one level up was found by `hunt.py` without knowing the domain in advance. `R1`, a TTP-level rule, fired on the Word-to-PowerShell step regardless of the file's hash or origin. Had the attacker re-packed the document and moved to a new domain, only the upper levels would still have caught them.

**Security implications.** Blocking bottom-level indicators is still worth doing, because it is cheap and stops unsophisticated reuse, but it should never be the only layer. Detections at the top need tuning and analyst time; budget for both.

**Key learning.** The higher an indicator sits on the pyramid, the longer it stays useful and the more it costs the adversary when you deny it; aim your detections as high as your telemetry allows.

## Shelf life: why indicators decay

An indicator is a claim about the world at a moment in time: "this address was used for password spraying", "this domain was command-and-control for a loader". The world moves on. The attacker abandons the server, the hosting provider reassigns the address to a new customer, the domain expires and is re-registered by someone innocent, the malware is recompiled. The indicator keeps its value in your SIEM long after its meaning has gone. **Shelf life** is the period during which a match on the indicator is still good evidence of the activity it was reported for.

Shelf life depends mainly on the type, which is why it belongs in this lesson rather than only in the lesson on confidence:

- **IP addresses decay fastest.** Attackers use cloud instances, VPS hosts, residential proxies and compromised machines that they lose or abandon within days. After that the address is recycled, often into a shared pool. A stale address is the classic source of intelligence false positives, because the new holder is almost always benign.
- **Domains last longer but still decay.** A domain costs money and set-up time, so attackers reuse them for weeks or months. Once burned, they are dropped; after expiry they may be parked, sink-holed by researchers or re-registered. A look-alike domain aimed at *your* organisation stays relevant for as long as it is registered, because its only purpose is to impersonate you.
- **Hashes never stop identifying the file, but stop finding it.** A SHA-256 will identify `invoice.docm` forever. The problem is relevance: the campaign moves to re-packed variants with new hashes, so an old hash stops matching anything new. Old hashes are rarely *wrong*, just increasingly useless, and they cost storage and matching time.
- **Artefacts, tools and TTPs last longest.** A beacon rhythm or URL pattern survives infrastructure changes; tooling is reused across campaigns for months or years; a group's way of operating changes slowly. Their shelf life is measured in the lifetime of the tooling or the group, not of a server.

The lab feed makes shelf life explicit with a `valid_until` date for every entry, evaluated against a fixed "as of" date (`AS_OF = date(2026, 11, 3)`). Counting days from 3 November 2026, the producers gave the VPN-spraying address `203.0.113.66` only 7 more days, the look-alike domain 27, the C2 domain 58 and the file hash 89. The open-feed address `198.51.100.104` had expired 126 days earlier, on 30 June. The ordering is no accident: it follows the type, and the address with the shortest life is the one that fell off the end. These are the feed producers' choices for this fictional dataset, not universal rules; what generalises is that addresses get short windows and file or behaviour indicators get long ones.

**Purpose.** The table is a field guide to the indicators of the Banyan case. For each one it answers the four questions an analyst should ask before relying on an indicator: what type is it, how is it matched, how long will it stay useful, and do we collect the data it must match against?

**Components.** There are five columns: **Indicator (lab)**, **Class**, **Pyramid level**, **Shelf life** and **Telemetry needed**. There are six rows. The first four are feed entries from `ioc_match.py`: `9f2c...e1a0` (the `invoice.docm` hash), `203.0.113.66`, `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` and `banyan-payroll-update.example`. The fifth is the beacon pattern from `hunt.py` (`cv=0.004`, 300 s) and the sixth is the behavioural rule `R1` (Word starts PowerShell). A coloured strip at the left of each row, matched by the colour of the first cell, climbs with the pyramid: slate for the hash, blue for the address, violet for the two domains, orange for the beacon artefact and dark for the behavioural rule.

**Relationships.** Reading across a row connects the theory to a decision. The hash row, for example, reads computed, bottom of the pyramid, long-lived as an identifier but quickly irrelevant, and needing *file hashes (EDR, mail gateway)* - which the lab does not have. The `R1` row reads behavioural, TTP level, long-lived, and needing *Sysmon process creation with parent*, which the lab does have.

**Execution flow.** Use the table top to bottom when a new indicator arrives: place it in a row by type, read off its expected shelf life to set or check its validity window, then confirm the telemetry column against your data sources. If the telemetry is missing, the right action is a collection-gap ticket, not a silent "no match".

**Decision logic.** Two columns drive the decision. *Shelf life* decides how long the indicator may stay active before it must be reviewed. *Telemetry needed* decides whether it can match at all. An indicator that is long-lived but unmatched (the hash) points to a collection gap; one that is short-lived and matched (the address) needs a timestamp check before anyone acts.

**Technical interpretation.** The atomic rows are equality tests on one column (`url`, `src_ip`). The computed rows need a function over data: a hash function over file bytes, a statistic over timestamps. The behavioural row joins conditions on several fields of the same event (`source`, `event_id`, `parent_image`, `image`).

**Practical example.** The `R1` hit at 06:14 on `WS-104` would have fired even if the attacker had used a new document and a new domain, while the `203.0.113.66` match would be worthless a week later. The table predicts exactly that difference.

**Security implications.** A feed that is mostly bottom-row indicators gives a false sense of coverage. Map every feed to this table periodically and track the share of indicators you cannot match because the telemetry is absent.

**Key learning.** An indicator's type predicts both its lifetime and its data requirements; check both before you trust a match or a non-match.

## Worked example: sorting the lab feed by type

The lab kit was copied to a private working folder and run there, never inside `LAB_ASSETS`. `minisiem.py load` first builds `minisiem.db` from `banyan_events.jsonl`; `ioc_match.py` then compares its five-entry feed against those events as of 3 November 2026. The script takes no arguments.

```terminal
$ python minisiem.py load
loaded 340 events
$ python ioc_match.py
MATCH    domain cdn-telemetry-sync.example       conf= 85 hits= 18 first=2026-11-03T06:17:00Z src=ISAC-A TLP:AMBER
MATCH    domain banyan-payroll-update.example    conf= 70 hits=  1 first=2026-11-03T06:12:00Z src=internal TLP:GREEN
MATCH    ipv4   203.0.113.66                     conf= 60 hits=  9 first=2026-11-03T06:42:00Z src=ISAC-A TLP:GREEN
EXPIRED  ipv4   198.51.100.104                   conf= 40 hits=  1 first=2026-11-03T13:33:00Z src=open feed TLP:CLEAR
         expired indicator still matches: review before acting
no match sha256 9f2c...e1a0                      conf= 90 hits=  0 first=- src=vendor TLP:AMBER
```

The output was identical to the kit's saved `ioc_match_output.txt`. Module 5 read these lines as triage evidence for individual alerts. Read them now by indicator type:

1. **The two domains matched, and both are rooted in the case.** `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` has 18 hits, all from `WS-104`, between 06:17 and 07:42 - the same 18 requests `hunt.py` found by timing alone. `banyan-payroll-update.example` has one hit: the 06:12 download of `invoice.docm`. Domains sit in the middle of the pyramid and their windows (58 and 27 days) are long enough that both are still current.
2. **The live address matched exactly the spray.** The nine hits on `203.0.113.66` are the eight VPN failures (`asha` to `hema`, seven seconds apart from 06:42:00) and `gopal`'s success at 06:46. The match is real, but the indicator has a seven-day window: by 10 November it should no longer drive automatic action, because the address is likely to have moved on.
3. **The expired address matched an innocent-looking event.** `198.51.100.104` matched one event: `gopal`'s successful VPN login at 13:33 with country `IN`. The indicator's context says "scanning (old, shared hosting)". This is the textbook stale-IP false positive: the type decays fast, the window lapsed in June, and the current holder of the address may be a legitimate service. The script is right to say "review before acting".
4. **The hash could not match.** The best-graded indicator in the feed is the one the SOC cannot use, because it is computed over data the SOC does not collect.

Now set the feed beside the rule hits from the same dataset. `python minisiem.py rules` reports `R1 office_spawns_script` at 06:14 on `WS-104` and `R4 large_upload` at 08:12 to `https://files-share-drop.example/upload` with 48,200,000 bytes. No feed entry mentions `files-share-drop.example`; the exfiltration destination was found only because a behavioural rule looks for unusually large uploads. The lesson of the worked example is not that feeds are useless (the C2 domain match confirmed the intrusion in seconds) but that the feed's atomic values and the SOC's behavioural rules cover different parts of the same intrusion, and only together do they cover most of it.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Two matches in this output involve the *same user*: `gopal`. One is the genuine spray at 06:42-06:46, the other an expired-indicator match on his 13:33 login. Type and shelf life, not the word "MATCH", separate the evidence from the noise.

## Choosing what to do with each type

The type of an indicator should shape the action taken on a match. A practical default, which each SOC adapts to its own risk appetite:

| Type | Default use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| File hash (current) | Block or quarantine automatically where telemetry exists | Near-zero false positives while the file is in circulation |
| IP address | Alert and enrich; block only when fresh, well graded and not shared infrastructure | Fast decay; shared hosting, CDN and NAT cause collateral damage |
| Domain | Alert; block when fresh and the domain is not a shared platform | Longer life, but blocking a shared service breaks business traffic |
| Look-alike of your own domain | Block, and report through the registrar's abuse process | Its only purpose is impersonation |
| Artefact or behaviour | Detection rule with tuning and analyst review | Durable, but broader; matches need judgement |

Blocking is an action with business cost; detection is an action with analyst cost. The type tells you which cost you are likely to pay. How *well graded* an indicator must be before it may block is the subject of the next lesson.

## Climbing the pyramid from a single indicator

Most intelligence arrives at the bottom of the pyramid. The professional habit is to use each low-level indicator as a starting point and climb, turning a value that will be useless next month into a detection that will still work next year. With the Banyan data the climb looks like this:

1. **Start from the atomic value.** The feed says `cdn-telemetry-sync.example` is C2 for a loader. Search for it: 18 requests from `WS-104`.
2. **Look at the artefacts around it.** All 18 requests go to the same path, `/api/v2/ping`, each sends 220 bytes, and `hunt.py` measures a mean gap of 300 seconds with a CV of 0.004. Those are network artefacts: a fixed path, a fixed small size, a machine-perfect rhythm.
3. **Generalise the artefact into a computed indicator.** `R6 beacon_regular_small` encodes the size part (ten or more proxy requests from one host to one URL whose sizes differ by less than 50 bytes) and `hunt.py`'s H1 search encodes the timing part (eight or more requests with an interval CV below 0.2). Neither needs the domain's name.
4. **Look at what happened just before.** On the same host, two minutes before the first beacon, `R1` saw Word start PowerShell, and two minutes before that the user downloaded `invoice.docm` from a look-alike domain. That chain - look-alike domain, macro document, Office spawning a shell, regular beaconing - is a description of the adversary's procedure.
5. **Check the behavioural detections exist and are tested.** `R1` and `R6` already exist in the lab; Module 4 covered testing them. If one were missing, writing it would be the most valuable output of the whole exercise.

Each step trades precision for durability. The domain matches only this campaign; the behaviour matches this campaign and its successors. The analyst's output is no longer "block one domain" but "we can see this way of operating whatever infrastructure it uses next".

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"A hash match is the strongest indicator, so collect hashes."** A current hash match is precise, but the hash level is the cheapest for the adversary to change. Precision and durability are different properties.
- **"No match means we were not targeted."** A non-match is only meaningful if the data searched could have contained the indicator. The lab's hash could never match because no file hashes were collected.
- **"IOCs and TTPs are alternatives."** They complement each other. Atomic matches give fast, explainable confirmation (the C2 domain); behavioural rules find what no feed knew about (`files-share-drop.example`).
- **"An indicator stays valid until someone removes it."** Every indicator decays. Without a validity window or a review date, old addresses accumulate and match innocent traffic, as `198.51.100.104` did.
- **"An IP address identifies an attacker."** It identifies a network endpoint at a moment. Shared hosting, NAT, VPN exits and cloud reallocation mean many unrelated parties may sit behind one address over time.
- **"Behavioural indicators are just rules, not intelligence."** A well-described behaviour, shared with context, is often the most valuable intelligence a partner can receive because it survives infrastructure changes.

## Security implications, detection and defence

From the defender's side, indicator types translate into concrete engineering choices:

- **Telemetry coverage decides which types you can use.** Hash indicators need file hashing at the endpoint or mail gateway; domain indicators need DNS or proxy logs; behavioural indicators need process lineage (Sysmon event ID 1 or an EDR equivalent) and authentication logs. Keep a map of indicator types against log sources and treat gaps as risks.
- **Blocking low-level indicators has collateral cost.** Blocking an address or domain on shared infrastructure can cut off legitimate services. Prefer detection and enrichment for fast-decaying, shared types, and reserve automatic blocking for fresh, well-graded, unshared values.
- **Expiry is a control.** Expired indicators must stop driving automatic actions. The lab script flags them and prints "review before acting"; a production platform should either drop them or downgrade them to context only.
- **Adversaries know the pyramid too.** Re-packing a file, rotating addresses and registering fresh domains are routine for capable groups. Detections at the artefact and TTP levels force them into the expensive changes.
- **Indicators can leak.** A list of the hashes and domains you hunt for tells an adversary what you know. Respect the TLP marking each indicator carries (the lab feed's entries are AMBER, GREEN and CLEAR); sharing rules are covered later in this module.

## Best practices

1. Record the type, structural class and validity window for every indicator you accept; never load bare values.
2. Before activating an indicator, name the field and log source it must match, and confirm the source is collected.
3. Give each type a default validity window and review date, short for addresses and longer for domains, files and behaviours, and override it only with a reason.
4. Treat every low-level match as a starting point: pivot to artefacts and behaviour around it and ask whether a durable detection can be written.
5. Do not automatically block addresses on shared hosting, CDNs or cloud ranges without human review.
6. Measure coverage by pyramid level, not by indicator count: a feed of ten thousand hashes you cannot match is worth nothing.
7. When sharing, include the behaviour and context, not only the values.

## Key takeaways

- An indicator is an observable plus a judgement and context; its type determines how it is matched, what data it needs and how long it lasts.
- Atomic indicators (addresses, domains) and computed indicators (hashes, statistics) match exact values; behavioural indicators combine them with logic to describe what the adversary does.
- The Pyramid of Pain ranks types by the cost to the adversary of replacing them: hashes, IPs, domains, network/host artefacts, tools, TTPs.
- Shelf life follows type: addresses decay in days, domains in weeks or months, hashes stay precise but lose relevance, behaviours last longest.
- In the lab, the C2 domain match confirmed the intrusion, the expired address matched a different event, the hash could not match without hash telemetry, and only a behavioural rule found the exfiltration destination.
- Use low-level indicators as starting points and climb towards durable behavioural detections.
