## Why hunt documentation matters

A hunt that is not written down did not happen, as far as the rest of the organization is concerned. The analyst may remember that they checked VPN failures last Tuesday, but the incident responder who arrives next month cannot see that, the detection engineer cannot turn it into a rule, and the manager cannot tell whether the team's hunting effort covered the behaviors that matter. Documentation turns a hunt from a private activity into an organizational asset.

Good documentation does four jobs. It makes the hunt **reproducible**: another analyst, given the same saved data, can rerun the queries and get the same results. It makes the hunt **reviewable**: a peer can check the reasoning from hypothesis to conclusion and disagree with specific steps. It makes the hunt **actionable**: incident response and detection engineering can pick up the parts relevant to them without re-reading the whole investigation. And it makes the hunt **cumulative**: over months, the hunt records show which behaviors have been tested, with which data, and what was found, including negative results.

Negative results are the part most often left undocumented. A hunt that found nothing seems to have produced nothing to write. In fact it produced a scoped statement: in this population, over this window, with these sources and this validated query, the predicted behavior was not observed. That is exactly the kind of statement that lets a team prioritize, avoid duplicate work and justify collection investments.

## The hunt record

A hunt record is a structured document, usually one per hypothesis. Teams format it differently, as a ticket, a wiki page or a notebook, but the sections below are the minimum for the four jobs above.

**Header.** A unique hunt identifier, analyst, reviewer, date started and closed, and status.

**Trigger.** Why this hunt was run: intelligence note, anomaly, change, prior incident or analyst observation, with a reference.

**Hypothesis.** The full statement from Lesson 1: behavior, scope, predictions, refuting evidence.

**Scope.** Entities and UTC time window, and what was excluded and why. Exclusions for data reasons and for priority reasons should be distinguished.

**Data sources and coverage.** For each source, the population, event types, fields and time coverage actually available, plus known gaps. This is the data-needs matrix from Lesson 2, updated with what the analyst actually found.

**Baseline.** Peer group, baseline window, metric, and any limitations, such as a single-day baseline.

**Queries.** Each query in full, with its purpose, inputs, assumptions, expected positive and negative, and version. Queries should be copied as text, not described.

**Results.** What each query returned, with row counts and evidence citations. Include zero-result queries.

**Analysis.** The reasoning from results to conclusion, including benign alternatives considered and how they were assessed.

**Outcome and confidence.** Positive, benign or no-data, with a confidence level and a one-sentence justification.

**Limitations and gaps.** What the hunt could not see, and what that means for the conclusion.

**Handoffs.** Notes for incident response, detection engineering, data engineering or asset owners, with ticket references.

## Separating observation, enrichment and inference

The most important writing discipline in hunt documentation is to label every statement by what kind of claim it is. Three kinds appear in almost every hunt.

**Source observation.** Something a log source recorded. "Proxy row P06: WS-203 requested invoice-viewer.example at 09:10:00Z." The reader can check it against the saved row.

**Enrichment.** Context added from another reference that did not observe the event, such as an asset inventory, a user directory, a threat intelligence feed or a change calendar. "Asset inventory (export dated 2026-10-01) lists WS-203 in the finance department." The reader should know the source and its date, because enrichment can be stale.

**Analyst inference.** A conclusion the analyst drew by combining observations and enrichment. "The Office child process on WS-203 at 09:11:30Z was probably triggered by opening the file downloaded at 09:10:00Z." The reader should be able to see what it rests on and judge it.

Mixing the three is the source of most misleading hunt reports. The sentence "Finance user's workstation downloaded a malicious invoice and ran a command shell" combines the asset inventory's assigned department with two observations: a proxy request and a shell starting. Those observations alone do not establish a completed download, the file's contents, or a causal link between the events. A reader cannot tell what was seen and what was guessed.

The Banyan case uses a specific evidence rule: assets associated from inventory are displayed as enrichment, not source observations. If an inventory record associates WS-104 with a particular user, a hunt record can mention it, labeled as enrichment with its date. It cannot be used to state that the user was at WS-104 on 2026-11-03, because no source in the reported dataset observed that.

## Calibrated language

Hunt documentation should use words that match the strength of the evidence. A short vocabulary used consistently helps readers.

- **"Observed" or "recorded"** for source observations.
- **"Consistent with"** when an observation fits a hypothesis but other explanations remain.
- **"Supports"** when observations from more than one source fit the hypothesis and the main benign alternatives have been checked.
- **"Not observed"** for a validated query that returned no results, with scope.
- **"Could not be determined"** when data is missing.
- **"Confirmed"** only when the evidence leaves no reasonable alternative, which is rare in hunting and usually requires incident response work beyond the hunt.

Words like "malicious," "compromised," "attacker" and "exfiltrated" are conclusions. They belong in a hunt record only when the evidence supports them, and even then with a citation. The 48,200,000 outbound bytes from WS-104 to files-share-drop.example should be documented as "48,200,000 outbound bytes recorded by the proxy; content unknown," not as "48 MB exfiltrated." The first is an observation. The second asserts intent and content that the data does not show.

Confidence levels should be defined once for the team and used the same way every time. A simple scheme: **high** when multiple independent sources agree and benign alternatives have been ruled out; **moderate** when sources agree but at least one plausible alternative remains open; **low** when only one source or one weak signal supports the hypothesis.

## Evidence citation

Every observation in a hunt record should be traceable to a specific saved record. A citation names the source and a record identifier: "Proxy, row P06" or "Sysmon, row E01" in the synthetic fixtures used in this module. In a real environment, record identifiers might be event IDs, SIEM document IDs, or a file path plus line number for exported logs.

Citations make the record reproducible and protect the analyst. If a reviewer questions a conclusion, the analyst can point to the rows. If a later analysis finds that a row was misparsed, the affected conclusions can be found and corrected.

For the Banyan data, a learner should cite the identifier used in the saved records. The evidence summaries give counts and timestamps without source row identifiers, so a hunt record should not invent them. Where only the summary is available, the citation should say so: "Saved-record summary of saved proxy records; individual row identifiers not yet cited."

Zero-result queries are cited too. The citation is the query text, its time range, its validation against a known positive, and the statement that it returned no rows.

## A worked hunt record

The record below documents the synthetic join query from Lesson 3. It is a synthetic example authored for this lesson. The rows it cites are from the Lesson 3 fixture, not from Banyan data, and the query was traced by hand, not executed.

```
Hunt ID:      HUNT-SYN-0007 (synthetic example)
Analyst:      [name]   Reviewer: [name]
Opened:       2026-10-14   Closed: 2026-10-15   Status: Closed - escalated

Trigger:      Lesson 3 rarity stack showed two domains on one host each.

Hypothesis:   If a downloaded file led to code execution on a finance
              workstation on 2026-10-14 09:00-10:00 UTC, we expect a proxy
              request to a domain seen on only one host, followed 0-10 min
              later on the same host by a process with an Office parent.
              Refuted if no such pair exists, or if each pair is a routine
              Office helper.

Scope:        WS-201..WS-205, 2026-10-14 09:00:00Z to 10:00:00Z.
              Excluded: none for priority. Row P11 (null host) could not be
              assigned to a host.

Data:         Proxy (12 rows, all 5 hosts, host/domain/bytes populated
              except P11 host). Endpoint process (4 rows, parent populated).
              Both UTC. No DHCP mapping available.

Baseline:     Same-window prevalence across 5 hosts. No historical baseline.

Query:        Rare-domain join, version 2026-10-14a (text in appendix).
              Expected positive: rare download + Office child within 10 min.
              Expected negative: Office child on host without rare download.

Results:      Rare domains: invoice-viewer.example, sync-relay.example.
              Candidate pairs: 1 (P06 + E01).
              Not matched: P10 (no Office child after), E03 (no rare download).
              Unresolved: P11 (null host, 10.20.5.77).

Analysis:     OBSERVED: P06 WS-203 -> invoice-viewer.example 09:10:00Z.
              OBSERVED: E01 WS-203 WINWORD.EXE -> cmd.exe 09:11:30Z.
              OBSERVED: P10 WS-203 -> sync-relay.example 09:40:00Z,
                        3,400,000 bytes out.
              ENRICHMENT: inventory (2026-10-01) lists WS-203 in finance.
              INFERENCE: E01 is consistent with execution following the
                        P06 download. Benign alternative (routine helper)
                        assessed: cmd.exe is not a routine Office child in
                        the fixture baseline.

Outcome:      Positive for escalation. Confidence: moderate (two sources
              agree on host and order; file content and command line unknown).

Gaps:         No command line in E01. No DHCP for P11. No mail data.

Handoffs:     IR: review WS-203 from 09:10Z. DE: candidate rule for rare
              download + non-routine Office child. Data: request DHCP logs.
```

Notice what the record does. It states the hypothesis and refuting evidence before results. It lists the unmatched and unresolved rows rather than only the match. It labels each analysis line. It gives a confidence level with a reason. It records gaps and hands off three separate actions.

## Writing for each audience

A single hunt record serves several readers. Short, targeted handoff notes help each of them act without reading everything.

**Incident response** needs the entity, time window, evidence citations, confidence and what is unknown. They do not need the baseline methodology in detail.

**Detection engineering** needs the behavior, the query, its expected positive and negative, its false-positive sources and the data source requirements. A hunt query is a rule candidate, not a rule; the handoff should say what tuning the hunt suggests.

**Data engineering** needs the specific visibility gap and the hunting question it blocked. "No DHCP logs: could not assign 10.20.5.77 to a host for a rare-domain transfer" is more persuasive than "please add DHCP logs."

**Management** needs the outcome, confidence and any business impact in plain language, plus a count of hunts closed per outcome over time.

## Documenting negative and no-data results

A benign or refuted hunt should be documented with the same structure. The results section states that the predictions were not observed or were explained, and the analysis section explains how. The value lies in the scope statement, which should be precise enough that a future analyst can decide whether to repeat the hunt: "Not observed in WS-201 to WS-205, 2026-10-14 09:00 to 10:00 UTC, proxy and endpoint process sources, query validated against seeded positive."

A no-data result should name the missing source and the question it blocked. For the Banyan VPN thread: "Could not be determined whether the gopal session that authenticated at 06:46:00Z from 203.0.113.66 accessed any resource: VPN records contain no later session activity and no endpoint identifier. No other source in the saved dataset records VPN session activity." That sentence states the question, the gap and the consequence, and it does not suggest either guilt or innocence.

## Bounded case reasoning

Two synthetic report excerpts follow, authored for this lesson.

**Excerpt A:** "Attacker brute-forced VPN and logged in as gopal, then pivoted to WS-104 to steal 48 MB of payroll data." Every clause overreaches. "Attacker" and "brute-forced" are inferences stated as facts. "Pivoted to WS-104" asserts a link that no shared entity supports. "Payroll data" takes content from a domain name. "Steal" asserts intent. The only observations it reflects are the VPN failure pattern, the gopal success and a byte count.

**Excerpt B:** "VPN records show failed authentication for eight distinct identities from 203.0.113.66 and a successful authentication for gopal at 06:46:00Z (saved-record summary; row identifiers to be cited). This is consistent with password guessing across accounts; a shared-egress explanation has not been ruled out. Subsequent session activity could not be determined. No available session record connects the successful gopal authentication with the WS-104 proxy or endpoint activity." Every claim is typed, sourced and bounded.

## Common errors

- **Documenting only positive results** and losing negative and no-data outcomes.
- **Describing queries instead of copying them**, so they cannot be rerun.
- **Mixing observation, enrichment and inference** in single sentences.
- **Using conclusion words** such as "malicious" or "exfiltrated" without supporting evidence.
- **Omitting unmatched and unresolved rows** so reviewers cannot see what was set aside.
- **Inventing record identifiers** when only a summary is available.
- **One undifferentiated handoff** instead of targeted notes for each audience.

## Practical exercise

Using the synthetic VPN fixture from Lesson 3 (rows V01 to V08), write a complete hunt record for the hypothesis "a single source attempted authentication across several identities within ten minutes on 2026-10-14."

Your record must:

1. Include every section of the hunt record.
2. Label each analysis line as OBSERVED, ENRICHMENT or INFERENCE.
3. Cite fixture rows for each observation.
4. Record 198.51.100.23 as a benign comparison with its reasoning.
5. State an outcome and confidence for 192.0.2.140 with a benign alternative.
6. Write separate handoff notes for incident response and detection engineering.

Then rewrite this synthetic sentence in calibrated, labeled form: "Hacker used noor's account after guessing passwords for four users."

**Model answer notes.** The record should cite V04 to V07 as four distinct failed identities from 192.0.2.140 between 07:02:00Z and 07:03:05Z, and V08 as a success for noor at 07:03:30Z. 198.51.100.23 shows one identity, amara, failing twice then succeeding (V01 to V03), consistent with a mistyped password. The outcome for 192.0.2.140 is positive for escalation at moderate confidence, with a shared-egress benign alternative open. The rewritten sentence might read: "OBSERVED: four distinct identities failed from 192.0.2.140 (V04 to V07), followed by a success for noor (V08). INFERENCE: consistent with guessing across accounts; whether the noor session was used by the same party could not be determined from authentication records alone."

## Keep evidence references reproducible

The saved Banyan JSON Lines file does not give every record a globally unique event identifier. Cite the file, source, UTC timestamp and observed entity, with the exact selection used if several records have the same time. A recorded line number can help locate a row in the preserved copy, provided the reviewer knows that a reformatted or filtered copy can change the line numbers. Do not present a local worksheet label as a source-issued identifier. If you assign a label to help order the exercise, state that it is a teaching label and keep the original fields beside it.

Try the method with the two WS-104 records covered by the query lesson. Cite the proxy request at 06:12:00Z and the Sysmon event at 06:14:00Z as separate source observations. Explain why reporting a same-host sequence is supportable, and separately record the question of whether the particular file caused the process. A second analyst should be able to find both records and test the scope without referencing the internal notes used to write the lesson.

## Key takeaways

- A hunt record makes the hunt reproducible, reviewable, actionable and cumulative.
- Document every outcome, including benign and no-data results, with precise scope.
- Label every claim as observation, enrichment or inference, and treat inventory associations as enrichment.
- Use calibrated language and defined confidence levels, and keep conclusion words for supported conclusions.
- Cite records by source and identifier, and do not invent identifiers when only a summary exists.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

The figure presents the hunt record as a table of sections, showing what each section must contain and the omission that most often weakens it. The rows run in the order a record is written, from trigger to handoffs, and the colors group them into three phases: blue for the planning sections written before data is examined, violet for the execution sections that capture queries and results, and green for the conclusion sections that carry outcome and handoffs.

The relationship to notice is that the blue sections constrain the violet ones. The hypothesis and scope written in the planning phase define what the queries are allowed to test, and the refuting evidence defines how results will be read. A record where the hypothesis appears to have been written after the results is a warning sign for reviewers, because it suggests the hunt was shaped to fit what was found.

The violet rows carry the reproducibility. Queries must be copied as text with a version, and results must include zero-result queries and unresolved rows. The most common omission in the results row is leaving out what did not match. Without it, a reviewer cannot tell whether the analyst considered and set aside other candidates or simply never saw them.

The green rows carry the record's usefulness to others. The outcome row needs a confidence level and a reason. The limitations row turns missing data into a visible gap. The handoff row splits actions by audience. As an example, the worked synthetic record HUNT-SYN-0007 in this lesson fills each row: its results list P10, E03 and P11 alongside the matched pair, and its handoffs give incident response, detection engineering and data engineering separate actions.
