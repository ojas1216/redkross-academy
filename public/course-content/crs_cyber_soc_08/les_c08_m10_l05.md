## ATT&CK as a hunting vocabulary

MITRE ATT&CK is a publicly maintained knowledge base of adversary behavior, organized into tactics, which describe the adversary's goal at a stage of activity, and techniques, which describe how a goal is pursued. Examples of tactic names include Initial Access, Execution, Credential Access, Command and Control and Exfiltration. Under each tactic, technique entries describe behaviors, give examples of reported use, and list data sources that may help observe them.

For a hunter, ATT&CK is most valuable as a shared vocabulary. It gives a team a consistent way to say what behavior a hunt is about, to compare hunts over time, to communicate with threat intelligence and detection engineering, and to see which behaviors have received no attention. It is less valuable, and sometimes harmful, when it is treated as a checklist to complete or as proof that an observed event is malicious.

This lesson shows how to move from an ATT&CK behavior to a hunt hypothesis and observable predictions, how to choose which behaviors to hunt, and how to map an observation back to ATT&CK honestly. It deliberately does not cite technique identifiers. Technique IDs and names are revised between ATT&CK versions, and a lesson that quotes them from memory risks teaching outdated labels. When a hunt record cites a technique, the analyst should look up the current entry on the ATT&CK site at the time of writing and record the ATT&CK version used.

The lesson also stays on the defender's side of each behavior. It describes what a behavior looks like in saved records, not how to carry it out.

## From tactic to observable: the translation chain

An ATT&CK-driven hunt follows a chain with five links. Each link narrows the question and makes it more testable.

**1. Tactic.** The adversary goal, for example Credential Access: obtaining account credentials.

**2. Behavior.** A specific way of pursuing the goal, described in plain language. For example: trying a small number of common passwords against many accounts from one source, so that no single account locks out.

**3. Observable.** What that behavior leaves in records. For the example: many authentication failures across distinct identities from one source address within a short period, possibly followed by a success.

**4. Data source.** Where those records live. For the example: VPN or identity provider authentication logs with source address, identity and result fields.

**5. Hypothesis.** The testable statement for the local environment: "If guessing across many accounts from one external source occurred against Banyan VPN on 2026-11-03, we expect one source address with failures across several distinct identities within a short period. If failures are spread across many addresses, or one identity accounts for most failures, the hypothesis is not supported."

Each link is a place where the hunt can stop early. If no data source in the inventory records the observable, the chain ends at link four with a visibility gap. That is a useful result: it tells the team which ATT&CK behaviors it cannot currently hunt.

The chain also prevents a common mistake, which is jumping from tactic to query. A query written directly from "Credential Access" is either too broad to be useful or quietly assumes one behavior out of many. Writing the behavior and observable first makes the assumption visible.

## Choosing which behaviors to hunt

ATT&CK lists hundreds of behaviors. No team can hunt all of them, and trying to "cover the matrix" leads to shallow hunts that test nothing well. Selection should weigh three factors.

**Threat relevance.** Which behaviors are reported against organizations like yours? Sector intelligence, shared incident reports and the team's own incident history are the main inputs. A microfinance lender with remote staff on VPN has good reason to care about credential guessing against remote access and about data leaving finance workstations.

**Data visibility.** Which behaviors produce observables in sources you actually have? This is the data-needs analysis from Lesson 2, applied per behavior. A behavior that is highly relevant but invisible in current data is a collection priority, not a hunt.

**Detection gaps.** Which relevant, visible behaviors do current detections not cover? Hunting where a mature, tested rule already exists adds little. Hunting where no rule exists can both find activity and produce a detection candidate.

A coverage heat map that colors ATT&CK cells by "has a rule" or "was hunted" can help communicate these gaps, but its colors are easy to misread. A green cell for a technique might mean one narrow rule that catches one tool, while the behavior has many variants. Treat a heat map as a conversation starter, and record in the hunt documentation exactly which behavior variant was tested.

## Writing observables at the right level

ATT&CK technique entries often describe many procedures that share a goal. A hunt observable should be written at the level of the shared behavior, so that it catches variants, while staying specific enough to discriminate.

Consider document-driven execution, a behavior many intrusions begin with: a user opens a document, and the document application starts another program. The observables could be written at three levels:

- **Too specific:** a particular word processor starting a particular command interpreter with a particular argument. This catches one procedure and misses the rest.
- **Too general:** any process started on a workstation. This has no discriminating power.
- **Behavior level:** an Office application as parent of a process that is not a known routine helper, on a workstation, shortly after a download. This catches variants and still discriminates.

The behavior level depends on a baseline of routine Office children, which is why ATT&CK-driven hunts and baselining work together. The ATT&CK entry suggests the behavior; the baseline tells the hunter which instances of it are ordinary locally.

## Applying the chain to the Banyan threads

The saved-record Banyan facts can be examined through this chain. The aim is to generate hypotheses and observables, not to assign labels to events as findings.

**WS-104 proxy and endpoint thread.** The saved proxy and endpoint records show a proxy request from WS-104 to banyan-payroll-update.example at 06:12:00Z, an Office child process at 06:14, 18 requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example between 06:17:00Z and 07:42:02Z, and a request to files-share-drop.example at 08:12:00Z with 48,200,000 outbound bytes.

Each observation suggests a tactic that an analyst might hypothesize:

- The download and Office child process suggest hypotheses under Initial Access and Execution, specifically document-driven execution.
- Repeated requests to one destination suggest a hypothesis under Command and Control, if the destination is rare and the timing is regular.
- A large outbound transfer to a file-sharing destination suggests a hypothesis under Exfiltration.

These are candidate tactics, not conclusions. Each has a benign alternative. An Office child process may be a routine helper. Repeated requests to a telemetry-named domain may be a legitimate agent. A large upload may be a sanctioned file share used by the business. The hunt's job is to test each hypothesis against the data and the baseline.

**VPN thread.** The saved records show that the VPN summary for 203.0.113.66 contains eight distinct identities that failed authentication and a successful authentication for gopal at 06:46:00Z. This fits the observable for guessing across many accounts under Credential Access, and the success raises a hypothesis about a valid account being used for access. The benign alternatives include a shared egress address for a partner or a hotel network where several staff mistype passwords, though eight distinct identities failing in a short period from one address is not a common benign pattern. The VPN records contain no later session activity, so any hypothesis about what the gopal session did afterwards ends at link four with a no-data outcome.

## Sequence does not prove connection

ATT&CK tactics are often drawn left to right, and it is tempting to read a series of observations as a story that walks across the matrix. That reading is a hypothesis about connection, and it must be tested like any other.

In the Banyan data, the two threads sit near each other in time on the same morning. The WS-104 thread can be arranged as Execution, then Command and Control, then Exfiltration. The VPN thread can be read as Credential Access followed by account use. Putting them together suggests a single intrusion: guess a password, log in, reach WS-104, take data. But the available records do not connect gopal's successful VPN session to WS-104. The VPN events have no endpoint identifier, the proxy and endpoint events do not reference the VPN identity, and there is no session data showing the VPN user reaching any host. The failed accounts include divya, who is also named in the WS-104 evidence. That overlapping identity does not link gopal's successful VPN session to WS-104.

The correct position is that two separate hypotheses each have supporting observations, and the connection between them is untested and, with this data, untestable. Writing it any other way would turn ATT&CK's tactic ordering into evidence, which it is not. Many real intrusions skip tactics, repeat them or carry them out in an order different from the matrix layout, and many unrelated events fall near each other in time.

Even within one thread, sequence needs support. The WS-104 events share a host, which is a real link. But sharing a host does not prove the events are causally connected: a workstation can be used for unrelated tasks within two hours. The sequence strengthens the hypothesis; it does not settle it.

## Mapping observations back to ATT&CK with confidence

When a hunt produces a result, teams often record which ATT&CK behavior it relates to. Three practices make that mapping honest.

**State confidence.** Use a simple scale, such as low, moderate or high, and say why. A rare download followed within two minutes by a non-routine Office child process on the same host might be mapped to document-driven execution with moderate confidence. Without payload or command-line evidence, high confidence is not justified.

**State the benign alternative.** For each mapping, write the most plausible innocent explanation and whether it was ruled out. This keeps the mapping a hypothesis rather than a verdict.

**Map behaviors, not events.** A single proxy request is not a technique. A pattern of requests with rarity and regularity might be consistent with a Command and Control behavior. The mapping should describe the pattern and the evidence that supports it.

A mapping recorded this way is useful downstream. Detection engineering can see exactly which behavior variant was observed and in which data source. Threat intelligence can compare it with reporting. Incident response can see which parts are supported and which are inferred.

## Bounded case reasoning

Three synthetic mapping drafts follow, authored for this lesson; none is Banyan source data.

**Draft 1:** "Proxy row 118: Exfiltration." This maps a single event to a tactic with no confidence, no behavior and no benign alternative. It should be rewritten as: "WS-207 sent 39 MB in one request to a destination no peer used in the baseline window (proxy row 118). Consistent with an Exfiltration hypothesis, moderate confidence by volume and rarity; content unknown. Benign alternative: personal file sharing or an unsanctioned business tool; owner not yet contacted."

**Draft 2:** "Brute force attack from 198.51.100.40, user compromised." This states a conclusion and uses a label without stating the observable. A better version gives the count of distinct identities, the time span, whether any success followed and what data would show account use afterwards.

**Draft 3:** "Kill chain complete: Initial Access via VPN, Execution on WS-209, Exfiltration to sync-relay.example." This joins three observations into one intrusion. Unless a shared entity connects the VPN identity to WS-209, the draft should be split into separate observations with the link marked as untested.

## Common errors

- **Hunting the matrix instead of the threat**, spreading effort across behaviors with no local relevance.
- **Quoting technique IDs from memory** without checking the current ATT&CK version.
- **Mapping single events to tactics** without describing the behavior or pattern.
- **Treating tactic order as evidence** of a connected intrusion.
- **Writing observables at the tool level** so that variants of the behavior are missed.
- **Reading a green heat-map cell as complete coverage** when only one variant is detected.
- **Omitting confidence and benign alternatives** from mappings.

## Practical exercise

Work on paper. No query is run.

Choose two behaviors in plain language: guessing passwords across many accounts from one source, and repeated requests from one workstation to a rare destination at regular intervals.

For each behavior:

1. Name the ATT&CK tactic it relates to. If you want to cite a technique, note that you would look up the current entry and version before recording it; do not write an ID from memory.
2. Write the observable in terms of fields and values.
3. Name the source in the saved-record Banyan inventory that would record it, or state that none does.
4. Write a local hypothesis with refuting evidence.
5. Apply it to the Banyan facts given in this lesson and state the confidence of any mapping, the benign alternative, and what remains untested.

Then write two or three sentences explaining why the VPN thread and the WS-104 thread should be recorded as separate hypotheses.

**Model answer notes.** The guessing behavior relates to Credential Access; the observable is failures across distinct identities from one address in a short window; the source is VPN authentication records; on the Banyan summary, eight distinct failed identities from 203.0.113.66 and a success for gopal at 06:46:00Z support the hypothesis at moderate confidence, with a shared-egress benign alternative not yet ruled out, and post-authentication activity untested. The repeated-request behavior relates to Command and Control; the observable is many requests from one host to a destination rare among peers, with regular intervals; the source is proxy records; the 18 requests from WS-104 to cdn-telemetry-sync.example support only a low-to-moderate confidence hypothesis until prevalence is checked and individual intervals are read. The threads should stay separate because the records do not connect gopal's successful session to WS-104, and ATT&CK tactic ordering is not evidence of connection.

## Key takeaways

- Use ATT&CK as a vocabulary: tactic, behavior, observable, data source, hypothesis.
- Choose behaviors by threat relevance, data visibility and detection gaps, not by matrix coverage.
- Write observables at the behavior level so variants are caught and routine activity is excluded by baseline.
- Map observations with stated confidence and a benign alternative, and verify any technique citation against the current ATT&CK version.
- Tactic order is not evidence; observations need a shared entity before they are linked.
