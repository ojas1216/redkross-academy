## Why hunting starts with a hypothesis

Threat hunting is the proactive search for adversary activity that existing detections did not alert on. A hunt is different from an alert triage: in triage, a rule has already fired and the analyst decides what it means. An earlier alert can motivate a wider hunt. The hunter decides what to look for, where to search and what evidence would support or refute the hypothesis. Without that decision made up front, a hunt drifts into browsing. The analyst opens a SIEM, scrolls through events, notices things that look odd, and spends an afternoon without being able to say what was tested or what was ruled out.

A hypothesis is the tool that prevents that drift. It is a short, testable statement about a behavior that might be present in a defined part of the environment, written so that the data can support it, contradict it, or fail to address it. The hypothesis does three jobs at once. It tells the analyst which records to pull. It tells a reviewer what the hunt was and was not about. And it tells the team what to do next, because a supported, refuted, or untestable hypothesis each leads to a different follow-up.

This lesson treats hypothesis design as its own skill. Later lessons in the module cover the data a hypothesis needs, the queries that test it, the baselines that give its predictions meaning, ATT&CK as a source of behaviors, documentation, and validation. All of those depend on getting the hypothesis right first. A weak hypothesis cannot be rescued by a clever query.

## The anatomy of a testable hypothesis

A useful hunt hypothesis has five parts. Not every team writes them in the same order, but a hypothesis missing any of them is usually hard to test.

**Behavior.** What would an adversary, or a misused account, actually be doing? Behaviors are verbs about systems: a document application starting a command interpreter, one source address failing authentication for many different accounts, a workstation sending far more data outbound than it usually does. A behavior is not a threat actor name, a malware family, or a feeling that something is wrong.

**Scope.** Which entities and which time window are in play? Scope might be "all Windows workstations in the finance subnet" or "VPN authentication events on 2026-11-03 UTC." Scope keeps the hunt finite and makes the result interpretable. A hypothesis about "the network" over "recent weeks" cannot be cleanly refuted.

**Observable predictions.** If the behavior happened in scope, what would the records show? A good hypothesis has at least two predictions, ideally from different sources, because one prediction alone often has many innocent causes. Predictions name fields and values, such as "a proxy request from the host to a domain not seen in the prior 30 days, followed within 10 minutes by an endpoint process event whose parent is an Office application."

**Refuting evidence.** What result would make the analyst say the hypothesis is not supported? Writing this before looking at data is the single most effective guard against confirmation bias. If the analyst cannot state what would refute the hypothesis, the hypothesis is not yet testable.

**Data feasibility.** Which sources would hold the predicted observations, and are they available for the scope? This part is covered in depth in the next lesson, but it belongs in the hypothesis statement because it decides whether the hunt can produce a supported or refuted outcome at all, or only a no-data outcome.

A compact template many teams use is: "If [behavior] occurred on [scope] during [window], we expect to see [prediction A] in [source] and [prediction B] in [source]. If we see [refuting result], the hypothesis is not supported."

## Where hypotheses come from

Hypotheses are not invented from nothing. They come from a handful of recurring sources, and knowing the source helps the analyst judge how much weight to give the result.

**Threat intelligence.** A report describes a behavior relevant to the organization's sector, such as attackers sending invoice-themed documents to finance staff. The analyst turns the reported behavior into a local question: did any finance workstation open an Office document and then spawn an unusual child process in the reporting window? Intelligence-driven hypotheses inherit the uncertainty of the report, so the hunt should test the behavior, not just the indicators in the report, which may have already changed.

**ATT&CK behaviors.** The ATT&CK knowledge base describes adversary tactics and behaviors in a common vocabulary. A hunter can pick a behavior that the organization has little detection coverage for and ask whether it is present. Lesson 5 covers this in detail.

**Situational awareness.** Something changed in the environment: a new remote access product was rolled out, a merger added unmanaged laptops, or a team began working from a new region. Changes create both new attack surface and new noise, and a hypothesis can target either.

**Prior incidents and near misses.** If an earlier incident involved a specific behavior, the team can hunt for the same behavior elsewhere to check whether it was isolated. This is one of the most productive hypothesis sources because the behavior is known to be realistic for the environment.

**Baseline anomalies.** A frequency analysis shows a rare value, such as a domain contacted by only one host. Anomalies are prompts, not hypotheses. The analyst still has to state what behavior the anomaly might represent and what else would be true if it did.

**Analyst observation during other work.** During triage of an unrelated alert, an analyst notices something that does not fit. Writing it down as a hypothesis, rather than chasing it immediately, lets the team prioritize it alongside other work.

## Hypothesis versus conclusion

The most common design error is writing a conclusion and calling it a hypothesis. "WS-104 was compromised by a phishing document" is a conclusion. It names a cause (phishing), a mechanism (a document), and an outcome (compromise), and in the saved Banyan dataset, none of the three can be directly observed. There is no mail gateway log, no mailbox audit, and no direct payload evidence. A hunt organized around that statement will either reach a conclusion it cannot support or quietly redefine success along the way.

The testable version narrows to what the records can show. The saved-record Banyan facts describe a proxy request from WS-104 to banyan-payroll-update.example at 06:12:00Z on 2026-11-03, an Office child process recorded by endpoint data at 06:14, eighteen requests to cdn-telemetry-sync.example from 06:17:00Z through 07:42:02Z, and one request to files-share-drop.example at 08:12:00Z with 48,200,000 outbound bytes recorded by the proxy. A testable hypothesis would read:

"If a downloaded file led to code execution on WS-104 on 2026-11-03, we expect a proxy download shortly before an endpoint process event with an Office parent, and later outbound requests from WS-104 to destinations not seen on peer workstations. If no Office child process follows the download, or if the later destinations are common across peers, the hypothesis is not supported."

Notice what this version does not claim. It does not say how the file arrived, because the dataset cannot show mail. It does not say what was in the 48,200,000 bytes, because payload is unknown. It does not say the activity was malicious; it states what pattern would be consistent with execution and what would contradict it.

## Scoping: entities and time

Scope is where many otherwise good hypotheses fail. Two scoping decisions matter most.

**Entity scope.** Choose the population that the behavior could plausibly affect and that the data covers. If endpoint telemetry exists for workstations but not servers, a hypothesis about Office child processes should be scoped to workstations, and the documentation should say servers were out of scope because of data, not because they are safe. Entity scope also controls false linking. In the Banyan brief, the VPN thread involves identities authenticating from 203.0.113.66, including a successful authentication for gopal at 06:46, while the proxy and endpoint thread involves WS-104. No observed client or session identifies WS-104 as the source of gopal's successful VPN authentication: the VPN events carry no endpoint identifier and no later session activity. A hypothesis that assumes gopal used WS-104 has smuggled in a link the data does not contain.

**Time scope.** Choose a window wide enough to include the predicted sequence and its plausible precursors, but narrow enough that rare events remain rare. A window of a few hours around the Banyan proxy events is appropriate for testing a download-then-execution sequence. A baseline window, discussed in Lesson 4, is a separate and usually longer period used to decide what is normal. State all times in UTC and say so; mixing local time and UTC is a frequent source of false sequences.

## Writing predictions that discriminate

A prediction is useful when it is more likely to be observed if the hypothesis is true than if it is false. Analysts sometimes call this the discriminating power of a prediction. "WS-104 made proxy requests" has almost no discriminating power, because every workstation makes proxy requests. "WS-104 made a request to a domain first seen that day, followed within minutes by an Office application starting a child process" is much stronger, because benign workstations rarely produce that pair.

Three techniques improve discrimination:

1. **Combine sources.** A prediction that requires agreement between proxy and endpoint data is harder to satisfy by coincidence than one that requires a single source.
2. **Specify order and timing.** A download followed by execution within ten minutes is more specific than a download and an execution on the same day.
3. **Specify rarity.** A destination seen on one host is more interesting than one seen on every host. Rarity needs a baseline, which is why hypothesis design and baselining are linked.

Discrimination has a cost. Very specific predictions miss variants. If the hypothesis requires the Office parent to be a word processor, it will miss a spreadsheet parent. The analyst should write predictions at the level of the behavior, not the tool, and note deliberate exclusions.

## Writing refuting evidence first

Before pulling data, write down the results that would count against the hypothesis. For the WS-104 example, refuting results include: the endpoint process event precedes the download, which breaks the proposed order; the Office child process is a known update helper that appears on most workstations at that time each day; or the later destinations appear on many peer workstations with similar volumes.

For a VPN hypothesis such as "a single external source attempted authentication against many Banyan identities in a short period," refuting results include: the failures are spread across many source addresses, the identities are all the same account retrying, or the source address belongs to a known partner gateway that aggregates many legitimate users. Writing these first does two things. It keeps the analyst honest when a result is ambiguous, and it tells the reviewer what alternatives were considered.

Refutation is not failure. A refuted hypothesis is a completed hunt that has reduced uncertainty. The team now knows that, in the stated scope and window, the behavior was not present in the available data, and it can record that as a negative result.

## The three outcomes and what they mean

Every hypothesis should be designed so it can end in one of three outcomes, which the course uses throughout the module.

**Positive (supported).** The predictions were observed and the refuting results were not. A positive outcome is not proof of compromise; it means the pattern is present and warrants escalation or further validation.

**Benign (refuted or explained).** The predictions were not observed, or they were observed but a validated benign explanation accounts for them, such as a scheduled software update confirmed by an asset owner.

**No-data (untestable).** The required data was missing, incomplete, or could not be joined, so the hypothesis could not be tested. A no-data outcome is a real finding about visibility and should be recorded with the specific gap.

The Banyan VPN thread illustrates a likely no-data outcome for some questions. The hypothesis "the successful gopal authentication at 06:46 was followed by unusual session activity" cannot be tested because the VPN records contain no later session activity. The hunt can still test a narrower hypothesis about the authentication pattern itself, which the saved-record VPN summary does support: eight distinct identities failed from 203.0.113.66 and gopal succeeded at 06:46:00Z.

## Prioritizing hypotheses

Teams usually have more hypotheses than time. A simple scoring approach weighs three factors.

**Impact.** If the behavior were present, how bad would it be? Data leaving a finance workstation scores high at a microfinance organization.

**Plausibility.** How likely is the behavior given threat intelligence, prior incidents, and environment? A behavior seen in a recent sector report and consistent with saved anomalies scores higher than a theoretical one.

**Feasibility.** Can the available data test it? A high-impact hypothesis that only cloud identity audit logs could answer, when the dataset has none, scores low on feasibility. The analyst should still record it as a visibility gap, because the gap itself is worth fixing.

Ranking is a judgment call, and the justification matters more than the numbers. A short sentence for each factor is enough for a reviewer to agree or disagree.

## Bounded case reasoning

Consider three synthetic hypothesis drafts from a fictional review session. These are authored for this lesson and are not Banyan source data.

**Draft A:** "Check if anything bad happened on finance laptops last month." This names no behavior, no prediction, and no refuting result. Its scope is vague. It should be rejected and rewritten.

**Draft B:** "If an account was used from a new external address, we expect a successful VPN authentication from an address not seen for that account in the prior 30 days, and a failure burst from the same address within the preceding hour." This is testable if VPN data covers 31 days. It scores well on discrimination because it combines novelty with a preceding failure burst. Its refuting result is a successful authentication with no prior failures from the same address, which is more consistent with travel than with guessing.

**Draft C:** "Attackers exfiltrated customer data from WS-104 to files-share-drop.example." This is a conclusion. The proxy count of 48,200,000 outbound bytes shows volume, not content, and the payload is unknown. The testable rewrite asks whether WS-104's outbound volume to that destination departs from the peer baseline and whether the destination appears elsewhere.

The reasoning is bounded because each judgment refers to what the stated records could show. None of the drafts is improved by speculation about attacker intent.

## Common errors

- **Writing conclusions instead of hypotheses.** The statement names a cause the data cannot observe.
- **No refuting evidence.** Any result can be read as support, so the hunt cannot end.
- **Scope too broad.** "All systems, all time" produces unmanageable noise and an unfalsifiable result.
- **Tool-level predictions.** Predicting a specific filename misses variants of the same behavior.
- **Assumed links between entities.** Treating two threads as one intrusion without a shared entity in the records.
- **Indicators instead of behaviors.** Searching only for a reported domain finds nothing once the adversary changes infrastructure.
- **Ignoring feasibility.** Designing a hunt that the available data cannot answer, then reporting "nothing found" as if it were a negative result.

## Practical exercise

Work on paper or in a text file. No queries are run in this exercise.

The following hunt triggers are synthetic and authored for this lesson:

1. A sector intelligence note says invoice-themed documents are being used against microfinance lenders.
2. A frequency count shows one workstation contacted a domain that no other workstation contacted in the last 30 days.
3. A VPN summary shows a burst of failures across many identities from one external address, followed by one success.

For each trigger:

- Write a hypothesis using the five-part structure: behavior, scope, at least two observable predictions with sources, refuting evidence, and data feasibility.
- State which of the three outcomes would be most likely if the data inventory contained only proxy, endpoint process, Windows event, and VPN authentication records, with no mail, cloud identity, or session data.
- Rank the three hypotheses by impact, plausibility, and feasibility in one sentence each.

**Model answer notes.** Trigger 1 cannot be tested at the delivery step without mail data, so the hypothesis should shift to the observable consequence, an Office child process after a download, and record mail delivery as a gap. Trigger 2 needs a stated behavior: rarity alone is a prompt. A good rewrite predicts a download-then-execution sequence or unusual outbound volume. Trigger 3 is directly testable for the authentication pattern, but any prediction about post-authentication activity will produce a no-data outcome because session records are absent.

## Key takeaways

- A hunt hypothesis is a testable statement about a behavior in a defined scope, with observable predictions and stated refuting evidence.
- Write refuting evidence before looking at data to guard against confirmation bias.
- Predictions gain discriminating power by combining sources, specifying order and timing, and specifying rarity.
- Hypotheses must stay within what the records can show; in the Banyan data, mail delivery, payload content and post-VPN session activity cannot be observed.
- Every hypothesis should be able to end positive, benign or no-data, and each outcome is a legitimate result.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

The figure shows hypothesis design as a top-to-bottom flow with a deliberate checkpoint before any data is touched. It starts with a trigger, which is the reason the hunt exists: an intelligence note, an anomaly, a change, or a prior incident. The trigger is not yet a question. The second step converts it into a behavior statement, which forces the analyst to name what a system would actually do, rather than who might be responsible.

The third step adds scope, meaning the entities and the UTC time window. Scope and behavior together define the population the predictions apply to. The fourth step lists observable predictions, each tied to a named source. The fifth step, shown in orange, is the checkpoint: refuting evidence must be written down before the data is pulled. Its color marks it as the step most often skipped and the one that most protects the hunt from confirmation bias.

The sixth step is a feasibility check. If the sources that would hold the predictions are missing, the flow leads directly to a no-data outcome, and the hunt records a visibility gap rather than a negative result. If the sources exist, the hypothesis is tested and the result lands in one of three outcomes at the bottom: positive, benign, or no-data.

As an example, take the saved-record WS-104 thread. The trigger is a rare destination. The behavior is "a downloaded file led to code execution." Scope is WS-104 on 2026-11-03 UTC. Predictions are a proxy download followed by an Office child process within minutes, and later rare outbound destinations. Refuting evidence is the process preceding the download or the destinations being common on peers. Feasibility passes for proxy and endpoint data but fails for mail delivery, so delivery stays a recorded gap while the execution sequence is testable.
