## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Earlier lessons in this module describe what an organisation should do during an incident. None of it is proven by being written down: an untested plan is a hypothesis. It assumes the contact list is current, people know their roles, the authority to isolate a payments workstation is clear, legal can be reached in the evening, and someone is watching the six-hour CERT-In clock. The first real incident is a poor time to find out which assumptions were wrong.

A **tabletop exercise** is the cheapest, safest way to test them. The people who would handle a real incident sit together while a facilitator walks them through a realistic scenario, releasing new information at intervals. At each step they discuss what they would do, who decides, what they need and whom they tell. Nothing is touched. The output is not a score but a list of gaps, each turned into an action with an owner and a date.

Tabletops matter for a SOC for three reasons. The SOC is usually where an incident starts, so its escalation, evidence and timeline are what every other participant works from; an exercise shows whether that material is good enough for a business owner, a lawyer or an executive. Many incident failures are not technical: organisations detect the problem but escalate late, tell the wrong people, or let a well-meaning technician destroy evidence, and a tabletop is designed to surface exactly those decision and coordination failures. And exercises are expected of a mature programme: NIST SP 800-84, *Guide to Test, Training, and Exercise Programs for IT Plans and Capabilities* (2006), describes tabletop and functional exercises as ways to validate IT plans, and auditors increasingly ask not "do you have a plan?" but "when did you last test it, and what did you change?".

This lesson teaches you to design, run and evaluate a tabletop, and ends with a ready-to-run scenario built on the Banyan Microfinance incident. Lesson 8 then works the same incident in the lab: the discussion shows what decision-makers will ask the SOC for, and the lab shows whether the SOC can produce it.

> [!NOTE]
> A tabletop is a discussion exercise. Participants describe what they *would* do; they do not do it. Any exercise that touches live systems (isolating a test host, restoring from backup, calling the real CERT-In contact) is an operations-based exercise and needs its own authorisation, change control and safety plan.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Exercise | A planned event in which people practise or test a plan, procedure or capability against a scenario. |
| Tabletop exercise (TTX) | A discussion-based exercise in which participants talk through their responses to a scenario presented by a facilitator. |
| Discussion-based exercise | Seminar, workshop, tabletop or game: tests plans, policies and decision-making by talking. |
| Operations-based exercise | Drill, functional or full-scale exercise: participants perform actions with real or simulated systems. |
| Scenario | The storyline of the exercise: a plausible incident tailored to the organisation's threats and systems. |
| Inject | A piece of new information released during the exercise (an alert, a phone call, an email, a demand) that moves the scenario forward and forces a decision. |
| MSEL | Master Scenario Events List: the timed, ordered list of injects, with the expected player actions for each. |
| Facilitator | The person who presents the scenario, releases injects, asks questions and keeps the discussion on time and on objective. |
| Player | A participant who responds in their real role (incident lead, SOC analyst, legal, communications, IT operations, business owner). |
| Evaluator / note-taker | A participant who does not play but records decisions, times, gaps and quotes against the objectives. |
| Hotwash | The short, immediate debrief at the end of the exercise, while memories are fresh. |
| AAR / IP | After-action report and improvement plan: the written result of the exercise, listing strengths, gaps and owned actions. |
| Scenario time | The time inside the story (for example 08:30 UTC on the day of the incident), as opposed to the real time of the session. |

The terms MSEL, hotwash and AAR/IP come from the US Homeland Security Exercise and Evaluation Program (HSEEP), which is widely used as a reference model for exercise design well beyond the United States. You do not need to adopt HSEEP in full, but its vocabulary is what most exercise packages, including the free tabletop packages published by CISA, use.

## Where a tabletop fits among exercise types

Exercises sit on a ladder of increasing realism, cost and risk. **Seminars** and **workshops** explain or draft plans. The **tabletop** is the first rung that tests decisions against a developing scenario. Above it are operations-based exercises: a **drill** performs one task for real (an EDR isolation on a test machine, a call-tree test), a **functional exercise** has teams perform their functions in a simulated environment, and a **full-scale exercise** mobilises real people and systems.

| Type | What is tested | Touches systems? | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Seminar / workshop | Awareness of the plan; draft procedures | No | Hours |
| Tabletop | Decisions, roles, escalation, communication, authority | No | Half a day including preparation |
| Drill | One procedure performed for real (isolate, restore, call tree) | Yes, in a controlled way | A day |
| Functional | Teams performing their functions against a simulated incident | Simulated environment | Weeks of preparation |
| Full-scale | The whole response, with real resources | Yes | Months of preparation |

Security teams also run **red team** and **purple team** exercises, which are operations-based and need written authorisation and rules of engagement (Course 05). They test whether you *can detect and act*; a tabletop tests whether you *know what to decide and who decides it*. A purple team exercise might show that the Banyan beacon is detected within minutes, while a tabletop shows that nobody knew who could authorise isolating the payments approver's workstation.

Climb the ladder in order: tabletop a plan before you drill it, because practising a procedure whose decision points nobody has agreed has little value. Because tabletops are cheap, run them most often: at least yearly per major scenario, and after any significant change to the plan, the organisation or the threat picture.

## Designing a tabletop

Good tabletops are designed backwards from what you need to learn. The scenario is a means, not the end. A dramatic scenario that tests nothing in particular produces an entertaining afternoon and an empty improvement plan.

### 1. Set objectives

Start with three to five objectives, each naming a capability and how an evaluator will judge it, drawn from the plan and from known worries (a new reporting procedure, a reorganisation, an audit finding). "Participants agree a declaration and severity within 15 minutes of scenario time of the first escalation" can be evaluated; "test the IR plan" cannot, so it produces no actions. The Banyan objectives below are worked examples.

### 2. Define scope, participants and constraints

Scope says which teams are in play (for example the SOC, IT operations, finance, legal and communications) and which are simulated (the regulator, the media, the attacker). Invite the people who hold the real roles, and their deputies, because incidents happen when the primary is on leave. Keep to roughly six to twelve players; beyond that, quieter roles disappear. Fix the duration (60 to 120 minutes for one scenario) and constraints such as recording and the classification of materials.

### 3. Write the scenario

A good scenario is **plausible** (it matches the real threat profile and systems), **specific** (real host names, business processes and times) and **escalating** (it starts ambiguous and grows more serious). Build it from things that have happened: your own incidents, sector reports or relevant threat intelligence. For a SOC the best source is often an anonymised past investigation, whose details are known to be realistic.

Two rules prevent common failures. The scenario must not depend on players making one specific choice: if inject 3 only makes sense when the host was isolated at inject 2, phrase it so that it works either way. And the facts in each inject must be unambiguous, so that players debate decisions rather than what a badly written alert means.

### 4. Build the injects and the MSEL

Each inject is a short message in a realistic form: a SIEM alert line, an analyst's escalation, a call from a business head, a customer email. For each one the MSEL records the scenario time, the recipient, the content, the decision it should provoke, the expected actions (what the plan says should happen) and the objective it serves. Players never see the expected actions.

Aim for one inject every ten to fifteen minutes, with the key decisions mid-session rather than at the end, when time pressure cuts discussion short. Prepare **contingency injects**: one to raise the pressure if the group moves too fast, and a hint if it is stuck.

### 5. Assign roles

Besides players, appoint one **facilitator** (who does not play), at least one **evaluator** per objective group, and a **controller** who plays outsiders (the regulator, a journalist, a supplier) when players "call" them. A **trusted agent** in each team can help design the scenario without playing. Observers do not speak.

### 6. Prepare logistics and ground rules

Prepare a short briefing, the plan and contact lists as they really are (not cleaned-up versions), a visible scenario clock and the evaluation sheets. State the ground rules at the start:

- It is a no-fault environment: the plan and the organisation are being tested, not individuals.
- Respond as you would in your real role, with the authority and information you would really have.
- Do not "fight the scenario": accept the injects as given, even if you think the attack is unlikely.
- Decisions made in the room are discussion outcomes, not commitments, unless they are later approved through the normal process.
- What is said in the room stays in the exercise record and the AAR.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the whole life of a tabletop exercise as seven ordered steps, from setting objectives to producing the after-action report, with a feedback arrow that closes the loop into the next exercise. It matters because most weak tabletops fail at the two ends of this chain: they skip step 1 (so nothing can be evaluated) or stop at step 6 (so the hotwash comments are never turned into actions).

**Components.** The steps run top to bottom. **1 Set objectives** (violet) is the control step: three to five capabilities with a test for each. **2 Scope + participants** (slate) fixes which teams play, which are simulated and who attends. **3 Scenario + MSEL** (blue) is the content: the storyline and the timed inject list with expected actions. **4 Roles + ground rules** (blue) assigns facilitator, players, evaluators and controller, and prepares the briefing. **5 Facilitate** (orange) is the live session, the step participants actually see. **6 Hotwash** (green) is the immediate debrief. **7 AAR + improvement plan** (dark) is the trust anchor of the whole process: the only durable output.

**Relationships and arrows.** Each solid arrow means "the output of this step is the input of the next". Objectives decide scope (you invite the people whose decisions the objectives test); scope constrains the scenario (it must exercise the teams that are in the room); the MSEL dictates which roles are needed; and so on. The dashed feedback arrow from step 7 back to step 1 carries the improvement actions: the next exercise should include an objective that tests whether last time's gaps were fixed.

**Execution flow.** Steps 1 to 4 happen in the weeks before the session and are invisible to players. Step 5 is the session itself, steps 6 and 7 follow immediately and within a few weeks respectively.

**Decision logic.** There are no branches in the lifecycle itself, but two judgements sit inside it. At step 3 the designer decides whether an inject needs a branch because the players' earlier choice might change its meaning. At step 7 the evaluators decide, objective by objective, whether each was met, partly met or not met, which determines what goes into the improvement plan.

**Technical interpretation.** The lifecycle is a small version of the plan-do-check-act loop that runs through security management: steps 1 to 4 plan, step 5 does, steps 6 and 7 check, and the feedback arrow acts.

**Practical example.** For Banyan, step 1 produced the objective "establish the CERT-In deadline in UTC and IST"; step 3 placed a legal inject at scenario time 08:50 UTC to test it; in step 5 the group calculated the deadline; in step 6 legal noted that nobody knew who held the CERT-In portal credentials; step 7 recorded that as an action with an owner and date, and the next exercise checks it.

**Security implications.** The MSEL and the AAR describe the organisation's weaknesses in detail and are sensitive documents; classify and store them accordingly.

**Key learning.** A tabletop is only as good as its first and last steps: evaluable objectives at the start and an owned improvement plan at the end.

## Facilitating the session

The facilitator keeps the group moving towards the objectives while letting the players do the thinking. It is a distinct skill: an incident lead often facilitates their own team badly because they cannot resist answering.

**Open with the frame.** Spend five to ten minutes on purpose, ground rules, roles and the starting situation, and warn that scenario time will jump: ninety minutes may cover six hours of incident.

**Release injects as the real channel would.** Read an alert aloud, hand out a printed escalation, or have the controller "phone" a player. An inject handed only to the service desk tests whether the service desk knows to escalate it.

**Ask, do not tell.** Ask open questions by role: "Incident lead, what is your decision and who must approve it?", "Legal, what do you need from the SOC first?", "SOC, how long would that query take, and how confident would you be?". Probe the plan: "Where is that written down?", "Who is your deputy?". When a player says "we would just do X", ask how, who and with what authority.

**Manage time and voices.** Time each inject; use a hint if discussion stalls and the pressure inject if it races. Direct questions by role so that one senior voice does not dominate.

**Do not rescue, do not punish.** Let an unsupported decision stand and ask what happens next: the gap is the finding. Restate the no-fault rule if blame starts.

**Capture decisions visibly** on a shared screen: scenario time, decision, decider, what was needed. It doubles as the single incident log of Lesson 2.

> [!TIP]
> Ask the SOC players to say aloud what they would need to run, and how long it would take, before you give them the answer in the next inject. It shows the other participants how long "just check the logs" really takes, and tests whether the SOC knows its own data (for example, that the VPN source carries no host field, so a VPN login cannot be tied to a device from VPN logs alone).

## Evaluating the exercise

Evaluation turns a discussion into evidence. It has three parts.

**During the session**, evaluators record against each objective what was decided, when (scenario time), by whom, with what information, what was missing, and notable quotes ("I don't know who has the CERT-In portal login"). They note strengths as well as gaps.

**At the hotwash**, immediately afterwards, go round the room asking each participant what went well, what did not, and the single most important change. Keep it to twenty minutes and capture observations rather than designing solutions.

**In the after-action report and improvement plan**, written within a few weeks, rate each objective met, partly met or not met with evidence; list strengths; and for each gap record the root cause (the plan was silent, wrong, or unknown, or a tool or access was missing) and an action with an owner and due date. Track the actions like any corrective action and test them in the next exercise.

Useful AAR measures are simple: the scenario time of declaration, the time to a containment decision, the decisions that needed someone not in the room, and whether the reporting deadline was calculated correctly. Like the SOC metrics of Module 1, they make exercises comparable year to year.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Classify the MSEL, evaluation notes and AAR. They describe exactly where the organisation's response is weak. Share them on a need-to-know basis (TLP:AMBER or the internal equivalent is typical), store them with access control, and never put real personal data or real credentials into exercise materials.

## The Banyan tabletop: a ready-to-run scenario

The scenario below uses the Banyan Microfinance incident from the course dataset. Every technical fact in the injects comes from the dataset or from the kit's saved outputs (`LAB_ASSETS/C08/triage_output.txt`, `hunt_output.txt`, `ioc_match_output.txt`), so the SOC players can verify them in Lesson 8. Injects 5, 6 and 7 add business events that are **not** in the dataset; they are exercise material, invented to test communication and decision-making.

### Exercise overview

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Duration | 100 minutes: 10 briefing, 70 injects, 20 hotwash |
| Format | Discussion only; no system is touched |
| Players | Incident lead, SOC Tier 2 analyst, IT operations lead, legal / DPO, communications, Head of Finance (business owner), service desk lead |
| Exercise staff | Facilitator, two evaluators, one controller (plays CERT-In, the ISAC, customers and the media) |
| Scenario date | 3 November 2026; the scenario clock starts at 08:30 UTC (14:00 IST) |
| Classification | Exercise materials TLP:AMBER; all data synthetic |

### Objectives

1. **O1 Declare.** Agree within 15 minutes of scenario time whether the alerts constitute an incident, its severity under the Lesson 5 matrix, and who is notified.
2. **O2 Contain.** Reach a containment decision for WS-104 and the VPN account, naming the person who authorises it and the business workaround, without destroying evidence.
3. **O3 Report.** Establish the "noticed" time, the CERT-In deadline in UTC and IST, the DPDP questions and who owns each external notification.
4. **O4 Communicate.** Use need-to-know and out-of-band communication correctly, and agree who approves any external statement.

### Starting situation (read aloud)

"It is Tuesday 3 November 2026, 08:30 UTC, 14:00 in Mumbai. The SOC's Tier 1 analyst has just opened the triage queue. Banyan Microfinance is a fictional lender with a finance team that approves loan disbursements daily; the next batch is due for approval by 17:00 IST. The incident response plan, contact list and severity matrix are in front of you, exactly as they exist today."

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure is the players' view of the Banyan MSEL: eight injects in scenario-time order, what each one gives the players and the decision it is designed to force. It lets a facilitator see at a glance that every objective is exercised and that the pressure builds through the session.

**Components.** The table has four columns. **#** numbers the inject; the last row is **C1**, a contingency inject used only if time allows. **Scenario time** gives UTC first and IST second, because the reporting deadline must be stated in both. **Inject** names the content in a few words. **Decision tested** is the question the players must answer. Row colours carry meaning: blue rows (1 and 2) are SOC-originated technical information; orange rows (3 and 4) are the high-pressure decision points on reporting and on the executive's wipe demand; red rows (5 and 6) introduce a second possible compromise and a compromised channel; the violet row (7) is the external communications test; the slate row (C1) is the optional contingency.

**Relationships and flow.** Read the table top to bottom: each inject assumes only the facts of the ones above it, not any particular decision. Inject 1 (the triage queue) and inject 2 (the escalation) supply the evidence for every later discussion. Inject 3 starts the legal discussion from the 08:30 UTC noticed time, so the CERT-In deadline is 14:30 UTC, which is 20:00 IST. Inject 4 then collides that evidence with business pressure. Injects 5 and 6 widen the scope from one host to an account and a communication channel. Inject 7 brings the outside world in.

**Decision logic.** Several rows have no single right answer, and that is intended. At row 4 an acceptable outcome is "isolate now, image, give the user a clean replacement device, rebuild WS-104 later"; an unacceptable one is wiping before evidence is captured. At row 5 the players must decide whether to contain gopal's account together with WS-104 even though the link between the two threads is a hypothesis based on timing. At row C1 the correct instinct is restraint: the matched indicator has expired and has low confidence.

**Technical interpretation.** Rows 1, 2 and C1, and the VPN facts in row 5, come from real kit output: the triage scores 10.8 and 7.2, the 18 beacons at a mean gap of 300 seconds, the spray across all eight users from 203.0.113.66 with one success for gopal at 06:46 UTC, and the expired match on 198.51.100.104. Gopal's statement in row 5, and rows 6 and 7, are invented business events.

**Practical example.** An evaluator assigned to O3 watches rows 3 and 7 and records the scenario time at which someone states "14:30 UTC, 20:00 IST" and names the person who submits the report.

**Security implications.** The inject list reveals which weaknesses the organisation expects; keep it from the players before the session and classify it afterwards.

**Key learning.** A good MSEL is ordered so that evidence comes first, then pressure, then widening scope, and each row names the decision it exists to test.

### Inject 1: 08:30 UTC (14:00 IST), the triage queue

*Given to: SOC Tier 2 analyst, read aloud.* The top of the SOC queue, as produced by the kit's triage helper:

```text
10.8  R5 log_cleared             host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 7.2  R2 vpn_spray_then_success  host=WS-107 (sales laptop) user=gopal (standard) ip=external (documentation range in this lab)
 7.2  R1 office_spawns_script    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
```

*Prompts:* Is this an incident yet? Who is paged, on which channel? What severity now, and what would raise it? *Expected (staff only):* Tier 2 links the two WS-104 alerts, declares a suspected incident (code execution on a business-critical asset), pages the incident lead and opens a case. The R2 host (WS-107) is inferred by the helper from gopal's primary host: VPN events carry no host.

### Inject 2: 08:45 UTC (14:15 IST), the escalation

*Given to: incident lead, as a printed six-part escalation.* WHAT: likely compromise of finance workstation WS-104 (user divya) through a malicious document, with persistence and possible exfiltration. CONFIDENCE: high. NEED: approval to isolate WS-104 and reset credentials within 30 minutes. EVIDENCE (UTC):

| Time | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| 06:12 | `invoice.docm` downloaded from `banyan-payroll-update.example` |
| 06:14 | Word starts hidden, encoded PowerShell |
| 06:17-07:42 | 18 requests of 220 bytes to `cdn-telemetry-sync.example`, mean gap 300 s (ISAC-A, confidence 85, TLP:AMBER) |
| 07:02-07:03 | Local account `svc_update` created and added to Administrators |
| 08:12 | 48.2 MB uploaded to `files-share-drop.example` |
| 08:17 | Security log cleared |

*Prompts:* Who may approve isolating a payments approver's workstation, and what does the business do meanwhile? *Expected:* EDR network isolation (which preserves memory) is approved, a "do not touch, do not reboot" instruction goes to IT operations and the service desk, and finance is asked for a workaround.

### Inject 3: 08:50 UTC (14:20 IST), legal asks

*Given to: legal / DPO, as a message from the incident lead.* "Do we have to report this, to whom, and by when? Did the 48.2 MB contain customer personal data?"

*Prompts:* What is the noticed time, and the CERT-In deadline? What is known and unknown about the upload? Which other obligations may apply (DPDP, the RBI, contracts, insurer)? *Expected:* noticed time 08:30 UTC is recorded; the six-hour deadline is 14:30 UTC, 20:00 IST; the SOC states what is known (size, time, destination, host) and not known (content); legal owns the decision and names who submits.

### Inject 4: 09:05 UTC (14:35 IST), the wipe demand

*Given to: incident lead, as a phone call from the controller playing the Head of Finance.* "Divya must approve today's disbursement batch by 17:00. Wipe and reimage her PC now and get it back to her."

*Prompts:* What is lost by wiping now, and what can you offer instead? *Expected:* wiping destroys the evidence needed for scoping and reporting; keep WS-104 isolated, image it, give divya a clean device with fresh credentials, rebuild later, and record who decided.

### Inject 5: 09:20 UTC (14:50 IST), a second account (exercise-only statement)

*Given to: service desk lead.* Gopal, a sales user, calls about an unrelated printer problem and mentions he was at a client site all morning and has not used the VPN today. *Dataset fact the SOC can add:* a VPN password spray from 203.0.113.66 tried all eight users between 06:42:00 and 06:42:49 UTC, and gopal's account logged in successfully from the same address at 06:46.

*Prompts:* Does the service desk know this matters? Same incident? Contain gopal's account now, and how? *Expected:* the service desk escalates; the account is treated as in scope until shown otherwise, and gopal's password is reset with sessions and MFA tokens revoked at the same moment as the WS-104 containment, so that one action does not warn the attacker before the other.

### Inject 6: 09:35 UTC (15:05 IST), the channel

*Given to: IT operations lead.* "One of my team thinks the attacker may be able to read our IT chat: a message about isolating WS-104 was marked as read by an account nobody recognises." (exercise-only)

*Expected:* move to the pre-arranged out-of-band channel, cut to need-to-know, stop discussing containment on the suspect platform, consider accelerating containment, and log the change.

### Inject 7: 09:50 UTC (15:20 IST), outside pressure (exercise-only)

*Given to: communications, as a customer email forwarded by the support team.* "A forum post says Banyan loan customers' data is for sale. Is my data safe?"

*Expected:* only an approved holding line goes out; communications drafts a statement for legal and the incident lead to approve; the SOC logs the post as a lead to verify, not a fact.

### Contingency C1: 13:40 UTC (19:10 IST), an expired indicator

*Use only if time allows.* The intelligence platform flags gopal's VPN login at 13:33 UTC from 198.51.100.104, which matches an open-feed indicator with confidence 40, TLP:CLEAR, marked as expired and "scanning (old, shared hosting)". *Expected:* a weak lead to check, not confirmation: the VPN log records country IN for this login (unlike "??" at 06:46), and an expired shared-hosting indicator means little alone.

> [!NOTE]
> Build one habit in players throughout: state UTC and IST together and check the arithmetic aloud. IST is UTC+05:30 with no daylight saving time, so 09:35 UTC is 15:05 IST and 13:40 UTC is 19:10 IST. A wrong conversion in a regulatory report is an avoidable error.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

- **"A tabletop is a test the players pass or fail."** It tests the plan; treating it as an assessment of individuals hides the gaps.
- **Unevaluable objectives** produce no actions.
- **Over-clever scenarios** invite players to fight the scenario; plausible, local ones find more gaps.
- **Injects that depend on one decision** force the facilitator to steer.
- **The senior person answers everything.** Ask by role.
- **Stopping at the hotwash.** Without an AAR and owned actions the same gaps recur.
- **Using real data** in materials is a confidentiality risk for no benefit.

## Security implications, detection and defence

Exercise materials document weaknesses in detection, authority and communication, so protect them like any sensitive security document. Mark exercise messages clearly (many organisations prefix them "EXERCISE EXERCISE EXERCISE") so that nobody mistakes a scenario for a real incident, and never contact real external parties such as CERT-In, the RBI or the media during a discussion exercise; the controller plays them.

Exercises are themselves a control: they validate the plan, the contact list and decision authority, and they train people safely. Their actions often land in the SOC: a missing detection, a telemetry gap (Banyan shows that VPN events have no device field and that WS-104 process telemetry ends at 06:14 UTC), a playbook step or a faster evidence export. Track them to closure and re-test them.

## Best practices

- Derive objectives from real concerns; build local scenarios from your own incidents.
- Separate facilitator, evaluators and players, include deputies, and give every role an inject.
- Show scenario time in UTC and local time, and log decisions against it.
- Hold the hotwash immediately and the AAR within weeks; every gap gets an owner and a date.

## Practical application (discussion exercise)

Run the Banyan tabletop with your study group or colleagues. It needs no lab and touches no system.

1. Choose a facilitator and an evaluator; assign the player roles (one person may hold two).
2. Hand out the starting situation, objectives and evaluation sheet; keep the expected actions with the facilitator.
3. Brief for 10 minutes, then release injects 1 to 7 about every 10 minutes; hold C1 in reserve.
4. Keep a visible decision log, then hold a 20-minute hotwash.
5. Within a week, write a one-page AAR with each objective rated and at least five owned actions.
6. Keep the AAR: in Lesson 8 you can check whether the SOC could really produce, in time, what the players assumed.

## Key takeaways

- A tabletop tests decisions, roles, authority and communication by discussion; it touches no system.
- Design backwards from three to five evaluable objectives; the MSEL orders injects by scenario time with expected actions.
- Facilitators ask by role, manage time and let gaps stand rather than rescuing the group.
- Evaluation runs from notes, to the hotwash, to an AAR and improvement plan with owners and dates.
- Classify exercise materials and use synthetic data.
- The Banyan scenario tests declaration, containment authority, the CERT-In deadline (14:30 UTC, 20:00 IST from an 08:30 UTC noticed time) and out-of-band communication.
