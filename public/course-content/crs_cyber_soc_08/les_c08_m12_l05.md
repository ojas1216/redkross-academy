## Case 3: a log-clear signal during maintenance

The third Harbourline Finance case is a test of evidence-based closure. A Windows audit event records a Security log clear on OPS-03. Such an observation deserves review because it can affect visibility. In the exercise it occurred during approved maintenance, and additional records support that explanation. Your assignment is to document why the specific alert can be closed, keep its evidence, and define conditions that would require renewed investigation. This is an analysis of supplied synthetic records. The lesson does not ask you to clear a log or operate any system.

A SOC often investigates activities that can be legitimate or harmful. A service restart, a change to logging and an unusual administrative login may occur for approved work and in incidents. The signal's value depends on context. A mature response verifies the matching conditions rather than treating an entire class of behaviour as safe or treating every alert as a confirmed compromise. The goal is a decision someone else can reproduce.

## Start with the alert, not the explanation

B16 gives the actual observation. OPS-03 recorded the Security log clear at 08:23 UTC, event O-401. B28 repeats the event with the same source identity. Account for both receipts and retain one underlying event in the chronology. A13 is an earlier operations notice that says maintenance is planned for OPS-03 between 08:20 and 08:30. On its own it does not prove authority or demonstrate that this specific operation belongs to the change. Use it as a lead to locate the authoritative record.

B17 is the change record. It covers OPS-03, the relevant time window and the specified log-clear step. B19 is the owning team's confirmation that the authorised team executed that approved work on the named asset. B18 independently verifies that a forwarded log copy for the approved window is available. The evidence is complementary. The change proves planned authority, the owner verifies what occurred, and the remote copy addresses historical visibility for the checked window. Keep these separate rather than citing a maintenance ticket as proof of every part of the case.

Compare exact fields. The asset in the event must match the approved asset or a scope that explicitly includes it. The occurrence time should fall in the approved time window, adjusted only where the underlying timestamps and time base support that. The actual operation should match the authorised one, with any deviations noted. Verify credible execution context, rather than just whether an entry exists in the change system. Check availability of expected evidence. A maintainer's statement can be valuable, but should not silently expand authority beyond the approved record.

A similar hostname elsewhere would not match without an explicit link. The second compares the event at 08:23 with the approved 08:20?08:30 window. It is inside the interval. A late receipt does not change the recorded occurrence time. If one system uses a different time zone or an uncertain clock, the analyst should resolve that before relying on fine timing boundaries.

The third row verifies the operation itself and the owning team's confirmation. An approved general maintenance window is too broad to explain every state change in that period. The record in this exercise explicitly includes the activity, and the team responsible identifies it as authorised work. Compare that with a scenario where the owner confirms attending the device but does not know when or why local logging changed. Such a case would remain uncertain. The fourth row checks that forwarded evidence can be examined. Archived visibility helps preserve an independent record even though the local observation includes a log clear.

The final row is the disposition. It applies to O-401 on OPS-03 under CR-82 and the verified evidence available for this review. It does not grant permission for a future event, approve any activity outside this scope or justify disabling alerting globally. A useful closure entry includes what would reopen the case. Another clear outside the approved window, an unexpected asset, a mismatch in the approved action or loss of required visibility can all change the decision. By reading the rows from event to authorisation and then to evidence of actual execution, an analyst can explain why the benign classification is grounded and which conditions must be preserved for similar reasoning to hold.

## Write a defensible closure

Lead with the case disposition and the source references. A good report might state that event O-401 at 08:23 UTC on OPS-03 is consistent with approved change CR-82 and confirmed by the authorised team. Cite the relevant approval window, included operation and archived log evidence. Record B28 as a duplicate receipt. If required by the case process, give the reviewer role and time. Explain that the case is closed for the checked event unless later evidence identifies a discrepancy.

The closed status should remain linkable to the alert and records. Avoid enough detail to expose sensitive infrastructure beyond the case's authorised audience. An executive summary can say planned maintenance explained a reviewed operations alert and no response change was required. The technical note should include the event and change identifiers, time, matching conditions and archive check. This lets the next shift recognise a duplicate of this event while retaining the ability to investigate a different one.

Keep an explicit confidence statement. The supplied scenario combines an observed event, properly scoped approved change, owner corroboration and a verified archive copy. That supports a high-confidence administrative explanation for the specific event. The evidence does not establish the full condition of every endpoint or the adequacy of the organisation's logging process outside that interval. An owner may still review whether future maintenance notifications and archives give the SOC enough context. That process improvement is separate from leaving the incident open indefinitely.

If you find that a severe event was labelled low priority solely because maintenance was planned, adjust the workflow. A planned window can enrich a case but should not convert all alerts to benign without supporting context. The difference is important for a controlled detection policy. For example, you might ask for a tag when the verified change record overlaps, while still preserving the alert and the duplicate trace. The operating team can later assess how frequently that tag is accurate and whether analysts need a quicker way to inspect the matching conditions.

## Compare variations before deciding

**Wrong asset.** Suppose CR-82 covers OPS-04. The event still says OPS-03. Ask the owner to locate the correct approved change or investigate unexpected activity. The similar label does not establish asset match. Keep the operations notice and reported event as separate facts. If the source's identity was mapped incorrectly, verify the original sensor record and inventory key before changing the case. A mapping correction with provenance is stronger than editing the worksheet until it appears correct.

**Outside the window.** Suppose the event occurred at 08:37. The record's approved window ended at 08:30. A later review needs to know whether an authorised extension existed, whether the timestamp is reliable and whether the operation was the one intended for maintenance. An operator's recollection that the work ran late may explain the timing but does not on its own establish a valid approved extension. Treat the record as unresolved and give the right owner a verification question. Review time-source integrity before deciding that a seven-minute difference necessarily indicates a second event.

**Missing archive.** Suppose the approved change and owner confirmation remain, while the forwarded copy is absent. The action may still be legitimate under the documented scope, but the evidence visibility problem remains and needs a collection owner. The case note can distinguish the explanation of the action from the unresolved monitoring effect. Depending on the organisation's procedures, the latter may continue as a separate follow-up. Be precise that missing records are not a completed negative finding.

**Conflicting actual action.** Suppose the administrator confirms a different operation was performed on the same device. Ask the operations owner and case lead to reconcile the observed signal and approved task. Keep both records and verify evidence before closing. A conflict about what happened is stronger reason to review than a generic maintenance note is reason to dismiss. Do not overwrite one account with the other or change scope based on an assumption. The reviewer needs to see what remains contested and who can resolve it.

## Separate reopening from suppression

A reopen condition identifies new evidence that would invalidate the current case's resolution. A suppression policy changes how future events reach analysts. They have different review requirements. A weak maintenance note could lead to a dangerously broad rule if its author assumes every event with the same product label is harmless. Under the exercise, preserve the original alerting and document a scoped operator disposition. If staff later propose an enrichment or suppression change, test it against benign and unexpected cases under authorised change review.

Specify exceptions concretely. Events outside a verified window, events on unlisted devices, missing authorisation or follow-on activity inconsistent with the expected operation should remain reviewable. A narrowly scoped change association can reduce investigation time by showing analysts a relevant record; it should still retain the observed event. Document how the association expires when the maintenance window ends, who owns the setting, and how it is audited. Reassess when collection or host identity changes so the procedure does not rely on stale assumptions.

If an approval association fails, decide whether the alert should preserve an unverified context tag or route for manual review. A lookup outage is different from evidence that there was no applicable change. Giving these states distinct reasons can protect operator attention and improve later diagnosis. For example, a reviewer can inspect repeated connection failures without dismissing the alerts that arrived while the service was down. A context feature should not become the single point that hides significant evidence.

## Perform a closure challenge

Review the case as if you were the person on the next shift receiving another alert. Read the original event identity and compare it with the new one before deciding whether it is a repeat. A duplicate during maintenance can link to the same disposition. A uniquely identified event with the same general category should still be checked against the correct scope. If the next alert concerns another approved change, preserve both records independently. The closure note should help the analyst compare them without encouraging a broad presumption that similar events are legitimate.

Create a timeline of when each conclusion became available. At the start of the shift, the SOC knew a maintenance window was planned. At 08:23 it had the observed event, and it later received archival and owner evidence. The recorded change time at 08:20 is not necessarily the time the SOC had its evidence in hand. Preserve the difference between occurrence and receipt where the record states it. If the incoming analyst asks why the alert was not closed earlier, identify the evidence needed to establish authorisation and preservation. A defensible explanation protects both the response and the integrity of the review.

Write a test for a misleading match by changing one field at a time. The case can retain the approved interval but change the host, keep the host but change the operation, or keep both while moving the event outside the interval. In each version the detective signal still has value, and its administrative explanation is incomplete until someone produces independent evidence. This tests whether a reviewer actually checks the match dimensions in the record. A passive assignment that accepts any matching maintenance title would fail these tests, even if it works on the single expected example.

Then compare the closure note with the organisation's logging objectives. The archive validates the availability of a copy for the defined interval. It does not guarantee that future events are delivered or that all sensors are healthy. Introduce a separate monitoring request if the verified closure exposes a missing source elsewhere. Operators can close a scoped alert while documenting a systemic improvement. The action register should record each work item with its own owner, evidence and review goal so the lead can see what remains without reopening a case that was correctly dispositioned.

## Key takeaways

- Verify the actual asset, time, operation, authority and preserved evidence before closure.
- Preserve duplicate receipts without inventing additional events.
- Close the specific alert and retain clear conditions for reopening.
- Treat any monitoring or suppression redesign as a separate reviewed decision.
