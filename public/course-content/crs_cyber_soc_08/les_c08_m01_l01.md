## Introduction and conceptual foundation

Every organisation of any size produces a continuous stream of security-relevant events: a user signs in over the VPN, a laptop starts a process, a proxy forwards a request, a domain controller adds someone to a group. Almost all of these events are harmless. A very small number are the first visible traces of an intrusion. A **security operations centre (SOC)** is the team, process and technology that watches this stream, decides which traces matter, and acts on them quickly enough to limit the damage.

MITRE's guide *11 Strategies of a World-Class Cybersecurity Operations Center* (Knerler, Parker and Zimmerman, 2022) describes a SOC as a team primarily composed of security analysts, organised to detect, analyse, respond to, report on and prevent cybersecurity incidents. Each verb in that definition is a distinct activity. **Detect** means noticing that something may be wrong, usually because a rule or model raised an alert. **Analyse** means establishing whether the alert is real, what happened and how far it reaches. **Respond** means containing and recovering, usually in partnership with IT teams who own the systems. **Report** means telling management, regulators and sometimes customers what happened. **Prevent** means feeding what was learned back into controls and detections so that the next attempt is caught earlier or fails altogether.

In the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, the SOC lives mainly in the **Detect** and **Respond** Functions, but it cannot work without **Identify** (an asset inventory that tells the analyst whether a host is a test laptop or the payments server) and **Protect** (the controls whose logs the SOC reads). A SOC that does not know what the organisation owns, or which systems matter most, will treat every alert as equally important and will drown.

That last word is the central problem of security operations, and it is the reason this lesson exists. The events that reach a SOC as alerts are still far too many for every one to receive expert attention. Experienced investigators are scarce and expensive; alerts are plentiful and cheap. The **tier model** is the organisational answer: it arranges the team so that a large number of alerts can be handled consistently at the first level, and only those that need deeper skill move upward to people who can provide it. The alert lifecycle, shift handover, service levels and metrics in the rest of this module are all defined in terms of tiers.

> [!NOTE]
> The tier model is a way of organising work, not a ranking of people's worth. Tier 1 analysts make the highest number of decisions in the SOC, and a wrong Tier 1 closure can hide an entire intrusion. The tiers differ in the depth and duration of the work, not in its importance.

## Key terminology

| Term | Precise meaning |
| --- | --- |
| SOC | Security operations centre: the team, processes and tools that detect, analyse, respond to, report on and help prevent incidents. |
| Event | A single recorded occurrence, such as a log-on, a process start or a proxy request. |
| Alert | An event or combination of events that a rule, model or analyst has flagged for human review. |
| Case | The record in the case-management system that holds an alert (or several related alerts), the analyst's notes, evidence and decisions. |
| Incident | A confirmed or strongly suspected security breach or attempt that needs a coordinated response. |
| Triage | Rapid assessment of an alert to decide whether it is real, how urgent it is and who should handle it. |
| Escalation | The formal transfer of a case to a higher tier or another team, with evidence and a reason. |
| Disposition | The conclusion recorded when an alert is closed: for example true positive, benign true positive, false positive or inconclusive. |
| Playbook | A documented set of steps for handling a particular type of alert or incident. |
| Tier 1, 2, 3 | Levels of the classic SOC model: triage, investigation and response, and specialist work respectively. |

## Why a SOC is organised in tiers

Imagine a SOC with no structure at all: every analyst picks alerts from one shared queue and takes each one as far as it needs to go. On a quiet day this works. On a normal day it fails in three predictable ways.

First, **the queue stops moving**. An analyst who picks up an alert that turns out to be the start of a real intrusion may spend the rest of the shift on it, quite rightly. While they do, the dozens of other alerts they would have looked at wait. One of them may be the more urgent case.

Second, **the work is inconsistent**. Without agreed boundaries, one analyst closes a certain alert type after a thirty-second check and another spends an hour on the same pattern.

Third, **expertise is wasted**. The colleague who can reverse-engineer a malicious document spends most of the day closing alerts caused by a misconfigured backup job, because that is what the queue contains.

Tiers solve these problems by **separating breadth from depth**. The first tier works the whole queue quickly and consistently, using playbooks, and has a clear duty to pass on anything that needs more time or skill. The second tier takes the smaller number of cases that are probably real and investigates them fully. The third tier provides specialist skills that are needed occasionally but intensely, and also works proactively on problems the queue never shows. Because each tier receives fewer, harder cases than the one below it, the team as a whole behaves like a funnel: wide at the top where alerts arrive, narrow at the point where specialists apply scarce expertise.

## The tiers in depth

### Tier 1: triage and first response

Tier 1 analysts watch the alert queues that the SIEM (security information and event management system), EDR (endpoint detection and response) and other tools populate. For each alert they answer a small set of questions as quickly as accuracy allows:

1. **Is it real?** Does the evidence in the alert actually show what the rule claims, or did the rule fire on something it was not designed for?
2. **What does it touch?** Which host, which user, and how important is each? A finance workstation used by a payments approver is not the same as a kiosk in the reception area.
3. **Is there an obvious benign explanation?** A scheduled change, a known administrative tool, an approved test.
4. **What happens next?** Close with a documented disposition, or escalate with evidence.

To answer these questions Tier 1 **enriches** each alert: it pulls asset context from the configuration management database (CMDB), identity context from the directory, reputation from threat intelligence, and the surrounding events from the SIEM. It follows playbooks, which tell it what to check for a given alert type and what conditions require escalation. It closes false positives and documented benign activity, and it escalates true or probable positives.

Tier 1 typically has a **time budget** per alert, and that budget is the reason the tier exists. If a case cannot be resolved within it, or if the playbook's escalation criteria are met, the correct action is to escalate rather than to keep digging.

Tier 1 also often handles **first-response actions** that the playbook pre-authorises: for example, isolating an endpoint through the EDR when a high-confidence ransomware detection fires out of hours, or disabling an account that is clearly compromised. These are deliberately limited, reversible actions, agreed in advance with the system owners, so that the SOC does not have to wait for a meeting while an attack spreads.

### Tier 2: investigation and incident response

Tier 2 receives escalated cases and investigates them in depth. Where Tier 1 asked "is this real?", Tier 2 asks "what exactly happened, how far does it go, and what must we do about it?" Typical Tier 2 work includes:

- **Scoping**: building a timeline, finding related alerts and events, identifying every affected host and account.
- **Root-cause analysis**: working out how the activity started, for example a phishing email or a reused password on an external service.
- **Containment recommendations**: deciding which accounts to disable, which hosts to isolate, which indicators to block, and agreeing those actions with IT operations and the identity team.
- **Coordination**: keeping the case record current, informing the SOC lead, and providing facts for any notification decisions.

Tier 2 analysts need a broader and deeper knowledge of operating systems, networks, identity systems and attacker techniques than Tier 1. They are also the people who decide, together with the SOC lead, whether a case is declared an **incident**, at which point the organisation's incident response plan takes over (Module 7 of this course).

### Tier 3: specialists and proactive work

Tier 3 is less a queue than a pool of specialist capability. Its members are called in by Tier 2 for problems that need particular skills, and in between they work proactively. Typical Tier 3 functions are:

- **Threat hunting**: searching for attacker activity that no rule has alerted on, starting from a hypothesis (Module 8).
- **Malware analysis**: examining a suspicious file to learn what it does and to extract indicators.
- **Digital forensics**: acquiring and analysing disk, memory and log evidence in a way that preserves its integrity.
- **Detection engineering**: designing, testing and tuning the rules that generate alerts in the first place (Module 6).

The last item is important for understanding the whole structure. Tier 3 does not only sit at the top of the funnel; it also reaches back to the bottom of it. When Tier 2 or Tier 3 learns something from an investigation, for example that a rule fires on a legitimate backup tool or that an attacker technique went undetected, that lesson becomes a change to a rule or a playbook, and Tier 1 receives a better queue tomorrow.

### The SOC lead

Above or alongside the tiers sits the **SOC manager or SOC lead**, who owns staffing and shift rotas, the quality of analysis, metrics, and communication with stakeholders: the CISO, IT leadership, legal and compliance, and external bodies. In India, the lead must make sure the SOC can support the CERT-In Directions of 28 April 2022, which require covered entities to report specified cyber incidents to CERT-In within six hours of noticing them and to retain logs for 180 days. A six-hour clock is a strong argument for clear tier boundaries and an out-of-hours escalation path: an alert that waits for the morning shift may already have used up the reporting window. Lesson 2 examines the lead and the other supporting roles in detail.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Escalation is a duty, not a failure. A Tier 1 analyst who escalates a case that Tier 2 later closes as benign has done the job correctly if the evidence at the time met the escalation criteria. A Tier 1 analyst who closes a real intrusion because escalating "looked weak" has not.

## How work flows through the tiers

The tiers are connected by two kinds of flow. **Escalation** moves cases upward: from the alert queue to Tier 1, from Tier 1 to Tier 2 when a case is probably real or exceeds the Tier 1 time budget, and from Tier 2 to Tier 3 when it needs specialist skills. **Feedback** moves knowledge downward: from investigations back to the rules and playbooks that Tier 1 uses. Both flows pass through the **case-management system**, which is the single place where an alert becomes a case, where notes and evidence accumulate and where ownership is recorded. A SOC that escalates by chat message or by tapping a colleague on the shoulder has no record of who owns a case, and cases fall through the gaps, especially at shift change.

### Visual Walkthrough & Architectural Breakdown

**Purpose.** The figure shows the classic three-tier SOC as a pipeline from raw telemetry to specialist analysis, with a feedback path that improves the pipeline over time. It matters because almost every SOC process you will meet, from service levels to metrics, is defined in terms of these boxes and the arrows between them.

**Components.** At the top, **Telemetry** (sub-labelled "endpoint, network, identity, cloud") is the raw material: logs and sensor data from every part of the estate. **SIEM / EDR alerts** ("rules and models fire") is where detection logic turns a small fraction of that telemetry into alerts. **Case management** ("one record per case, owner, evidence") is the system of record. **Tier 1: triage** ("validate, enrich, close or escalate") works the queue. **Closed with disposition** ("FP or benign true positive, documented") is the exit where most alerts leave. **Tier 2: investigation** ("scope, contain, coordinate IT") takes probable true positives. **Tier 3: specialists** ("hunt, malware, forensics, detections") provides deep expertise. Beside the pipeline, **SOC lead** ("metrics, quality, stakeholders, CERT-In") oversees the whole flow.

**Relationships and arrows.** Solid arrows show the movement of work. Telemetry feeds alerts; alerts become cases; cases go to Tier 1. From Tier 1, one arrow leads to the closed exit and another, labelled "escalate", leads to Tier 2; a second "escalate" arrow leads from Tier 2 to Tier 3. The dashed arrow from Tier 3 back to SIEM / EDR alerts, labelled "tuning and playbook feedback", is the flow of knowledge rather than of cases. The dashed arrow from the SOC lead to case management, labelled "oversees", shows that the lead governs the process through the case record rather than by working individual alerts.

**Execution flow.** An event is recorded, a rule fires, a case opens, Tier 1 triages it. If it is benign, it is closed with a written disposition. If not, it is escalated, and Tier 2 scopes and contains; if Tier 2 needs malware analysis or forensics, Tier 3 joins. When the case closes, any lesson learned returns to the detection layer.

**Decision logic.** The key branch sits at Tier 1: close or escalate. The criteria for that branch are written in playbooks and include the strength of evidence, the criticality of the asset and user, and whether the Tier 1 time budget has been used. A smaller branch sits at Tier 2: handle directly, or call in specialists.

**Technical interpretation.** Each tier receives fewer cases than the one before it, and each case it receives takes longer. That is the funnel. The feedback arrow is what keeps the funnel efficient: without it, the same false positives reach Tier 1 every day.

**Practical example.** In the course lab, `minisiem.py` plays the SIEM, and `triage.py` produces a prioritised Tier 1 queue from the Banyan Microfinance dataset. A cleared Security log on the finance workstation WS-104 would be validated by Tier 1, then escalated to Tier 2, which would scope the other alerts on the same host.

**Security implications.** The two arrows labelled "escalate" are the most fragile points: a missing or slow escalation is how real alerts go unanswered. Alerts on unacknowledged high-severity cases, and an escalation path that works at 03:00, are the controls.

**Key learning.** Work flows up through case management; knowledge flows back down to the detections. A SOC needs both directions.

## Escalation criteria: where the tier boundaries sit

A tier model is only as good as the rules that decide when a case crosses a boundary. Vague criteria such as "escalate anything suspicious" either send everything to Tier 2, which recreates the original overload one level up, or leave Tier 1 unsure and reluctant to escalate. Good SOCs write their criteria down, per alert type, in the playbooks. The criteria usually combine four factors.

**Evidence strength.** How confident is the detection, and does the surrounding data support it? An alert for an Office application spawning a command shell, where the same host then shows a new local administrator account, is stronger than either event alone.

**Asset and identity criticality.** The same alert on a domain controller, a payments approver's workstation or a production database deserves faster escalation than on a test virtual machine. This is why enrichment from the CMDB and the identity provider is a Tier 1 task rather than a luxury.

**Potential impact and time sensitivity.** Some alert types are time-critical by nature: signs of ransomware staging, a cleared security log, or mass account lock-outs. For these, the playbook should require immediate escalation regardless of how far triage has got, through an out-of-hours on-call path if necessary.

**Effort.** If a case cannot be resolved within the Tier 1 time budget, it is escalated or reassigned, not left open indefinitely on one person's screen.

| Situation | Typical Tier 1 action | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rule fired on a documented, approved activity (change ticket exists) | Close as benign true positive, cite the ticket | Detection worked; activity was authorised |
| Rule logic matched something it was not meant to | Close as false positive, flag the rule for tuning | Feeds detection engineering |
| Evidence supports malicious activity on a low-criticality asset | Escalate to Tier 2 within the service level | Real activity needs scoping |
| Evidence supports malicious activity on a critical asset or privileged user | Escalate immediately, pre-authorised containment if the playbook allows | Time and impact |
| Cannot decide within the time budget | Escalate as inconclusive with notes | Protects the queue and the case |

Notice that both closing rows produce an output beyond the closure: a ticket reference or a tuning request.

> [!WARNING]
> Playbooks support judgement; they must not block urgent action. A playbook that says "escalate during business hours" must never stop an analyst from using the emergency on-call path when evidence suggests ransomware at 03:00. If a playbook and the evidence disagree, escalate, document why, and request a playbook change afterwards.

## Beyond the classic model: functional teams and automation

The three-tier model is the one most job descriptions and most textbooks use, and it is the right starting point. Many modern SOCs, however, have reshaped it, for reasons that follow directly from its weaknesses.

The classic model has three well-known problems. **Context is lost at every hand-off**: the Tier 1 analyst who noticed something odd about an alert writes a note, and some of what they knew does not survive the transfer. **Tier 1 work is repetitive**, which drives burnout and staff turnover; analysts who spend months closing the same false positives leave, taking their knowledge of the environment with them. And **the career path is a ladder with crowded lower rungs**, so analysts see Tier 1 as something to escape rather than a craft.

In response, organisations have moved in three directions, often at once:

- **Functional teams**. Instead of levels, the SOC is organised by function: a detection team that writes and tunes rules, a response team that owns cases from triage to closure, and a hunting and intelligence team that works proactively. A case stays with one response analyst for longer, so context is kept, and senior analysts mentor junior ones within the same team.
- **Automation of Tier 1 tasks**. Enrichment, de-duplication, the closure of well-understood benign patterns (with sampling review) and the gathering of standard evidence are scripted, often in a SOAR (security orchestration, automation and response) platform. The analyst starts each case with the context already attached.
- **Rotation**. Analysts rotate between queue work, detection engineering and hunting, which spreads knowledge and reduces fatigue.

None of these removes the underlying need for triage, investigation and specialist work; they redistribute it. Even a SOC with no "Tier 1" job title still has someone making the first close-or-escalate decision on each alert, and still needs written criteria for it. The tier vocabulary describes the work, whatever the organisation chart says.

**Components.** The left column, **Classic tiered SOC**, lists five properties: "Work organised by depth: T1, T2, T3"; "Case changes hands at each escalation"; "Clear entry role and career ladder"; "Tier 1 queue is repetitive, burnout risk"; and "Easy to staff and measure per tier". The right column, **Functional teams**, lists the matching five: "Work organised by function: detect, respond, hunt"; "One analyst owns a case end to end"; "Mixed seniority within each team"; "Repetitive triage automated (SOAR)"; and "Needs strong automation and mentoring". The "vs" between the columns marks them as alternatives, though most organisations blend them.

**Relationships.** Read the columns row by row; each row is one design concern. Row one is the organising principle. Row two is case ownership, which determines how much context survives. Row three is how people grow. Row four is how repetitive work is handled. Row five is the price of each model: tiers are simple to staff and measure, while functional teams depend on investment in automation and senior time.

**Execution flow.** In the tiered column, a case moves between people as it deepens. In the functional column, the case stays with its owner and specialists are pulled in, while the detection and hunting functions work in parallel rather than above.

**Decision logic.** The choice depends on scale and maturity. A young SOC with many new analysts and little automation benefits from the structure of tiers. A mature SOC with good automation and experienced staff can move towards functional teams. A managed provider serving many clients often keeps tiers at the front for consistency across customers.

**Technical interpretation.** Both models implement the same funnel. The tiered model implements it with people at fixed levels; the functional model implements part of it with automation and keeps human ownership continuous.

**Practical example.** At the fictional Banyan Microfinance, a two-person night shift cannot run three tiers. It runs a functional model: both analysts triage, either can take a case to closure, and the on-call Tier 3 specialist is paged for forensics when needed.

**Security implications.** Hand-offs in the tiered model are where context is lost; ownership in the functional model is where a single overloaded analyst can become a bottleneck. Either way, the case record must hold the evidence so that the work survives a change of owner.

**Key learning.** Tiers and functional teams are two ways to build the same funnel; what matters is clear ownership, written criteria and a working feedback loop.

## Worked example: a Tier 1 queue from the course lab

The course lab kit contains a small teaching SIEM and a triage helper. `LAB_ASSETS/C08/minisiem.py` loads the synthetic dataset `banyan_events.jsonl` (one day at the fictional Banyan Microfinance; 340 events from proxy, Sysmon, Windows and VPN sources) into SQLite and defines six correlation rules, R1 to R6. `LAB_ASSETS/C08/triage.py` then does the enrichment a Tier 1 analyst would otherwise do by hand: it runs the rules, attaches asset context (for example that WS-104 is a finance workstation) and identity context (that the user divya is a payments approver), applies documented suppressions, and computes a priority score from the rule's base severity, its confidence and the asset's criticality. You run `python minisiem.py load` once to build the database, then the triage helper. The saved output (`triage_output.txt`) is:

```terminal
$ python triage.py
10.8  R5 log_cleared             host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 7.2  R2 vpn_spray_then_success  host=WS-107 (sales laptop) user=gopal (standard) ip=external (documentation range in this lab)
 7.2  R1 office_spawns_script    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 5.4  R3 new_local_admin         host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 4.5  R4 large_upload            host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=divya (payments approver) ip=n/a
 3.6  R6 beacon_regular_small    host=WS-104 (finance workstation) user=None (?) ip=n/a
```

Each line is one alert in the queue. The first column is the priority score (severity multiplied by confidence multiplied by asset criticality, as defined in `triage.py`), then the rule identifier and name, then the enriched host, user and source-address context. The queue is sorted with the highest priority first, which is the order a Tier 1 analyst would work it.

The top item, a cleared Security log on a finance workstation used by a payments approver, is exactly the kind of time-sensitive, high-criticality alert that the escalation criteria above say should go to Tier 2 promptly; clearing the log is something attackers do to remove evidence. The next two items are different in kind. The VPN alert concerns a different host and user, WS-107 and gopal, and the pattern "many users failed from one address, then one succeeded" deserves its own case. The Office-spawns-script alert is on WS-104 again.

The most important Tier 1 observation is the one that no single line states: **six of the seven alerts concern the same host, WS-104**. A disciplined Tier 1 analyst does not treat them as six unrelated tickets; they link them into one case and escalate it as a probable incident, because together they describe a sequence (a document launches a script, local administrator changes appear, a large upload leaves, the log is cleared, and something checks in regularly) that none of them proves alone. That act of linking is the bridge between Tier 1 and Tier 2.

## Common mistakes and misconceptions

**"Tier 1 is just ticket clearing."** Tier 1 makes the first and most frequent judgement on every alert. If it closes the cleared-log alert above as "admin housekeeping" without checking, nothing above it in the funnel ever sees the case.

**Escalating by chat or verbally.** Escalation must happen in the case-management system with an acknowledgement. A message to a colleague who has already left the building is not an escalation; the case has no owner.

**Escalating without evidence.** "Looks dodgy, please check" forces Tier 2 to redo Tier 1's work. An escalation states what was observed, what was checked, what was ruled out and why the criteria were met.

**Treating the time budget as a target.** The budget is a ceiling that triggers escalation, not a quota. Rushing to close within it, especially when speed is rewarded, leads to real alerts being closed as false positives (Lesson 7 returns to this).

## Security implications, detection and defence

The tier structure is itself a security control, and it can fail like one. The best-known example is the 2013 Target breach: the US Senate Commerce Committee majority staff analysis reported that Target appeared to have failed to respond to multiple automated warnings from its intrusion detection software. The technology detected; the organisation did not act effectively.

The controls that protect the tier structure are mostly procedural:

- **Alerts on unacknowledged high-severity cases.** If a critical case has not been picked up within its service level, the SOC lead and on-call staff are notified automatically.
- **A tested out-of-hours escalation path.** On-call rotas with named people and a way to reach them that does not depend on someone noticing a chat message.
- **Pre-authorised first-response actions.** Agreed in advance with system owners, so that containment does not wait for business hours.
- **Quality review of closures.** A sample of Tier 1 closures is reviewed by a senior analyst every week; mis-closures become training and tuning items.
- **Access control on the case system.** Case records contain sensitive data about users and incidents; access is restricted and logged, and evidence handling follows the organisation's retention rules, including the 180-day log retention required for CERT-In covered entities.

## Best practices

- Write escalation criteria per alert type, combining evidence strength, asset and identity criticality, time sensitivity and effort.
- Use one case-management system for every escalation and require acknowledgement.
- Close every alert with a disposition and a reason; route false positives to detection engineering.
- Keep a feedback loop from Tier 2 and Tier 3 back to rules and playbooks, and measure that it is used.
- Match the structure to the team size: formal tiers for large teams, functional teams with on-call specialists for small ones.

## Key takeaways

- A SOC detects, analyses, responds to, reports on and helps prevent incidents; the tier model organises that work so a large alert volume can be handled consistently.
- Tier 1 triages and enriches, closes with documented dispositions and escalates; Tier 2 investigates, scopes and coordinates containment; Tier 3 provides hunting, malware analysis, forensics and detection engineering.
- Work flows upward through case management; knowledge flows back down to rules and playbooks.
- Escalation criteria must be written down and must allow urgent out-of-hours escalation, not least because of CERT-In's six-hour reporting window.
- Functional teams and automation reshape the tiers but do not remove the need for triage, investigation and specialist work.
